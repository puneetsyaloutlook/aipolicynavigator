AI policy in sixteen jurisdictions, as at 3 October 2026

How hard governments are pushed on AI, and how much their policy bites

Push is the pressure on a government to act. Bite is what its policy then does. The navigator scores sixteen jurisdictions on both, compares how each government's bite follows its push, and shows what the gap means for an organisation planning its own AI governance.

Push

The pressure on a government to act on AI. It comes from rival states, trading partners, industry, voters, incidents and the government's own economic and security aims. Push explains why a policy exists.

Chatbot harms to minors pushed United States states to legislate. Competitiveness concerns and pressure from Washington pushed the EU to delay its high-risk rules.

Bite

What the policy does to the firms and people it applies to. It is judged by effect on the reference date and not by legal text: a rule bites when it is in force, specific, monitored and enforced, and a program bites when money or capacity is committed.

A United States export control letter took two frontier models offline within days. The EU's high-risk rules were law and constrained no one.

Where to go next

All push and bite scores are judgements made from the research report, for sixteen jurisdictions as at 3 October 2026. They are not measurements. Government intent is scored. Self-regulation by firms is not.

Insights

  • Pressure to protect almost always leads to constraining bite. Pressure to promote does not always lead to enabling bite. Where the push is rivalry or security, as with chip export controls, governments promote AI and also restrict it.
  • Legal form is a poor guide to how hard policy bites. The strongest constraining bite comes from executive or administrative action in China and the United States, not from legislatures. Singapore's voluntary policy bites harder than the statutes in Brazil, South Korea, Japan and Taiwan.
  • Policy in China and the United States bites hardest, but it does not look that way on this chart. Both governments also promote AI heavily, so the enabling bite of their policy cancels out its constraining bite and they land near the middle. Strategic constraint, the box for chip export controls, is empty for the same reason. The strength chart below offers a better view of the magnitude of that bite.
  • Australian policy bites as hard as EU policy today. The EU's law is more ambitious, but its high-risk rules do not apply until December 2027 and August 2028. Australia's online safety and privacy rules are already in force, and both score 44 on constraining bite. California's policy scores 44 as well.

These patterns come from sixteen scores made by judgement, so treat them as indications and not proof.

How strongly does policy bite, given how hard the government is pushed?

Each box combines how strong the push on the government is with how strong its bite is.

  • Push, across: the eight push factors added together, each rated 0 to 3, then scaled so that 100 is the maximum possible push.
  • Bite, up: the average of enabling bite and constraining bite, each scored 0 to 100.
  • Dotted line: boxes along it are where the bite matches the push. Boxes below it have weaker bite than push, and boxes above it have stronger bite.

Insights

  • The harder a government is pushed, the harder its constraining bite. Enabling bite is less tied to pressure, so governments choose how much of it to provide.
  • When policy goes well beyond what the pressure on the government explains, the extra is always enabling bite. This applies to policy in the United Kingdom, Singapore, India, South Korea, Japan, and the UAE and Saudi Arabia. In each, enabling bite is strong and constraining bite is weak to moderate.
  • United States policy bites harder than EU policy, despite the US reputation for light regulation. US export controls and procurement rules constrain more than EU rules do today, and US enabling bite is much higher. Overall strength of bite is 67 against 47.
  • The size of a jurisdiction's private AI industry does not follow how much its government does to enable it. California's private investment is 5.1% of its economy, more than five times the next highest (the United States federal figure, 0.9%), yet its enabling bite is the lowest of the twelve jurisdictions with investment figures. Among the other eleven there is no consistent link either. China and Japan have very low investment and strong enabling bite, while the EU has similarly low investment and weaker enabling bite.

These patterns come from sixteen scores made by judgement, so treat them as indications and not proof. Investment figures exist for twelve of the sixteen.

Planning

What the scores suggest

Written for an Australian organisation deciding how to govern its own use of AI. It reads patterns across sixteen entries that were scored by judgement at a single date. The patterns are prompts for planning and are not statistical findings. None has been tested against outcomes.

The gap between push and bite as a forecast

Governments tend to move their bite toward their push, with a delay. Two measures show where that is likely to happen.

  • Gap: the strength of the push minus the strength of the bite, using the scores on the strength chart. A gap of 10 points or more is treated as a signal, because a one-step change in a single rating moves a score by about that much.
  • Push stronger than bite: bite is more likely to rise, toward the side the push comes from. A promotion-led push is met with funding and relief, and a protection-led push with duties.
  • Bite stronger than push: the government has acted before the pressure arrived, and the extra bite is more likely to be reduced if pressure falls. When the extra bite is enabling bite, a harm incident that raises the push is the more likely way for the gap to narrow.
  • Lean: the direction of the bite minus the direction of the push, using the direction chart. A positive lean means the bite is more constraining than the push behind it.

Scoring and evidence

How the scores are built

Every score is a judgement made from the research report. These tables show each rating, the evidence behind it and the limits of that evidence.

How push is scored

Each factor is rated 0 to 3 for how strongly it operated on policy in 2025 and 2026: 0 no evidence, 1 stated only or weak evidence, 2 documented, 3 dominant. Direction is the protection total as a share of both totals. Strength is both totals as a share of the maximum of 24.

Factors pushing toward promotion

Strategic rivalry. Competition between states and national security uses.

Sovereignty. Domestic control of compute, data and models.

Growth and investment. Productivity gains and competition for AI investment.

Industry and partner pressure. Lobbying by developers and deployers, and pressure from a larger state to loosen rules.

Factors pushing toward protection

Rights tradition. Fundamental rights, anti-discrimination law and constitutional values.

Public harm and demand. Child and consumer safety, public opinion, and incidents that create a window for action.

Labour and stability. Displacement, algorithmic management and the political cost of job loss.

Information control. Control of content and the information environment.

How bite is scored

Bite has two sides. Constraining bite is how hard rules bind the actors they apply to. Enabling bite is how much the state commits to building and adopting AI. Direction is constraining bite as a share of both. Strength is the average of the two. The sides describe what the policy does to firms, not why: a constraint can serve a promotion aim, as United States export controls do.

Constraining bite

Each component is rated 0 to 3 for the main instruments operating on the reference date. Constraining bite is 60 per cent the lowest component and 40 per cent the average, scaled to 100, because the components form a chain and a missing link disables the rest.

Duty in force. An obligation operates today. 0 none, 3 broad duties in force.
Specificity. The duty is concrete enough to breach. 0 aspirational, 3 thresholds and deadlines.
Point of control. 0 none, 1 penalty after harm, 2 reporting or ongoing supervision, 3 a gate before release.
Detection. A designated, resourced body can see non-compliance. 0 none, 3 routine inspection.
Sanction. Cost of a breach relative to the firm. 0 none, 3 loss of market access or turnover-based fines.
Enforcement. The sanction has been used. 0 no record or not yet possible, 3 routine use.

Enabling bite

Each component is rated 0 to 3. Enabling bite is the plain average scaled to 100, because these measures substitute for each other and do not form a chain.

Investment. Public funding, state compute and data centre programs.

Adoption drive. Diffusion targets, government adoption, procurement preference and skills programs.

Regulatory relief. Active loosening: delays, exceptions, sandboxes, pre-emption or withdrawal of planned rules.

Strategic backing. State-owned vehicles, export promotion, trade measures and deals with developers.

How dot size is set

Dot size shows private AI investment in 2025 as a share of GDP. It is a measured proxy for how much AI weighs in the economy and is not a measure of AI's contribution to output, for which no comparable statistic exists. The area of each dot is proportional to its exact share, so a dot with twice the share has twice the area. Shares run from 0.03 per cent to 5.1 per cent. California, at 5.1 per cent, is larger than the biggest circle in the legend. Small hollow dots have no figure.

All push and bite scores are judgements made from the research report. They are not measurements, and no published index was used. Dot size is the only measured quantity on the charts.

The investment figures count venture and private funding of AI companies by where the company is based. They favour jurisdictions that host developers and say little about adoption, so China and Japan appear small.

Strength of push partly reflects how much evidence the research found. A factor with no evidence is rated 0, so jurisdictions with less coverage (India, Brazil, the Gulf states, Africa, Montana) and those with little independent analysis of motives (Singapore, Japan) will score low on push strength whether or not the pressure is weak.

The nine boxes on each chart come from cutting each axis at one third and two thirds. The cut points are arbitrary, and a jurisdiction near a line could fall either side with a one-point change in a rating. A jurisdiction exactly on a line is placed in the higher cell. The dotted lines compare two scales built in different ways, so distance from the line is indicative.

The push factors condense ten of the report's fourteen drivers. Institutional structure, state capacity, diffusion and electoral politics shape the form of a policy more than its direction and are left out. Enabling bite was not a focus of the research, and compute and industrial policy were covered in less depth than regulation.

Constraining bite is scored on whichever instruments operate on AI products today. In Australia, the United Kingdom, Singapore, India, Brazil and Canada those are mostly online safety, privacy or intermediary laws. Detection and enforcement ratings rest on limited evidence.

Scores reflect what governments do and intend. Self-regulation by firms, such as a developer pausing training, is not counted as bite, and neither is its absence.

United States states are shown as two examples from opposite ends of the range of approach. Montana's scores are inferred from the text of its Act only.
