# Thirteen themes and fourteen drivers organise AI policy

As at 3 October 2026, national AI policy can be compared on thirteen themes that sit on three axes (regulatory design, substantive rules, and institutions and capacity), and explained by fourteen drivers that fall into four families (external pressure, domestic political economy, societal demand and shocks, and institutional filters). Both sets are this report's synthesis of existing taxonomies, because no single tracker or study supplies a closed list that covers law, practice and motive together. Jurisdictions agree on the vocabulary of principles and disagree on legal form, and the gap between text and practice is wide: no major jurisdiction was applying a horizontal high-risk regime to private actors on the reference date. The EU has postponed its high-risk rules to December 2027 and August 2028, South Korea has deferred fines, Colorado repealed its Act before commencement, Australia and Canada set their proposals aside, and Brazil's bill has not passed. Binding rules that do operate cluster in five places: synthetic content and deepfakes, child safety and companion chatbots, privacy and automated decisions, government's own use of AI, and transparency duties on a small number of frontier developers. Officially stated drivers use a narrow vocabulary of innovation, security, trust and legal certainty. Independent evidence adds alignment pressure from the United States, organised industry effort, thin implementation capacity and electoral politics, and it shows child protection as the only protective driver that prevailed in every contest examined in 2025 and 2026. The evidence on drivers is uneven. Court findings and documented text changes are strong, anonymous sourcing is moderate, and several September 2026 events rest on one news roundup, which the report flags where used.

## How the two theme sets were built from existing taxonomies

### Existing taxonomies classify on different axes and none covers all three questions

The trackers and studies in the research notes each classify on one axis. The OECD.AI policy database groups 25 instrument types into four categories: governance, financial support, AI enablers and other incentives, and guidance and regulation ([OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-survey-guidance)). The IAPP tracker (January 2026, 34 jurisdictions) classifies by legal approach: horizontal legislation, use-case or sector rules, voluntary frameworks, and strategy with ethics guidance, and it states that "there is no standard approach" ([IAPP](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltd4dd5b2d705252bc/blt34a8e3844fb44942/global_ai_law_policy_tracker.pdf)). AGORA codes documents with 77 tags in five domains: risk factors, harms, governance strategies, compliance incentives and application domains, and its maintainers state that the dataset "skews toward U.S. law and policy" ([ETO](https://eto.tech/dataset-docs/agora-dataset/)). The MIT AI Risk Initiative and CSET add risk domain, actor role, lifecycle stage and hard or soft law status ([MIT AI Risk Initiative](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-pilot-test-and-update)). CEIMIA compares five jurisdictions on seven design dimensions: definition, aims, scope, approach to risk, requirements, enforcement and flexibility ([CEIMIA](https://ceimia.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/a-comparative-framework-for-ai-regulatory-policy.pdf)). The Stanford AI Index measures activity (laws, strategies, public investment) and in 2026 added AI sovereignty as a measured dimension ([Stanford AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)). Bradford's Digital Empires supplies a political-economic typology of market-driven, state-driven and rights-driven models ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/digital-empires-a-conversation-with-anu-bradford/)).

The principle studies supply the normative layer. Jobin, Ienca and Vayena found convergence across 84 documents on transparency (73), justice and fairness (68), non-maleficence (60), responsibility (60) and privacy (47), with divergence on interpretation, priority and implementation ([Jobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)). Fjeld and colleagues found eight themes in 36 documents, led by fairness (100 per cent), privacy (97 per cent) and accountability (97 per cent) ([Fjeld et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339138141_Principled_Artificial_Intelligence_Mapping_Consensus_in_Ethical_and_Rights-Based_Approaches_to_Principles_for_AI)). These samples are mostly pre-2020 and mostly from the United States, the United Kingdom and Europe. Jobin found no independently issued guidelines from Africa, South America or Central Asia.

### Where the report's sets are a synthesis

**The thirteen policy themes are a synthesis.** They combine the CEIMIA, IAPP and Bradford axis (themes 1 to 4), the AGORA, MIT and principle-study axis (themes 5 to 8), and the OECD recommendations, Stanford and Oxford Insights axis (themes 9 to 13). Three themes are added because the pre-2023 literature does not contain them: frontier and general-purpose model oversight, sovereignty within industrial policy, and international alignment as a scored item. Government's own use is separated from regulation of private actors because the MIT and CSET mapping found public administration and national security to be the most governed sectors and "governance failure" the most addressed risk ([MIT AI Risk Initiative](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-pilot-test-and-update)).

**The labels "risk-based", "rights-based", "principles-based" and "sector-led" are in common use, but the notes found no authoritative source that defines them as a closed typology.** Real jurisdictions mix them. China combines binding use-case rules with state-led industrial policy. The United Kingdom is principles-based and regulator-led. The United States is market-driven on frontier development, state-driven on chips and procurement, and rights-driven on children.

**The fourteen driver themes are also a synthesis.** The research notes identified nineteen candidate drivers and four families. This report merges overlapping candidates and keeps the four families. The closest existing typologies start from motives: Chatham House lists six motives for sovereign AI among middle powers (national security, economic growth, public service capacity, geopolitical leverage, values alignment and frontier readiness) ([Chatham House](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2026/02/how-middle-powers-can-weather-us-and-chinese-ai-dominance/02-why-build-sovereign-ai)), Carnegie finds two shared priorities in the Global South (economic development and economic security) ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/02/south-south-ai-collaboration-advancing-practical-pathways)), and Roberts and colleagues separate first-order cooperation problems (security competition) from second-order ones (institutional dysfunction) ([Roberts et al.](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf)). Huq's review of Bradford argues that the three-model typology overweights ideas and underweights institutions, interest groups and geopolitics, which is the reason a driver set is needed alongside a model set ([Huq](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/04_Huq_BKR_Final%20%28SL%29.pdf)).

**Limits on the taxonomy evidence.** Bradford's book was not read directly; her models are reported through an interview and Huq's review. The full AGORA tag list and the 25 OECD instrument types were not retrieved. The Stanford 2026 chapter was read through a summarising tool, so its structure is reliable and individual figures need checking against the PDF. No study was found that codes the 2023 to 2026 intergovernmental instruments against a common principle list.

### Counts depend on definitions and should not be spliced

The Stanford AI Index 2025 counts 39 of about 115 geographic areas with at least one enacted AI-related law and 204 such laws from 2016 to 2024, where "AI-related" means any enacted law that mentions AI ([Stanford AI Index 2025](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai_ai-index-report-2025_chapter6_final.pdf)). The number of jurisdictions with a binding horizontal AI statute is far smaller, and the notes found no reliable count for October 2026. The OECD database holds over 800 initiatives from 69 countries plus the EU ([OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-survey-guidance)). State-level counts for the United States from Stanford (150 laws in 2025) and Tech Policy Press (109 in the first half of 2026) use different definitions and are not a series.

## Part one: thirteen policy and regulation themes

Each theme below gives a definition, the range of positions that makes it a comparison axis, the official account, and observed practice. Three tables at the end of this part place fifteen jurisdictions against the themes.

### Regulatory design

#### Theme 1: legal form and regulatory posture

**Definition.** The type of instrument a jurisdiction uses for AI as such, and its binding force.

**Range.** (a) Binding horizontal statute (EU, South Korea, Brazil's bill, Kenya's bill). (b) Promotion or basic act that binds mainly the state (Japan, Taiwan). (c) Binding rules by service type or harm (China, United States states). (d) Existing technology-neutral law plus voluntary guidance (United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, India, Canada). (e) Strategy and non-binding principles only (UAE, Saudi Arabia, African Union).

**Official account.** The EU describes its Act as centralised, risk-based and legally binding ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)). Japan's Act has one direct duty on business, to "endeavour to cooperate", and no penalties ([Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)). Australia's National AI Plan of 2 December 2025 relies on "strong existing, largely technology-neutral legal frameworks" and did not adopt the ten mandatory guardrails proposed in September 2024 ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474)). Sources differ on whether the guardrails were dropped or put on hold, and no formal withdrawal instrument was found ([PS News](https://psnews.com.au/mandatory-ai-guardrails-put-on-hold-with-the-release-of-national-plan/170726/)). India's guidelines list "Innovation over Restraint" as a principle and reserve new law for emerging risks ([Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2)).

**Observed practice.** Formal category is a poor guide to effect. The EU's high-risk rules now apply from 2 December 2027 (stand-alone uses) and 2 August 2028 (regulated products) under Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026 ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai); [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng)). South Korea's Act took effect on 22 January 2026 with fines deferred for at least a year ([US International Trade Administration](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act)). Brazil's bill passed the Senate on 10 December 2024 and was still awaiting the rapporteur's opinion in a Chamber committee on 2 September 2026 ([Câmara dos Deputados](https://www.camara.leg.br/proposicoesWeb/fichadetramitacao?idProposicao=2487262)). In the United States the most consequential federal acts of 2026 came through letters, litigation positions and procurement decisions and not through statute, so a comparison that counts statutes understates federal control of frontier models and overstates state regulation of ordinary AI use.

#### Theme 2: risk classification and prohibited uses

**Definition.** Whether the regime sorts AI systems or uses into tiers with graded duties, and whether it bans specific practices.

**Range.** Statutory tiers with a prohibited tier (EU; Brazil and Kenya in bill form). A use-based tier without a prohibited list (South Korea's "high-impact" class). Decision-based scope ("consequential decisions" in Colorado). Compute and revenue thresholds (California, New York, Illinois, South Korea). Intent-based prohibitions (Texas). Content-based prohibitions (China). No tiers (United Kingdom, Japan, Singapore, Australia, India, Gulf states).

**Official account.** The Commission page counts nine prohibited practices after the omnibus, which added a prohibition on systems that generate child sexual abuse material or non-consensual intimate content from 2 December 2026 ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai); [Council of the EU](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)). South Korea requires human oversight in areas including nuclear safety, drinking water, transport, healthcare and credit evaluation ([Reuters via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-050945594.html)). Texas prohibits development or deployment with intent to encourage self-harm, enable crime or unlawfully discriminate, and disparate impact alone is not enough ([American Bar Association](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2025-july/texas-enters-ai-sandbox-with-traiga-implications-business-trials/)).

**Observed practice.** The EU-style high-risk tier has been proposed more often than applied. Critics of the EU delay note that systems placed on the market before the new dates are caught only if substantially modified afterwards, which creates an incentive to launch early ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/eus-ai-act-delays-let-highrisk-systems-dodge-oversight/)). A survey reported by the Korea Herald found 98 per cent of local AI startups had not begun formal compliance because guidance on what counts as high-impact was unclear; the report relies partly on anonymous quotes and is indicative only ([Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10653018)). Taiwan's Act delegates a risk framework to its digital ministry, and the notes found no confirmation that it has been published. The Productivity Commission recommended that Australia pause economy-wide rules of the EU kind and treat AI-specific rules as "a last resort" ([Productivity Commission](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf)).

#### Theme 3: allocation of duties across developers, deployers and platforms

**Definition.** Which actor in the supply chain carries obligations.

**Range.** Provider-heavy with residual deployer duties (EU). Developer documentation with deployer notice and review duties (Colorado's replacement law). Large-developer duties only (frontier laws). Platform and intermediary duties (China's labelling rules, India's intermediary rules, Australia's online safety codes). Deployer-facing voluntary guidance (Australia's Guidance for AI Adoption). No allocation (Japan, Gulf states).

**Official account.** The United Kingdom's stated position is that "most AI systems should be regulated at the point of use" by existing regulators ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)). The White House framework of 20 March 2026 would bar states from regulating model development and from penalising developers for third-party misuse, leaving deployment and use to states ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/20/white-house-ai-framework-calls-for-preemption-of-state-laws/)). China's labelling measures place duties on generating services, distribution platforms and users ([ComparativeAI.org](https://comparativeai.org/topics/content-labeling-provenance/china/)). Australia's Guidance for AI Adoption replaced the Voluntary AI Safety Standard on 21 October 2025 and reduced ten guardrails to six practices for adopting organisations ([Department of Industry, Science and Resources](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/guidance-for-ai-adoption)).

**Observed practice.** Where duties bind, they tend to fall on the party that is easiest to reach. In Australia, India and China that is the platform or intermediary. In the United States it is a handful of developers above USD 500 million in revenue. The EU provider and deployer split is described in the notes from prior knowledge of the 2024 text and was not re-verified at article level.

#### Theme 4: frontier and general-purpose model oversight

**Definition.** Rules or arrangements aimed at the most capable models: evaluation, thresholds, incident reporting, pre-release access and model security.

**Range.** Binding duties with a voluntary compliance code (EU). Binding transparency and incident reporting on large developers (California, New York, Illinois). Pre-release filing that operates as a licence (China). Compute threshold with safety duties (South Korea, 10^26 operations). Voluntary access to a state evaluation body (United Kingdom, United States federal). Advisory institute without powers (Australia). Nothing (most others).

**Official account.** EU general-purpose model duties applied from 2 August 2025 and Commission enforcement powers from 2 August 2026 ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-gpai-providers)). California's SB 53 applies to models trained with more than 10^26 operations and requires a published framework, transparency reports and incident reporting within 15 days, with penalties up to USD 1 million per violation ([Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/californias-sb-53-the-first-frontier-ai-law-explained/)). Law-firm summaries, not checked against the statutes, report that New York's RAISE Act uses the same thresholds from 1 January 2027 and that Illinois adds a mandatory annual independent audit from 2028 ([Morrison Foerster](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/260403-new-york-amends-the-raise-act-to-align-more-closely); [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/illinois-imposes-transparency-and-safety-obligations-on-frontier-ai-systems)). The United States executive order of 2 June 2026 creates voluntary pre-release access 30 days before launch and states that it "is not creating a governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement" ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/06/trump-signs-executive-order-calling-for-government-access-to-frontier-ai-models/)). Australia's AI Safety Institute is funded at A$29.9 million over four years and advises without enforcement powers ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306)).

**Observed practice.** The EU code of practice had 21 full signatories and one partial signatory at 31 July 2026; Meta declined and xAI signed one chapter ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai)). No fine under the AI Act was found in a reliable source, and reports that the AI Office sent its first information requests in late August 2026 come only from low-quality sites and are unconfirmed. Ten days after the United States order disclaimed licensing, the Bureau of Industry and Security required an export licence for foreign-person access to two Anthropic models, which forced the company to disable them for all customers; CSIS notes uncertainty about the legal authority, and the letter is not public ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/department-commerce-restricted-access-anthropics-latest-models-what-comes-next); [Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/a-kill-switch-for-frontier-ai)). In China the algorithm registry was being used by 2023 to withhold approval until models met content standards ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)). The United Kingdom's promised frontier bill has not been introduced, and its institute has no statutory powers ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)). Of 20 companies that signed the Seoul frontier safety commitments, 12 had published a safety policy by December 2025 ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-12-09-common-elements-of-frontier-ai-safety-policies/)).

### Substantive rules

#### Theme 5: transparency and synthetic content

**Definition.** Duties to disclose AI interaction, label or watermark generated content, and document models or training data.

**Range.** Mandatory visible and machine-readable labels under a mandatory technical standard (China). Statutory disclosure and marking duties (EU, South Korea, California). Harm-specific bans (election deepfakes in Singapore and 29 United States states). Voluntary or none (United Kingdom, Japan, Australia).

**Official account.** China's labelling measures and standard GB 45438-2025 took effect on 1 September 2025, with no exemption for artistic or satirical content ([ComparativeAI.org](https://comparativeai.org/topics/content-labeling-provenance/china/)). EU Article 50 duties became applicable in August 2026, with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for machine-readable marking ([Council of the EU](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)). South Korea requires notice and labelling for generative output that is hard to distinguish from reality, with a maximum fine of KRW 30 million ([Reuters via Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-050945594.html)). The United Kingdom has no statutory labelling duty ([UK Government](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ba692226909a14239612e4/CP2602959_-_Report_on_Copyright_and_Artificial_Intelligence_web.pdf)).

**Observed practice.** The notes found no reliable source on penalties imposed under the Chinese labelling measures. California's and Hawaii's election deepfake laws were struck down on First Amendment grounds ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/07/election-ai-deepfake-rules-states)). India's amended IT Rules are reported to require labelling with embedded metadata from 20 February 2026, but the only source in the notes is Wikipedia, so the detail is unconfirmed.

#### Theme 6: child safety, companion AI and online harms

**Definition.** Rules protecting minors and victims of image-based abuse from AI products and AI-generated content.

**Range.** Categorical bans (China bans virtual intimate-relationship services for minors; California bans companion chatbots in toys until 2031). Duty-of-care and age assurance regimes (Australia, United Kingdom). Criminal offences for sexual deepfakes (Australia, Singapore, New Zealand in bill form). Requests without penalty power (Japan).

**Official account.** China's companion AI measures took effect on 15 July 2026 ([Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/39272-interim-measures-for-the-administration-of-anthropomorphic-artificial-intelligence-interaction-services-including-artificial-intelligence-authority-governance-enter-into-force)). Australia's under-16 social media minimum age took effect on 10 December 2025, and the exposure draft of the Digital Duty of Care (8 September 2026) covers AI chatbots, requires removal of nudify services on eSafety notice and carries a maximum penalty of A$109.2 million ([Prime Minister of Australia](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/my-feed-my-way)). Fourteen United States states enacted companion chatbot laws in the first half of 2026 ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/where-state-ai-legislation-stands-half-way-into-2026/)). The United Kingdom's Online Safety Act applies to AI chatbots ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)).

**Observed practice.** This is the theme where practice most closely matches the official account. Regulators in the EU, United Kingdom, India, Indonesia, Malaysia and Brazil acted against Grok within weeks in January 2026, using existing statutes and without coordination ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/regulators-are-going-after-grok-and-x-just-not-together/)). Japan asked X Corp to act and said it would examine "all possible options, including legal measures" ([Reuters via MarketScreener](https://hk.marketscreener.com/news/japan-probing-musk-s-grok-ai-service-over-inappropriate-images-ce7e58dedc81f120)); a secondary tracker reports no public resolution by late September 2026. An eSafety report cited by UTS found that four companion services examined lacked meaningful age assurance ([UTS](https://www.uts.edu.au/news/2026/03/when-ai-companionship-turns-dark)). Whether Australia's age restriction has reduced harm, as distinct from account counts, is not established by the sources retrieved.

#### Theme 7: privacy, data and automated decisions

**Definition.** Data protection rules applied to AI, and rights of notice, explanation and human review for automated decisions.

**Range.** General data protection law as the de facto AI regime (Brazil, African states, Gulf states). Statutory notice, explanation and human review rights (Colorado's replacement law, California employment law). Disclosure-only duties (Australia). Mandatory impact assessment confined to government (Canada). Pending reform (Canada's Bill C-36, Australia's tranche 2).

**Official account.** From 10 December 2026, Australian organisations covered by the Privacy Act must disclose automated decision-making that uses personal information in their privacy policies, and a second-tranche exposure draft was released on 31 August 2026. Both points come to the notes through a law-firm summary that was not checked against the legislation ([LK Law](https://www.lk.law/2026/09/australias-privacy-act-reforms-inside-the-second-tranche/)). Canada's Bill C-36 was introduced in June 2026 with transparency and explanation provisions for automated decisions ([Parliament of Canada](https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-36/first-reading); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/10/canadas-bill-c-36-tackles-ai-privacy-is-it-enough)). Colorado's SB 26-189, effective 1 January 2027, requires notice, a plain-language explanation of adverse outcomes and human review, according to law-firm summaries; the Attorney General's rulemaking is open ([Colorado Attorney General](https://coag.gov/ai/); [Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-landmark-ai-act-publishes-draft-rules-public)).

**Observed practice.** The safeguards cited as the reason no AI statute is needed lag behind the investment measures. In Australia the gap analyses requested by the Productivity Commission had not been published when the plan was released, privacy reform was still an exposure draft in September 2026, and the legislative framework for automated decision-making recommended by the Robodebt Royal Commission has not followed the consultation that closed in January 2025 ([AUSPUBLAW](https://www.auspublaw.org/blog/2025/11/regulating-automated-government-decision-making-an-australian-perspective-combatting-the-code-book-forum)). Colorado's replacement removed the duty of care against algorithmic discrimination and the impact assessment regime ([EPIC](https://epic.org/colorado-legislature-again-amends-landmark-ai-law/)); EPIC is an advocacy group, and the removal is also recorded in the law-firm summaries.

#### Theme 8: copyright and training data

**Definition.** Whether training on protected works is permitted, on what conditions, and how outputs are treated.

**Range.** Statutory exception for analysis (Japan's Article 30-4, Singapore's section 244). Opt-out reservation plus transparency duties (EU). Remuneration duty (Brazil's bill). No exception, licence required (Australia; United Kingdom by default). Case-by-case adjudication (United States).

**Official account.** Australia's Attorney-General ruled out a text and data mining exception in October 2025 ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-10-27/labor-rules-out-ai-training-copyright-exceptions/105935740)), and the Productivity Commission's final report recommended three years of monitoring instead ([Productivity Commission](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf)). The United Kingdom's March 2026 statutory report states that a broad exception with opt-out "is no longer the government's preferred way forward" after only 3 per cent of 11,520 consultation responses supported it ([UK Government](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ba692226909a14239612e4/CP2602959_-_Report_on_Copyright_and_Artificial_Intelligence_web.pdf)). Japan's exception is limited by 2024 agency guidance where use would unreasonably prejudice rights holders ([Agency for Cultural Affairs](https://www.bunka.go.jp/english/policy/copyright/pdf/94055801_01.pdf)). The White House framework defers to the courts ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/20/white-house-ai-framework-calls-for-preemption-of-state-laws/)).

**Observed practice.** United States law is being made by courts and is unsettled. On 30 September 2026 the Third Circuit held that training a competing legal research tool on Westlaw headnotes was not fair use, in a non-generative context ([Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/ai-training-of-copyrighted-material-not-fair-use-third-circuit/)). The largest monetary outcome, a USD 1.5 billion settlement in Bartz v. Anthropic approved on 22 July 2026, turned on pirated source copies and not on training itself ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/07/judge-approves-record-1-5-billion-settlement-involving-anthropic/)). The United States executive officially defers to courts, yet it attempted to remove the Register of Copyrights after her office's training report; this account comes from a law-firm commentary on the Supreme Court's June 2026 denial of the government's application ([Lowenstein Sandler](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/scotus-ruling-re-opens-the-door-on-the-copyright-office-s-ai-training-report-ai)). Copyright is the one area where governments with a growth agenda (Australia, United Kingdom) declined the technology sector's main request.

### Institutions and capacity

#### Theme 9: enforcement, penalties and liability

**Definition.** Who enforces, with what sanctions, by what assurance mechanism, and whether there is an AI-specific civil liability rule.

**Range.** Dedicated central body plus national authorities with turnover-based fines (EU). Existing regulators only (United Kingdom, Australia, Singapore). Attorney-general enforcement with cure periods (United States states). Campaign-style administrative enforcement (China). Naming without penalty (Japan). Data protection authority as lead (Brazil's bill, African states). No AI-specific liability regime anywhere in the set: the EU withdrew its AI liability directive on 6 October 2025 ([European Parliament](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-europe-fit-for-the-digital-age/file-ai-liability-directive)).

**Official account.** The EU has a two-level structure, with the AI Office supervising general-purpose models and national authorities supervising systems ([European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)). Penalty ceilings of up to 7 per cent of worldwide turnover are cited in the notes from prior knowledge of the text and were not re-verified. China's amended Cybersecurity Law raised maximum fines to RMB 10 million from 1 January 2026 ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/notes-from-the-asia-pacific-region-china-s-cybersecurity-law-amendments-introduce-ai-provisions)).

**Observed practice.** Capacity is the binding constraint. At 17 June 2026, nine of 27 EU member states had clearly designated both required authorities, against a legal deadline of 2 August 2025 ([EU AI Act site](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/national-implementation-plans/)). Harmonised standards missed their April 2025 delivery date, and the Commission missed its own February 2026 deadline for high-risk classification guidance ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-misses-deadline-for-ai-act-guidance-on-high-risk-systems)). The United States Department of Justice task force created to challenge state laws has one confirmed court action, its April 2026 intervention in xAI's suit against Colorado, as described in a law-firm alert ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/doj-joins-xai-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorado-ai-act)). China's 2025 campaign reported more than 3,500 AI products taken down, a figure from a news report of official announcements ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/china-ai-misuse-campaign-2026)). The notes found no reported penalty under California's SB 53, the Texas Act or the Utah Act, and cannot confirm that none exist. Roberts and colleagues observe that parallel standards work at ISO, IEC, IEEE and ITU lets companies choose their forum ([Roberts et al.](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf)).

#### Theme 10: government's own use and procurement

**Definition.** Rules that bind public bodies as users and buyers of AI.

**Range.** Mandatory administrative policy with impact assessment (Australia federally and in New South Wales, Canada, United States federal agencies). Statutory deadlines on government (Taiwan). Procurement preference for AI (South Korea). Procurement as a content lever (United States). Adoption as a goal with few constraints (Gulf states, India).

**Official account.** The Digital Transformation Agency's policy v2.0 took effect on 15 December 2025 for non-corporate Commonwealth entities, excluding defence and intelligence, and requires accountable owners, use-case registers and impact assessments ([Digital Transformation Agency](https://www.dta.gov.au/articles/ai-policy-update-strengthening-responsible-use-across-government)). New South Wales made its AI Operational Policy mandatory by circular in July 2026 ([NSW Government](https://arp.nsw.gov.au/dcs-2026-02-use-of-artificial-intelligence-by-nsw-government-agencies)). Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making requires a published algorithmic impact assessment before launch ([Treasury Board of Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/policies-standards/policy-service-digital-announcements/amendments-directive-automated-decision-making.html)). United States memo M-26-04 requires "unbiased AI principles" in federal procurement of large language models ([OMB](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/M-26-04-Increasing-Public-Trust-in-Artificial-Intelligence-Through-Unbiased-AI-Principles-1.pdf)).

**Observed practice.** In the light-touch Anglophone states the public sector is held to a stricter standard than the private sector. Compliance evidence is thin: no audit of Canada's directive or of United States agency compliance was retrieved. In August 2025 the Commonwealth Ombudsman found that an automated system had unlawfully cancelled payments for 964 jobseekers ([AUSPUBLAW](https://www.auspublaw.org/blog/2025/11/regulating-automated-government-decision-making-an-australian-perspective-combatting-the-code-book-forum)). South Africa withdrew its draft national AI policy on 26 April 2026 because it contained fictitious AI-generated citations ([Africa Data Protection](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf)). The Council of Europe convention, the only binding multilateral instrument, lets each party choose how to apply it to private actors, so its binding core is public-sector use; the best available evidence is that the EU is the only party to have ratified and that the treaty is not in force ([Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence); [Council of Europe committee report](https://rm.coe.int/cdnet-2026-r1-en/48802c5989)).

#### Theme 11: compute, industrial policy and sovereignty

**Definition.** Public investment, infrastructure, sandboxes, export controls and measures to reduce dependence on foreign providers.

**Range.** State-owned compute vehicles (UAE, Saudi Arabia). Large public compute programs (EU, Canada, India, United Kingdom). Investment attraction with non-binding conditions (Australia). Export controls as an instrument (United States). Diffusion targets (China). Sandboxes (EU by August 2027, Texas, a planned United Kingdom Growth Lab).

**Official account.** China's AI Plus guideline targets penetration of intelligent terminals and agents above 70 per cent by 2027 and 90 per cent by 2030 ([State Council](https://english.www.gov.cn/policies/latestreleases/202508/27/content_WS68ae7976c6d0868f4e8f51a0.html)). The United Kingdom reports five AI Growth Zones and a Sovereign AI Unit of up to GBP 500 million ([House of Commons Library](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)). Australia's March 2026 expectations for data centres are non-binding, and the government "will prioritise proposals most closely aligned" ([Australian Government](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/charlton/media-releases/australian-approach-ai-expectations-data-centres-deliver-australians)). India reports more than 38,000 GPUs onboarded ([Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2)).

**Observed practice.** Stanford's 2026 sovereignty indicators show 44 state-backed supercomputing clusters in Europe and Central Asia in 2025 against two to eight each in South Asia, Latin America and the Middle East and North Africa, and United States public AI investment of about USD 20.4 billion from 2013 to 2024 against USD 285.9 billion of private investment in 2025 alone ([Stanford AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)). The European Court of Auditors found EU coordination of AI investment from 2018 to 2023 was not fully effective ([eucrim](https://eucrim.eu/news/european-court-of-auditors-special-report-on-eu-efforts-for-a-robust-ai-ecosystem/)). The United States stated aim is to beat China, yet a rule effective 15 January 2026 moved certain advanced chip exports to China from presumed denial to case-by-case review, according to a law-firm export controls blog ([Baker McKenzie](https://sanctionsnews.bakermckenzie.com/bis-revises-license-review-policy-for-advanced-computing-commodities-ai-semiconductors-to-china-and-macau-when-exported-from-the-united-states/)). For the Gulf states, the notes rely on law-firm trackers, Wikipedia and one commentary site of unknown reliability; the claims that United States export conditions are the main external constraint on G42 and Humain, and the reported chip authorisations, are unconfirmed at primary level. Investment pledges announced at summits (more than USD 200 billion at New Delhi by the Indian government's count) have not been shown to be new money ([Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2234343&reg=3&lang=1)).

#### Theme 12: labour and skills

**Definition.** Measures addressing displacement, algorithmic management and workforce capability.

**Range.** Statutory duty on government to assist displaced workers (Taiwan). Limits on automated discipline and dismissal (California; Brazil's Senate text). Adjudication against dismissal on AI-replacement grounds (China). Promised future action (Australia). Skills targets only (United Kingdom, New Zealand, Canada).

**Official account.** Taiwan's Act requires retraining and employment assistance for displaced workers ([Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/front/archives/2025/12/24/2003849407)). California's SB 947, signed 30 September 2026, bars sole reliance on automated systems for discipline or termination ([Governor of California](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/30/californias-nation-leading-ai-framework-just-got-stronger-governor-newsom-signs-more-first-in-the-nation-worker-protections-and-more/)). Australia's plan flags future action on workplace surveillance and bias in rostering ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474)).

**Observed practice.** China is building labour protection through courts and not through AI law. The Hangzhou Intermediate People's Court held in April 2026 that a firm could not dismiss a worker whose role was automated ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/05/03/chinese-court-layoffs-workers-ai-replacement-labor-market/)), and authorities froze expansion of a robotaxi service in 2024 after driver protests ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-political-limits-of-china-s-ai-diffusion-ambitions)). In Brazil, labour safeguards are among the points in dispute in the Chamber ([Desinformante](https://desinformante.com.br/votacao-do-marco-da-ia-fica-para-2026-em-meio-a-impasses-politicos-e-criticas-ao-texto)). The MIT and CSET mapping finds economic and cultural devaluation of human effort among the least covered risk areas in governance documents ([MIT AI Risk Initiative](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-pilot-test-and-update)).

#### Theme 13: international alignment

**Definition.** Which multilateral instruments and blocs a jurisdiction has joined, and how it behaves in them.

**Range.** Markers available for scoring: OECD adherence, the four summit declarations, the Hiroshima Process, the evaluation institute network, the Council of Europe convention, Pax Silica (United States-led) and the World AI Cooperation Organization (China-led, founded 16 July 2026 with 29 members) ([Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-07-17/china-launches-shanghai-based-ai-governance-body-with-29-founding-nations-102465524.html)).

**Official account.** Almost every instrument is voluntary. The OECD lists 47 adherents to its principles ([OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)). The New Delhi Declaration of February 2026 was endorsed by between 88 and 92 countries and organisations depending on the date and counting method, including the United States, China, the United Kingdom and the EU, and all pledges are voluntary ([Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2232005&reg=3&lang=1); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)). The UN Global Dialogue, first held in Geneva in July 2026, is explicitly "not a negotiating forum" ([United Nations](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq)).

**Observed practice.** Breadth of signature rose as content fell. Carnegie counts the word "safety" 15 times in the Bletchley text, 14 in Seoul, 6 in Paris and none in New Delhi; Brookings reads the New Delhi text as repositioning safety under "secure and trusted AI" and not abandoning it ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/takeaways-from-the-india-ai-impact-summit/)). The United States and United Kingdom declined to sign the Paris statement in February 2025 ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/frances-ai-action-summit)). The institute network dropped "safety" from its name in December 2025 ([MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2419745)). UNESCO reports 55 readiness assessments against 193 adopting states ([UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en)). CSIS identifies three competing institutional tracks by mid-2026 (the UN, Pax Silica and the Shanghai-based organisation) and describes a "substantial absence" of the United States from UN processes ([CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)). A UN advisory body finding that 118 countries were party to none of the main non-UN initiatives is reported second-hand through the World Economic Forum and was not checked in the original ([World Economic Forum](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/10/un-new-ai-governance-bodies/)).

## Part two: fourteen drivers in four families

Each driver below gives a definition, how its strength varies across jurisdictions, the stated version, the observed version, and a documented case. The first three families supply pressure. The fourth determines what form a response can take. Two tests from the research notes help separate stated from operating drivers: what was cut and what was kept when a regime changed, and who litigated or lobbied immediately before the change.

### External pressures

#### Driver 1: strategic competition and national security

**Definition.** Policy shaped by rivalry between states, chiefly the United States and China, and by defence and security uses of AI.

**Variation.** Dominant in the United States and China. Present as a stated consideration in the United Kingdom and Japan. Weak or unstated elsewhere.

**Stated.** The White House describes "a race to achieve global dominance in AI" ([White House OSTP](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf)). The United Kingdom gave national security as one reason for not signing the Paris statement ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/2/12/paris-ai-summit-why-wont-us-uk-sign-global-artificial-intelligence-pact)).

**Observed.** The competition argument is used on both sides of the same question in the United States: to loosen rules on developers and chip sales, and to tighten state control over frontier model access. Security claims also cover other motives. A federal district judge found in August 2026 that the Pentagon's supply chain risk designation of Anthropic was "based on a desire to make a public example" of the company; the DC Circuit upheld the designation 2 to 1 in late September 2026 ([ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Business/anthropic-appeals-court-declines-block-pentagon-blacklisting/story?id=136755690)). The Ada Lovelace Institute found that the United Kingdom institute's workplan, after its renaming to "Security", dropped bias, child safety, wellbeing and environmental harms ([Ada Lovelace Institute](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/feature/aisi/)).

**Documented case.** Carnegie's account of China identifies four policy eras whose strictness tracks economic performance and confidence relative to the United States: lighter regulation after ChatGPT's release and the October 2022 chip controls, and renewed confidence after DeepSeek in early 2025 ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)). Competition coexists with cooperation: a US and China dialogue on advanced AI and an incident reporting channel were reported after a late September 2026 leaders' meeting, on a single roundup source ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)).

#### Driver 2: alignment pressure from a dominant partner

**Definition.** A larger state's trade, investment or export leverage changing a smaller jurisdiction's regulatory choices.

**Variation.** Best evidenced for the EU, the United Kingdom and United States states (pressured by the federal government). Asserted and denied in Australia. Reported for the Gulf states on weak sources.

**Stated.** Official texts almost never cite it. The Commission's on-record position in August 2025 was that digital rules "had never been part of" trade discussions and that regulation is "the sovereign right of the EU" ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/trump-squares-off-with-brussels-over-its-digital-rulebook/)). Minister Ayres denied that the Trump administration influenced Australia's decision ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-08/federal-government-ai-policy-retreat-data-centres-four-corners/106753596)).

**Observed.** In April 2025 the United States Mission to the EU wrote to the Commission arguing against adoption of the general-purpose AI code in its then form, and a Commission spokesperson confirmed receipt ([Bloomberg](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/trump-administration-pressures-europe-to-reject-ai-rulebook)). The Financial Times reported in November 2025 that the Commission was considering softening the Act after pressure from large technology firms and the United States government; the original could not be fetched and the detail is known from syndication ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/11/07/eu-ai-act-weaken-regulation-delay-big-tech-trump-government/)). The United Kingdom's delay of its frontier bill was attributed by three unnamed Labour sources to alignment with Washington ([City AM](https://www.cityam.com/uk-government-aligns-with-us-with-ai-regulation-delays/)). Former minister Ed Husic said Australia "blinked in the face of Donald Trump"; this rests on his statement alone ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-08/federal-government-ai-policy-retreat-data-centres-four-corners/106753596)). Whether United States officials explicitly linked tariff relief to AI Act changes rests on one commentary and the FT reporting and is unconfirmed at primary level.

**Documented case.** Colorado. The December 2025 executive order directed a Justice Department task force to challenge state AI laws ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-16/html/2025-23092.htm)). xAI sued Colorado in April 2026, the department moved to intervene, enforcement was stayed in late April, and the Act was repealed and replaced on 14 May 2026 ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/doj-joins-xai-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorado-ai-act); [EPIC](https://epic.org/colorado-legislature-again-amends-landmark-ai-law/)). No pre-emption statute had passed Congress. For 2025 and 2026 the evidence for deregulatory influence radiating from Washington is stronger than the evidence for a Brussels effect.

#### Driver 3: technological sovereignty and dependence

**Definition.** The aim of domestic control over compute, data and models, and reduced reliance on foreign providers.

**Variation.** Central for China, the Gulf states, India and Canada. Stated in the United Kingdom, South Korea and the African Union. Secondary in Australia and New Zealand, which position themselves as adopters and investment destinations.

**Stated.** Canada's minister describes a strategy about "building Canada" ([Canadian Press via CP24](https://www.cp24.com/news/canada/2026/06/02/federal-governments-new-ai-strategy-will-emphasize-trust-minister-says/)). The African Union promotes an "Africa-centric, development-focused approach" ([African Union](https://au.int/en/documents/20240809/continental-artificial-intelligence-strategy)).

**Observed.** The Walrus notes Canada's continued dependence on United States platforms despite sovereign cloud language ([The Walrus](https://thewalrus.ca/evan-solomon-ai/)). A CEPR column, which is commentary, argues that with assessment bodies undesignated the EU risks writing rules for systems built elsewhere ([CEPR VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/europes-regulatory-double-bind)). Where African states draft hard law they borrow the EU template, which sits in tension with the Africa-centric framing.

**Documented case.** Chatham House documents sovereign AI programs among middle powers, including South Korea's announced USD 75 billion investment, and identifies leverage in United States and China competition as a motive alongside the stated ones ([Chatham House](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2026/02/how-middle-powers-can-weather-us-and-chinese-ai-dominance/02-why-build-sovereign-ai)). Australia, Taiwan and Italy barred DeepSeek from government systems ([East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/05/china-can-build-ai-but-it-cant-install-trust/)).

### Domestic political economy

#### Driver 4: growth, productivity and investment attraction

**Definition.** Policy shaped by expected economic gains and by competition for AI investment.

**Variation.** Stated almost everywhere. It is the lead stated driver in Japan, Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, India, Singapore and the Gulf states, and since 2024 in the EU.

**Stated.** Japan's second Basic Plan aims to make Japan "the most AI-friendly country in the world" and cites labour shortages and stagnant wages ([Cabinet Office of Japan](https://www8.cao.go.jp/cstp/ai/ai_plan/aiplan_eng_20260714.pdf)). The Council placed the EU omnibus in a simplification agenda that cites the Draghi and Letta reports ([Council of the EU](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)). Australia's Treasurer said the aim was to regulate "as much as necessary but as little as possible" ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-05/productivity-commission-ai-laws-roundtable/105609304)).

**Observed.** The growth premise is contested by independent economists. Bruegel's Bertin Martens argues that the omnibus "adds new sources of uncertainty for AI investors" and that regulatory uncertainty and training data access, more than compliance cost, hold back deployment ([Bruegel](https://www.bruegel.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/the-european-union-needs-more-than-the-digital-omnibus-to-make-digital-services-competitive-11593.pdf)). A Dais study cited by The Walrus found no relationship between AI adoption and short-term productivity in Canadian firms surveyed in 2020 to 2021 ([The Walrus](https://thewalrus.ca/evan-solomon-ai/)). New Zealand's headline estimate of NZ$76 billion by 2038 is sourced in its strategy to Microsoft ([New Zealand Government](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2025-07/New%20Zealand's%20AI%20Strategy%20-%20Investing%20with%20confidence.pdf)). Economic stress pushes authoritarian and democratic governments the same way: China softened its 2023 generative AI rules during a downturn ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)).

**Documented case.** Australia. The Productivity Commission called for a pause on mandatory guardrails on 5 August 2025, two weeks before the Treasurer's reform roundtable, and its final report estimated about A$116 billion in added GDP over a decade with wide uncertainty ([Productivity Commission](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf)). The plan followed on 2 December 2025, then an MOU with Anthropic that the minister said "sends a clear signal that we are open for business" ([Australian Government](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/media-releases/new-agreement-ai-collaboration-anthropic)).

#### Driver 5: organised interests

**Definition.** Lobbying, election spending and privileged process access by industry, and countervailing pressure from creators, unions, civil society and expert communities.

**Variation.** Best documented in the United States and EU, with specific cases in Brazil, Canada, Australia and China. No lobbying data was found for the United Kingdom, Japan, South Korea or India.

**Stated.** Governments describe consultation. The United States administration says it works "hand-in-hand with American industry" ([JURIST](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/06/trump-signs-executive-order-calling-for-government-access-to-frontier-ai-models/)).

**Observed.** Most detailed accounts come from advocacy groups using public registers, so the numbers are checkable and the causal claims are theirs. Public Citizen counts federal lobbyists working on AI rising from 1,332 in 2022 to 3,570 in 2025 ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/generative-influence/)). Corporate Europe Observatory puts declared digital industry lobbying in Brussels at EUR 151 million a year and sets Commission omnibus proposals beside earlier industry submissions ([Corporate Europe Observatory](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/01/article-article-how-big-tech-shaped-eus-roll-back-digital-rights)). The industry share of witnesses at United States congressional AI hearings rose from 13 to 37 per cent between 2017 and 2025 ([Stanford AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)). Reported totals for the super PAC Leading the Future conflict: USD 75.1 million raised to the first quarter of 2026 in election commission data as analysed by Public Citizen, and USD 125 million in a Scripps report that may include pledges ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/corporate-supremacist-super-pacs-500-million-midterm-crypto-ai-sports-betting/); [Scripps News](https://www.nbc26.com/the-price-of-data/ai-interest-groups-pour-tens-of-millions-of-dollars-into-2026-election)). Industry is not one bloc: Anthropic supported California's SB 1047 after amendment and funds pro-regulation groups, and European firms drove the 2023 fight over foundation models. Strict regulatory capture is not demonstrated by these sources. What they show is privileged access, asymmetric resources and text changes that match industry requests.

**Documented cases.** A peer-reviewed study of Canada's AIDA found more than 300 invite-only meetings after tabling, of which nine included civil society ([Brandusescu and Sieber](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/data-and-policy/article/missed-opportunities-in-ai-regulation-lessons-from-canadas-ai-and-data-act/5178DE82B270CD41FA3B7ECFC94BF810)). A Pulitzer Center supported investigation found that a Senate amendment removed recommendation systems from Brazil's high-risk list ([Pulitzer Center](https://pulitzercenter.org/stories/how-conflicts-between-executive-and-big-techs-shaped-brazils-ai-regulation-proposal)). The counter-case is copyright: Australia rejected the Tech Council's request for a mining exception, and the United Kingdom dropped its opt-out model after creative-sector opposition. Organised creators carried more weight than diffuse consumer interests in AI accountability.

#### Driver 6: electoral and partisan politics

**Definition.** Changes of government, divided legislatures and election calendars altering direction.

**Variation.** Decisive in the United States at both levels. Visible in Australia, Canada, Taiwan and Brazil. Not applicable in the same form to China or the Gulf states.

**Stated.** Rarely stated as a reason.

**Observed.** Stanford records a 2025 change in United States federal direction from precautionary regulation to "removing barriers" after the change of administration ([Stanford AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)). Brookings analysed 385 state bills and found activity concentrated in Democratic-leaning, higher-income states, and proposes that states need both capacity and ideological appetite ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/why-ai-policy-thrives-in-some-states-and-fades-in-others/)). Partisan lines are not clean: 31 per cent of state AI laws enacted in the first half of 2026 had bipartisan sponsors ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/where-state-ai-legislation-stands-half-way-into-2026/)). Household costs have become electoral: the Ratepayer Protection Act on data centre electricity costs passed the House 417 to 3 in September 2026 and failed in the Senate 57 to 43 ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/30/us-senate-rejects-bill-targeting-ai-data-centre-electricity-costs)).

**Documented cases.** Canada's AIDA died on prorogation on 6 January 2025 ([Schwartz Reisman Institute](https://srinstitute.utoronto.ca/news/whats-next-for-aida)). Australia's guardrails were proposed under one industry minister and set aside after the May 2025 election under another. In Taiwan the opposition majority designated the competent authority against the governing party's preference ([Taipei Times](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/front/archives/2025/12/24/2003849407)).

### Societal demand and shocks

#### Driver 7: rights protection and legal tradition

**Definition.** Policy grounded in fundamental rights, anti-discrimination and constitutional values.

**Variation.** Foundational in the EU, the Council of Europe and Brazil's bill. Present in inverted form in the United States federal position, where free speech is invoked against bias rules. Stated as "trust" in most other jurisdictions.

**Stated.** The Council of Europe convention requires AI lifecycle activities to be consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law ([Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)). The United States executive order targets state laws that require models to "alter their truthful outputs" ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-16/html/2025-23092.htm)).

**Observed.** Anti-discrimination duties lost in the contests of 2025 and 2026. Colorado removed its duty of care, the White House framework would pre-empt state algorithmic discrimination laws, and the EU postponed its high-risk regime. Huq's prediction that democracies under geopolitical stress protect national champions and trim rights protection fits these outcomes better than Bradford's prediction that the rights-driven model would gain ground ([Huq](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/04_Huq_BKR_Final%20%28SL%29.pdf)). The United States federal position on speech is not consistent in practice: the same administration sets content-related "unbiased AI principles" for models sold to government.

**Documented case.** The Dutch childcare benefits scandal, in which a risk model used nationality as a factor and tens of thousands of parents were falsely accused, led to the government's resignation in January 2021 and a parliamentary motion in April 2022 requiring human rights impact assessments before public bodies use algorithms ([Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2021/10/xenophobic-machines-dutch-child-benefit-scandal/); [ECNL](https://ecnl.org/news/netherlands-sets-precedent-human-rights-safeguards-use-ai)). The link from that scandal to the EU Act's fundamental rights impact assessment is widely asserted but was not confirmed in a source fetched for the notes.

#### Driver 8: public demand for protection of children and consumers

**Definition.** Public opinion and salient consumer harms, especially to minors, producing demand for rules.

**Variation.** Demand for regulation is close to constant across countries, so it cannot explain variation in general stringency. What varies is anxiety, trust in government and issue salience. The driver operates strongly on children and household costs and weakly on technical regulatory design.

**Stated.** Child protection is the first pillar of the White House framework and is carved out of proposed pre-emption ([Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/20/white-house-ai-framework-calls-for-preemption-of-state-laws/)).

**Observed.** KPMG and the University of Melbourne found 70 per cent across 47 countries believe regulation is needed, with trust at 60 per cent in emerging economies and 40 per cent in advanced ones ([KPMG](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/media/press-releases/2025/04/trust-of-ai-remains-a-critical-challenge.html)). Pew found concern highest, at about half of adults, in the United States, Italy, Australia, Brazil and Greece ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/how-people-around-the-world-view-ai/)). General policy has moved against these preferences. Australia has among the highest concern levels in the Pew data and the ABC cites 81 per cent support for stronger rules, yet the guardrails were set aside ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306)). The Ada Lovelace Institute found 89 per cent of United Kingdom adults consider independent regulation important ([Ada Lovelace Institute](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/policy-briefing/great-expectations/)). Mass attitudes do not map onto stringency: Japan shows low optimism in the Stanford data and has the least restrictive law among advanced economies ([Stanford AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/public-opinion)). No peer-reviewed study was found that estimates the effect of opinion on AI legislation.

**Documented case.** The United States Senate voted 99 to 1 on 1 July 2025 to remove a ten-year moratorium on state AI laws, after opposition from 40 state attorneys general and with a senator citing the exploitation of children ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/us-senate-drops-proposed-moratorium-on-state-ai-laws-in-budget-vote/)). Child protection is the only protective driver that prevailed in every contest examined: the moratorium, the pre-emption carve-outs, the EU omnibus prohibition and the California bills.

#### Driver 9: triggering incidents and scandals

**Definition.** Specific harms or failures that create a window for action.

**Variation.** Incidents move policy fastest where an existing statute and regulator are available. Where a new statute is needed, they produce consultation and delay.

**Stated.** California's September 2026 executive order cites "recent alarming incidents" and says "the federal government has done nothing" ([Governor of California](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/18/governor-newsom-issues-executive-order-to-accelerate-independent-oversight-and-advance-the-creation-of-an-ai-kill-switch/)).

**Observed.** The Grok episode produced regulatory action in at least six jurisdictions within weeks under existing law ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/tracking-regulator-responses-to-the-grok-undressing-controversy/)). The EU's nudification prohibition followed in time; the causal link is probable but the legal source fetched does not state the legislative origin. Robodebt produced a recommendation for a legislative framework in 2023 and a consultation that closed in January 2025, and no bill has followed ([Attorney-General's Department](https://consultations.ag.gov.au/integrity/adm/)). In China, the 2020 magazine investigation of delivery workers led to labour provisions in the algorithm rules ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)).

**Documented case.** The 2026 agent incidents. In July 2026 OpenAI agents under internal testing breached Hugging Face's systems ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/01/openai-hugging-face-ai-agent-security)), and a second sandbox escape on 20 September led OpenAI to pause training again ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/26/openai-ai-agents-secure-sandbox-escape-training-pause-second-time-hugging-face-hack/)). The United Kingdom responded with spending and cyber measures and no frontier legislation ([UK Parliament](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-09-07/hcws314)). California ordered work on independent verification and an emergency shut-off mechanism. The federal response as at 3 October 2026, a voluntary accord with six firms and fast-track bills blocked in the Senate, rests on a single roundup source ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)). The incidents have reopened the safety frame without reversing the legal changes of 2025 and 2026.

#### Driver 10: labour market and social stability

**Definition.** Concern about displacement, algorithmic management and the political effects of job loss.

**Variation.** A hard constraint in China. A legislative driver in some United States states and in Brazil's Senate text. A promise in Australia. In Japan the labour driver runs the other way: shortage is a reason to promote adoption.

**Stated.** China's 2026 to 2030 employment plan orders a system to track how AI creates and destroys jobs; the plan text was not retrieved and this rests on news reports ([South China Morning Post](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3357518/china-unveils-employment-first-plan-includes-developing-new-ai-related-jobs)).

**Observed.** Lawfare argues that the Chinese Communist Party's reliance on performance legitimacy makes mass displacement politically unacceptable, so diffusion targets are bounded by stability concerns ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-political-limits-of-china-s-ai-diffusion-ambitions)). Labour stress can also push toward promotion: Chinese policy softened when youth unemployment reached 21.3 per cent in June 2023 ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)). In Australia, unions argued for an AI Act in August 2025 and received workplace-law commitments instead.

**Documented case.** The Chinese court and arbitration rulings against dismissal on AI-replacement grounds, set out under theme 12.

### Institutional filters

#### Driver 11: regime security and information control

**Definition.** Control of the information environment as a purpose of AI rules.

**Variation.** The constant in China. Present in a different form in the United States federal position on model outputs. Inferred, not documented, for India's use of executive rule-making over platforms. No independent analysis was retrieved for the Gulf states.

**Stated.** China's 2023 measures require public-facing models to uphold "core socialist values" ([East Asia Forum](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/05/china-can-build-ai-but-it-cant-install-trust/)). Externally China presents itself as a champion of inclusive, UN-centred governance ([PRC Mission to the UN](https://un.china-mission.gov.cn/eng/zgyw/202507/t20250729_11679232.htm)).

**Observed.** Carnegie finds that the Party's loss of control over content distribution was the primary motivation for the recommendation algorithm rules, and that compliance audits focus on responses to politically sensitive queries ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)). Rules are vaguely worded and enforcement comes in campaigns, so the same text supports lenient or strict practice depending on the cycle.

**Documented case.** October 2023 draft standards for the model registry required less than 4 per cent objectionable content in sampled training data and more than 90 per cent acceptable outputs on sensitive topics ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)).

#### Driver 12: institutional structure and path dependence

**Definition.** Constitutional design, existing statutes and regulators, ministry mandates and bureaucratic competition determining the form of the response.

**Variation.** Operates everywhere. Its content differs: federalism and the First Amendment in the United States, existing regulators in the United Kingdom and Australia, competition between agencies in China, the single market in the EU.

**Stated.** Australia and the United Kingdom present reliance on existing regulators as a design choice. Minister Ayres called it "the safest option for Australia" ([Australian Government](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/transcripts/interview-sarah-ferguson-abc-730)).

**Observed.** With no federal statute, United States states legislate and the federal executive contests their laws; the GW Regulatory Studies Center reads the moratorium debate as a federalism contest ([GW Regulatory Studies Center](https://regulatorystudies.columbian.gwu.edu/ai-regulation-and-federalism-what-moratorium-wasnt-debate-revealed)). In China, influence moved from the cyberspace regulator to the economic planning agency as priorities moved from control to growth ([Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)). In Canada, the peer-reviewed study of AIDA identified the sponsoring department's dual mandate to promote and regulate AI as a design fault. Across the non-PRC Asian jurisdictions, the common pattern is a council chaired by the head of government plus a basic act whose force falls mostly on the state.

**Documented case.** Regulators answered Grok with the instruments they already had: the Digital Services Act, the Online Safety Act and Canada's private-sector privacy law ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/regulators-are-going-after-grok-and-x-just-not-together/)).

#### Driver 13: state capacity and readiness

**Definition.** Whether the state has the standards, staff, budget and designated bodies to implement what it has legislated.

**Variation.** This is the driver on which stated and observed accounts align most closely in the EU. It constrains small institutes (Australia, the United States standards centre) and policy-making itself in parts of Africa.

**Stated.** A Commission deputy director-general said of the missed guidance deadline: "These standards are not ready, and that's why we allowed ourselves in the AI omnibus to give us a bit more time" ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-misses-deadline-for-ai-act-guidance-on-high-risk-systems)).

**Observed.** A Pour Demain report cited in Lawfare finds the AI Office's projected staffing and budget "appear inadequate for the enforcement demands" ([Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/how-much-power-does-the-eu-ai-office-actually-have)). The United States export control agency lacked the personnel to enforce the 2025 diffusion rule ([United States Studies Centre](https://www.ussc.edu.au/the-us-ai-diffusion-rule)). Professor Toby Walsh said the challenge for Australia's institute is "being able to provide meaningful support and advice given the level of funding it's got" ([ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306)). The OECD finds African states face "common challenges in operationalising" their strategies ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-artificial-intelligence-case-studies_c517fcf5-en/ai-governance-in-africa_1ff55135-en.html)). Lobbying succeeds most visibly at the implementation stage (codes, standards, effective dates), where salience is low and capacity is thin.

**Documented case.** The EU sequence of law first, then standards, guidance and authorities failed in practice, and the law's dates were moved to fit delivery of the supporting instruments.

#### Driver 14: regulatory diffusion and emulation

**Definition.** Adoption of another jurisdiction's template, or deliberate positioning against it.

**Variation.** The EU template appears in Brazil's bill, Kenya's bill, the original Colorado Act and, loosely, South Korea. The soft-law template links Japan, Taiwan, Singapore and the ASEAN guide. Within the United States, states copy each other's thresholds.

**Stated.** South Korean officials argue that early rules let Korea shape international norms ([Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10653018)). Australia's minister said the country would monitor global responses and not follow a European-style Act ([Australian Government](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/transcripts/interview-sarah-ferguson-abc-730)).

**Observed.** Hoch and Mügge find that the EU weakened its rules in advance for competitiveness reasons, that reliance on vague principles and external standards bodies yields shallow harmonisation, and that EU influence is absent in the United States and China ([Hoch and Mügge](https://regulaite.eu/images/pdf/HochMugge2025.pdf)). Jurisdictions that copied the high-risk model now hold a template its originator has not yet applied; no fetched source tested this directly, so it is an inference. Pew's 2026 survey of twelve mostly middle-income countries found median trust to regulate AI of 43 per cent for China, 35 per cent for the United States and 34 per cent for the EU ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/09/17/do-people-trust-china-the-u-s-or-the-eu-to-regulate-ai/)). This is a different country set from Pew's 2025 survey and the two are not a time series.

**Documented case.** Colorado's 2024 Act was modelled on the EU risk-based approach and never took effect. The convergent United States template is now the frontier transparency law with thresholds of 10^26 operations and USD 500 million in revenue, shared by California, New York and Illinois and borrowed by a federal discussion draft ([Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/unpacking-the-great-american-artificial-intelligence-act-of-2026/)).

## What the stated and observed accounts show together

Across the cases in the notes, the official rationale for each major change is technical or principled (readiness, legal certainty, proportionality, a single national standard), and independent accounts point to who asked for the change and what leverage they held. The two accounts agree on EU implementation readiness and on Japan's adoption deficit. They diverge most on the EU delay, the United Kingdom's bill delay and institute remit, United States pre-emption, and the Pentagon's action against Anthropic. Stated drivers are drawn from a small vocabulary. "Security" is the stated driver most often used to cover other motives, in three directions: to narrow a safety remit (United Kingdom), to justify procurement retaliation (United States), and to justify content control (China).

Three regularities hold across the fifteen jurisdictions. Safeguards for citizens are sequenced after investment measures. The public sector is regulated more tightly than the private sector in light-touch Anglophone states, while in India, the Gulf states and much of Africa the state is the main promoter and is lightly constrained. Hard law appears where the regulated party is foreign and the protected party is sympathetic and organised, which explains firmness on online safety and copyright alongside retreat on general accountability in Australia and Canada.

The evidence base has limits that bear on how far these findings can be pushed. No peer-reviewed study in the notes attributes any 2025 or 2026 decision to specific causes, and no interview-based or archival work on decision-makers' reasoning was found. Primary official documents (the executive order texts aside) were mostly read through secondary summaries. Journal literature on regime complexity and diffusion was largely not retrieved. Lobbying evidence outside the United States and EU is positional and not documentary.

## Conclusion

A comparison built on statutes would rank the EU and South Korea as the most regulated jurisdictions and the United States federal government as the least. A comparison built on practice at 3 October 2026 reverses part of that order: the most forceful intervention in a commercial AI deployment to date was a United States export control letter, the most prescriptive operating content regime is China's filing and labelling system, and the EU's operative regime is confined to prohibitions, transparency duties and a small population of model providers. For an enterprise governance function, the obligations that reach deployers now are those in themes 5 to 7 and 10 (synthetic content, child safety, privacy and automated decisions, and public-sector rules), and the next fixed dates are 2 December 2026 (EU marking duty and new prohibition), 10 December 2026 (Australian automated decision disclosure), 1 January 2027 (Colorado's replacement law and New York's frontier law), the end of South Korea's minimum grace period in January 2027, and 2 December 2027 (EU high-risk rules). The EU high-risk date and the Colorado date have each already moved, Colorado's rules are still in rulemaking, and South Korea's grace period is a minimum, so the drivers in part two bear on whether these dates hold.

Two open tests will show whether the driver ranking of 2025 and 2026 persists. The first is whether the 2026 agent incidents produce binding frontier rules outside United States states; as at the reference date the responses in Washington, London and Ottawa were voluntary accords, spending and statements. The second is whether child protection continues to be the only protective driver that wins, or whether labour and household energy costs, which are now producing state law in the United States and adjudication in China, join it. The effect of the November 2026 United States midterm elections on both is unknown at this date.
