# AI policy and regulation in Australia, New Zealand, Canada, India, Brazil, the Gulf states and Africa (state as at October 2026)

## Australia: what is regulated, with which instruments, and what drove the shift from mandatory guardrails to a lighter approach?

### Takeaway
As at October 2026 Australia has no AI-specific statute and no economy-wide mandatory AI obligations. The government proposed ten mandatory guardrails for high-risk AI in September 2024, then set them aside in the National AI Plan of 2 December 2025 in favour of existing technology-neutral laws, voluntary guidance (the six-practice Guidance for AI Adoption), a small advisory AI Safety Institute, and investment attraction for data centres. Binding action is concentrated in three places: online safety (under-16 social media minimum age, age-restricted material codes that reach AI companions, a draft Digital Duty of Care), privacy (automated decision-making transparency from 10 December 2026 and a tranche 2 exposure draft), and the Commonwealth's own use of AI (mandatory DTA policy v2.0). The government's stated reason for the shift is that existing laws are fit for purpose and this is "the safest option"; independent accounts point to the Productivity Commission's productivity argument, business and tech-sector pressure, the pursuit of US data-centre investment, and (according to the former minister) fear of retaliation from the Trump administration.

### Cited Findings

#### Timeline of core instruments
- AI Ethics Principles: eight voluntary principles (benefit to individuals, society and environment; human rights, diversity and autonomy; inclusion and non-discrimination; privacy and security; safe and reliable operation; transparency and responsible disclosure; contestability; accountability). They remain the reference point for later instruments. The page retrieved does not state the year (the principles date from 2019; year not confirmed from the page in this session). (Source: [Department of Industry, Science and Resources](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/australias-artificial-intelligence-ethics-principles))
- 5 September 2024: then Industry Minister Ed Husic released two documents together. The Voluntary AI Safety Standard gave immediate guidance. The "Proposals paper for introducing mandatory guardrails for AI in high-risk settings" defined high-risk AI, set out ten mandatory guardrails, and offered three regulatory options: adapt existing frameworks, new framework legislation, or a dedicated Australian AI Act. (Source: [Scimex expert reaction, 5 Sept 2024](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/expert-reaction-government-proposes-mandatory-rules-for-high-risk-ai))
- The ten proposed guardrails would have required developers and deployers of high-risk AI to have risk-management plans, test systems before and after deployment, set up complaints mechanisms, share data after adverse incidents and open records to third-party assessment. (Source: [ABC News, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474))
- Expert reaction in September 2024 was already split. Dr Shaanan Cohney warned the guidance was "very high-level" and "likely to create compliance-driven culture rather than meaningfully improving practices". Professor Toby Walsh said "good regulation takes time". (Source: [Scimex, 5 Sept 2024](https://www.scimex.org/newsfeed/expert-reaction-government-proposes-mandatory-rules-for-high-risk-ai))
- 21 October 2025: the National AI Centre published the Guidance for AI Adoption. It replaced the Voluntary AI Safety Standard and "streamlined the 10 guardrails down to 6 key practices" ("AI6"). It comes in two parts: foundational guidance for organisations starting out and detailed implementation practices for governance and technical staff. It is voluntary. (Source: [Department of Industry, Science and Resources](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/guidance-for-ai-adoption))
- 25 November 2025: the government announced an Australian AI Safety Institute, to sit inside the Department of Industry, Science and Resources, start in early 2026, and "advise the government on identifying areas of regulatory reform and capability gaps". (Source: [The Mandarin, 25 Nov 2025](https://www.themandarin.com.au/303667-federal-answer-to-ai-safety-will-kick-off-by-early-2026/))
- 2 December 2025: Industry Minister Tim Ayres released the National AI Plan. Three goals: capture the economic opportunity, spread the benefits (skills, public services), keep Australians safe. The plan relies on "strong existing, largely technology-neutral legal frameworks" and "regulators' existing expertise", and commits only to "minor opportunities to clarify existing rules" on consumer protection, copyright and health care. The ten mandatory guardrails were not adopted. (Source: [ABC News, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474))
- Sources differ on wording for the fate of the guardrails. PS News reports they were put "on hold" rather than scrapped. ABC reports the government "drops" them. No source found shows a formal withdrawal instrument. (Source: [PS News](https://psnews.com.au/mandatory-ai-guardrails-put-on-hold-with-the-release-of-national-plan/170726/); [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474))
- The plan was reported as bundling more than A$460 million of existing and new AI commitments (research grants, graduate programs, SME adoption). It also flags expansion of the GovAI platform across the public service, generative AI trials in schools, and Indigenous data governance. (Source: [Gadens summary of the plan, lead only](https://www.gadens.com/legal-insights/australia-launches-ai-safety-institute-and-releases-national-ai-plan/))

#### Policy elements in comparable form (Australia)
- Legal form: no horizontal AI statute. Voluntary guidance for the private sector, mandatory administrative policy for Commonwealth agencies, and binding rules through sectoral and general laws (privacy, consumer, online safety, criminal, work health and safety). Ayres: "Australian law ... applies now". (Source: [Minister Ayres, ABC 7.30 transcript](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/transcripts/interview-sarah-ferguson-abc-730))
- Scope and risk classification: the 2024 proposal used a high-risk category with ten guardrails. The 2025 plan contains no statutory risk tiers. The Productivity Commission recommended that AI-specific rules be "a last resort" and only for use cases where existing frameworks "cannot be sufficiently adapted" and "technology-neutral regulations are not feasible". (Source: [Productivity Commission, Harnessing data and digital technology, inquiry report, 10 Dec 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf))
- Prohibited uses: none that are AI-specific at the horizontal level. Specific criminal prohibition exists for non-consensual sexual deepfakes: the Criminal Code Amendment (Deepfake Sexual Material) Bill 2024 passed the Senate in August 2024 and creates offences for sharing such material, with an aggravated offence where the sharer created it. (Source: [ACS Information Age, 21 Aug 2024](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2024/deepfakes-crackdown-passed-into-law.html))
- Developer versus deployer obligations: the Guidance for AI Adoption is aimed at organisations adopting AI (deployers), with a technical implementation tier. There are no binding developer duties. (Source: [DISR Guidance for AI Adoption](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/guidance-for-ai-adoption))
- Frontier model rules: none. The AI Safety Institute monitors, tests and shares information on "upstream" risks (model capabilities, training methods) and "downstream" harms. It is not a regulator and has no enforcement powers. Ayres: "right now it's going to advise and it's the responsibility of the portfolio agencies" to act. (Source: [ABC News, 29 May 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306); [Ayres 7.30 transcript](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/transcripts/interview-sarah-ferguson-abc-730))
- AI Safety Institute resourcing and status: A$29.9 million over four years. Dr Kate Conroy (philosopher, RAAF responsible AI lead, former chief scientist at Trusted Autonomous Systems) was announced as inaugural general manager on 29 May 2026 and started that month. At that date she was the only confirmed staff member. An MOU with the UK AI Security Institute was signed the same week. (Source: [ABC News, 29 May 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306))
- Transparency and content labelling: no general AI content labelling law. Transparency duties arise through the Privacy Act ADM provisions (below) and agency transparency requirements under the DTA policy.
- Data and privacy: from 10 December 2026, organisations covered by the Privacy Act must disclose in their privacy policies the use of automated decision-making that uses personal information. An exposure draft of the Privacy Amendment (Personal Data Protection) Bill 2026 was released on 31 August 2026 with comment to 18 September 2026. It proposes a "fair and reasonable" test, a broader definition of personal information ("relates to"), stricter consent, 72-hour breach reporting, a controller and processor distinction, and a right to erasure limited to large digital platforms. It does not remove the small business or employee records exemptions. (Source: [LK Law summary of the exposure draft, Sept 2026, lead only for bill detail](https://www.lk.law/2026/09/australias-privacy-act-reforms-inside-the-second-tranche/))
- The Productivity Commission took a different line on privacy: it recommended an overarching outcomes-based duty to handle personal information "in a manner that is fair and reasonable in the circumstances" and a move away from control-based rules. (Source: [Productivity Commission, 10 Dec 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf))
- Copyright and text and data mining: the Productivity Commission's interim report (August 2025) asked whether there was a case for a TDM exception. On 26 to 27 October 2025 Attorney-General Michelle Rowland ruled it out: "we will not be entertaining a text and data mining exception". Tech Council chair Scott Farquhar had argued in July 2025 that "fixing this one thing could unlock billions of dollars of foreign investment". ARIA's Annabelle Herd called the tech proposals "a bit of a try-on". (Source: [ABC News, 27 Oct 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-10-27/labor-rules-out-ai-training-copyright-exceptions/105935740))
- After ruling out TDM, the government asked the Copyright and AI Reference Group to examine three matters: licensing arrangements for AI training (options reported as statutory licence, collecting society scheme, or voluntary licences), how copyright applies to AI-generated material, and a small claims forum for lower-value infringement. (Source: [Attorney-General's media release, 26 Oct 2025, not retrievable; contents per MinterEllison, lead only](https://www.minterellison.com/articles/copyright-and-ai-consultation-on-licensing-for-ai-training); release URL: [ministers.ag.gov.au](https://ministers.ag.gov.au/media-centre/albanese-government-ensure-australia-prepared-future-copyright-challenges-emerging-ai-26-10-2025))
- The Productivity Commission's final report (10 December 2025) did not recommend a TDM exception. Recommendation 2.1 is a three-year monitoring program of licensing markets, creative incomes and overseas case law, with an independent review only if problems persist. (Source: [Productivity Commission, 10 Dec 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf))
- Ayres on copyright: "there'll be no weakening of copyright law". (Source: [Ayres 7.30 transcript](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/transcripts/interview-sarah-ferguson-abc-730))
- Liability: no AI-specific liability regime. The National AI Plan relies on existing consumer, privacy, discrimination and criminal law. (Source: [ABC News, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474))
- Enforcement bodies and penalties: existing regulators (OAIC, ACCC, eSafety, sector regulators). The largest AI-relevant penalties sit in online safety: up to A$49.5 million for breaches of the social media minimum age and for failing to comply with a direction under the industry codes, and a proposed A$109.2 million maximum under the draft Digital Duty of Care. (Source: [AFP via Malay Mail, 16 Jan 2026](https://www.malaymail.com/news/world/2026/01/16/tech-giants-block-47-million-accounts-under-australias-worldfirst-teen-social-media-ban/205608); [Medianama, Mar 2026](https://www.medianama.com/2026/03/223-australia-age-verification-ai-chatbot-porn-apps-law-2026/); [Prime Minister's media release, 8 Sept 2026](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/my-feed-my-way))
- Public-sector use: the DTA Policy for the responsible use of AI in government v2.0 took effect on 15 December 2025. It binds all non-corporate Commonwealth entities and excludes the defence portfolio and the national intelligence community. Requirements include agency AI adoption plans, accountable owners, internal registers of AI use cases, AI impact assessments before deployment, incident pathways, and mandatory foundational AI training for all APS staff. The first new requirement applied from 15 June 2026 and the rest from December 2026. (Source: [Digital Transformation Agency](https://www.dta.gov.au/articles/ai-policy-update-strengthening-responsible-use-across-government))
- Robodebt response: the Royal Commission (2023) made 57 recommendations and the government accepted or agreed in principle to 56. Recommendation 17.1 called for a consistent legislative framework for automated decision-making in government. The Attorney-General's Department consulted from 13 November 2024 to 15 January 2025 and published a summary (page updated 22 August 2025). No bill has followed. (Source: [Attorney-General's Department consultation hub](https://consultations.ag.gov.au/integrity/adm/); [Anna Huggins, AUSPUBLAW, 24 Nov 2025](https://www.auspublaw.org/blog/2025/11/regulating-automated-government-decision-making-an-australian-perspective-combatting-the-code-book-forum))
- Online safety, children and deepfakes: the under-16 social media minimum age took effect on 10 December 2025. By mid-January 2026 platforms had restricted about 4.7 million accounts (Instagram 331,000, Facebook 173,000, Threads 40,000 among them). (Source: [AFP via Malay Mail, 16 Jan 2026](https://www.malaymail.com/news/world/2026/01/16/tech-giants-block-47-million-accounts-under-australias-worldfirst-teen-social-media-ban/205608))
- Age-restricted material codes registered by eSafety began on 27 December 2025 (search, hosting, carriers) and 9 March 2026 (app stores, devices, social media, gaming, messaging). Generative AI services able to produce sexually explicit material, high-impact violence or self-harm content must confirm users are 18 or over. (Source: [Medianama, Mar 2026](https://www.medianama.com/2026/03/223-australia-age-verification-ai-chatbot-porn-apps-law-2026/))
- An eSafety report in March 2026 found 79 per cent of Australian children aged 10 to 17 had used AI companions or assistants, about 200,000 (8 per cent) used AI companions specifically, and four companion services examined (Character.AI, Nomi, Chai, Chub AI) lacked meaningful age assurance and failed to redirect self-harm prompts. (Source: [Alena Radina, UTS Centre for Media Transition, Mar 2026](https://www.uts.edu.au/news/2026/03/when-ai-companionship-turns-dark))
- eSafety has also taken enforcement action against "nudify" services over child protection failures. (Source: [eSafety media release, not retrievable; title only](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/esafety-takes-action-against-another-major-nudify-service-for-failing-to-protect-australian-children))
- 8 September 2026: the government released an exposure draft of Digital Duty of Care legislation. It covers social media, online games, apps, messaging and AI chatbots. It requires minimum safety standards, documented risk mitigation, protection of under-18s from addictive design and listed harms, an algorithm choice ("My Feed, My Way") for users 16 and over, and removal of nudify apps and sites on eSafety notice. Maximum penalty A$109.2 million. Introduction to Parliament is planned for 2026. (Source: [Prime Minister's media release, 8 Sept 2026](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/my-feed-my-way))
- Standards: the Guidance for AI Adoption states alignment with the AI Ethics Principles and international standards. No mandatory conformity assessment exists. (Source: [DISR](https://www.industry.gov.au/publications/guidance-for-ai-adoption))
- Sandboxes and innovation support: none specific to AI found. Innovation support is delivered through the National AI Centre and grant programs bundled in the plan (figure of A$460 million per lead source above).
- Compute, data centres and industrial policy: the plan presents Australia as a leading data-centre destination. ABC reported A$10 billion of data-centre investment in the prior year and a forecast that data centres could reach 6 per cent of electricity demand by 2030. (Source: [ABC News, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474))
- 23 to 24 March 2026: Ministers Ayres, Bowen and Assistant Minister Charlton released "Expectations for data centres and AI infrastructure": prioritise the national interest, support the energy transition, use water sustainably, invest in Australian skills and jobs, strengthen research and local capability. The expectations are non-binding. The government "will prioritise proposals most closely aligned". Trade press describes the lever as approval speed. (Source: [Ministerial media release](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/charlton/media-releases/australian-approach-ai-expectations-data-centres-deliver-australians); [Data Center Knowledge, Mar 2026](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/regulations/australia-puts-ai-data-centers-on-notice-with-new-approval-rules))
- An MOU with Anthropic was announced around 31 March 2026 during Dario Amodei's visit. Anthropic agreed to align Australian operations with the data-centre expectations, work with the AI Safety Institute on evaluations, share Economic Index usage data, and fund A$3 million of research. Ayres: "This MOU sends a clear signal that we are open for business". (Source: [Ministerial media release](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/media-releases/new-agreement-ai-collaboration-anthropic); [SmartCompany, 31 Mar 2026](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/artificial-intelligence/anthropic-ai-deal-australia-usage-data/))
- Microsoft announced a A$25 billion Australian investment in May 2026. Industry sources told ABC that Anthropic's ambitions implied 5 gigawatts by 2030 and 20 gigawatts in the long term. Alan Finkel said 20 gigawatts "would be extremely difficult" within five years. (Source: [ABC Four Corners report, 8 June 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-08/federal-government-ai-policy-retreat-data-centres-four-corners/106753596))
- Skills and workforce: the plan flags "future action" on workplace surveillance, bias in rostering and AI discrimination. ACTU Assistant Secretary Joseph Mitchell welcomed "commitments to ensuring our workplace laws are fit for purpose in the AI age". (Source: [ABC News, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474); [PS News](https://psnews.com.au/mandatory-ai-guardrails-put-on-hold-with-the-release-of-national-plan/170726/))
- International alignment: Ayres said Australia would monitor global responses rather than follow a European-style Act. The AI Safety Institute joined the international network through the UK MOU. (Source: [Ayres 7.30 transcript](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/transcripts/interview-sarah-ferguson-abc-730); [ABC News, 29 May 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306))

#### State-level measures
- New South Wales: Circular DCS-2026-02 (issued 30 July 2026, updated 1 September 2026) makes the NSW AI Operational Policy mandatory for all NSW agencies. Agencies must follow the AI Ethics Principles, appoint an accountable official, register every AI use case on the AI Assessment Framework platform, apply the AI Assessment Framework where triggered, refer high-risk and critical-risk projects to the AI Review Committee within five business days, train staff, and attest annually (first attestation due 31 October 2027). The Operational Policy replaces the earlier NSW AI Ethics Policy. The framework was formerly known as the NSW AI Assurance Framework. (Source: [NSW Government circular DCS-2026-02](https://arp.nsw.gov.au/dcs-2026-02-use-of-artificial-intelligence-by-nsw-government-agencies))
- New South Wales also legislated the Work Health and Safety Amendment (Digital Work Systems) Act 2026, described as Australia's first express statutory duty on digital work systems. Section 21A requires duty holders to assess whether systems that allocate work create unreasonable workloads, metrics, surveillance or discriminatory decisions. Commencement was awaiting proclamation at 3 June 2026. (Source: [Mondaq reproduction of NSW Government Bulletin, 3 June 2026, lead only](https://www.mondaq.com/australia/employee-rights-labour-relations/1796214/nsw-government-bulletin-managing-the-use-of-digital-work-systems-in-government-agencies))
- South Australia appointed a Minister for Artificial Intelligence and Digital Economy in March 2026. (Source: [Wikipedia, Regulation of artificial intelligence, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_artificial_intelligence))

#### Official account of the shift
- The plan's stated aim: "capturing the economic opportunities of AI, sharing the benefits broadly, and keeping Australians safe" (Ayres). (Source: [ABC News, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474))
- Ayres on not pursuing an AI Act: "I think this is the safest option for Australia". He said existing law already applies across communications, criminal and financial contexts and that a single AI law would duplicate regulator responsibilities. (Source: [Ayres 7.30 transcript](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/transcripts/interview-sarah-ferguson-abc-730))
- Treasurer Jim Chalmers: regulate "as much as necessary but as little as possible" and treat AI as "an enabler, not an enemy". (Source: [ABC News, 5 Aug 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-05/productivity-commission-ai-laws-roundtable/105609304))
- Productivity Commission: Recommendation 1.1 asks the government to finish and publish gap analyses of existing law. Recommendation 1.2 says to pause "whole-of-economy regulation such as the EU AI Act and the Australian Government's previous proposal to mandate guardrails for AI in high-risk settings". It estimates AI could add about A$116 billion to GDP over a decade (multifactor productivity gains above 2.3 per cent, about 4.3 per cent labour productivity growth over the decade), with wide uncertainty. Commissioner Stephen King: economy-wide AI rules "could see Australia fall behind the curve". (Source: [Productivity Commission, 10 Dec 2025](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf); [ABC News, 5 Aug 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-05/productivity-commission-ai-laws-roundtable/105609304))

#### Observed practice and independent accounts of the drivers
- Former minister Ed Husic told ABC Four Corners (8 June 2026): "We put AI regulation in a 'too hard' basket. We blinked in the face of Donald Trump." He attributed the retreat after the May 2025 election to fear of US retaliation of the kind directed at Australia's social media laws and to criticism from Elon Musk. Ayres denied that Trump influenced the decision and pointed to the social media age restrictions as proof of willingness to regulate. (Source: [ABC News, 8 June 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-08/federal-government-ai-policy-retreat-data-centres-four-corners/106753596))
- Sequence matters. The Productivity Commission's call to pause the guardrails came on 5 August 2025, two weeks before the Treasurer's economic reform roundtable where AI was central. Industry group DIGI argued for building on existing regulation. The Business Council welcomed the plan as seizing a "once-in-a-generation economic opportunity". (Source: [ABC News, 5 Aug 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-05/productivity-commission-ai-laws-roundtable/105609304); [ABC News, 2 Dec 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474); [Montreal AI Ethics Institute](https://montrealethics.ai/ai-policy-corner-from-proposed-mandatory-guardrails-to-the-national-ai-plan-ai-governance-in-australia/))
- Unions pushed the other way. In August 2025 they argued for an AI Act and Husic publicly backed one on 3 August 2025. The plan gave unions workplace-law commitments but not an Act. The Coalition's Ted O'Brien called the plan "written by unions, for unions". Greens Senator David Shoebridge called it a "toothless tiger" choosing "corporate profits over community rights". (Source: [ABC News, 5 Aug 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-08-05/productivity-commission-ai-laws-roundtable/105609304); [PS News](https://psnews.com.au/mandatory-ai-guardrails-put-on-hold-with-the-release-of-national-plan/170726/))
- Public opinion runs against the lighter approach: ABC cites 81 per cent support for stronger rules and 68 per cent worried about losing control over AI decisions. (Source: [ABC News, 29 May 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306))
- Capacity gap in the safety function: Toby Walsh said "the big challenge for the AI Safety Institute is being able to provide meaningful support and advice given the level of funding it's got". A separate expert advisory body was scrapped in February 2026 after A$188,000 and 15 months were spent selecting 12 experts. (Source: [ABC News, 29 May 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-05-29/kate-conroy-leading-ai-safety-institute/106736306))
- Automated decision-making in government keeps failing while the legislative response waits. In August 2025 the Commonwealth Ombudsman found the employment department had unlawfully cancelled payments for 964 jobseekers through a flawed automated system under the Targeted Compliance Framework. Robodebt remediation has exceeded A$2.4 billion, including a A$475 million settlement in September 2025. No ADM framework law exists. (Source: [Anna Huggins, AUSPUBLAW, 24 Nov 2025](https://www.auspublaw.org/blog/2025/11/regulating-automated-government-decision-making-an-australian-perspective-combatting-the-code-book-forum))
- Professor Ed Santow described privacy laws as "dangerously out of date" in the Four Corners report, which matters because the plan leans on privacy law as the main safeguard. (Source: [ABC News, 8 June 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-08/federal-government-ai-policy-retreat-data-centres-four-corners/106753596))
- Where government has regulated firmly, it has done so on child safety and creators' rights, both of which target foreign platforms and have strong domestic constituencies. Communications Minister Anika Wells framed the Digital Duty of Care as a "reckoning" for US tech companies: "Tech companies can still move fast, but they will no longer be allowed to break things." (Source: [Macquarie University Lighthouse, Sept 2026](https://lighthouse.mq.edu.au/article/2026/september-2026/australians-opt-out-algorithms-digital-duty-of-care-experts); [ACS Information Age, Sept 2026](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2026/-digital-duty-of-care--to-let-aussies-opt-out-of-social-media-al.html))
- Critique of the duty of care draft: Dr Mark Williams said the fines are "far too small for companies whose annual revenues dwarf national budgets". (Source: [Macquarie University Lighthouse, Sept 2026](https://lighthouse.mq.edu.au/article/2026/september-2026/australians-opt-out-algorithms-digital-duty-of-care-experts))
- Unconfirmed: an opinion piece (Sue Barrett, 24 July 2026) states that on 15 July 2026 the government announced "mandatory standards for data centres" covering energy, water, connection costs and approval speed, with legislation promised for 2027 and no bill drafted. No primary or news source for a 15 July 2026 announcement was retrieved. Treat as a lead. (Source: [Pearls and Irritations, 24 July 2026, opinion](https://johnmenadue.com/post/2026/07/australias-new-ai-rules-protect-data-centres-not-citizens/))

### Inferences
- The Australian shift is best described as a change of object. Economy-wide obligations on AI developers and deployers were dropped. Regulation continued where the target is a foreign consumer platform and the protected group is children or creators. Infrastructure "expectations" replaced guardrails as the main new AI-labelled instrument in 2026.
- Four drivers are visible in the record, with differing evidence: (a) a productivity frame supplied by the Productivity Commission and adopted by Treasury (strong documentary evidence), (b) industry lobbying aligned with that frame (documented positions of DIGI, BCA and the Tech Council), (c) competition for hyperscaler and frontier-lab investment (MOU with Anthropic, Microsoft A$25 billion, "open for business" language), and (d) concern about the Trump administration's response (asserted by the former minister, denied by the current one).
- Copyright is the counter-example to a simple "industry capture" reading. The government rejected the tech sector's main ask (TDM) despite the same productivity framing, which suggests the organised creative sector and media carry more weight than diffuse consumer interests in AI accountability.
- The official claim that existing laws are fit for purpose sits awkwardly with three facts: the gap analyses the Productivity Commission asked for had not been published when the plan was released, the Privacy Act tranche 2 was still only an exposure draft in September 2026, and the ADM framework promised after Robodebt has not been legislated.
- The public sector is held to a stricter standard than the private sector (mandatory DTA policy, NSW mandatory assessment), which is a common pattern among light-touch jurisdictions (compare Canada and New Zealand).

### Gaps
- The full text of the National AI Plan (nine action areas, exact funding lines, wording on guardrails) could not be retrieved from industry.gov.au. Quotes above come from ABC and secondary summaries.
- The Attorney-General's 26 October 2025 media release could not be read directly. The CAIRG outcome and any 2026 decision on a licensing scheme were not found.
- No FOI documents or lobbying records were retrieved that directly evidence tech-sector influence on the guardrails decision. The Trump-pressure claim rests on Husic's statement.
- No analysis from HTI (UTS), the Gradient Institute or the ANU Tech Policy Design Centre specific to the December 2025 plan was retrieved. The Conversation's analysis of who benefits from the plan was blocked.
- OAIC guidance on AI and privacy, and any OAIC enforcement involving AI, were not retrieved.
- Status of the Digital Duty of Care bill after 8 September 2026 and the fate of the 15 July 2026 data-centre standards claim need checking.
- Whether the under-16 ban has reduced harm, as distinct from account counts, is not established by the sources retrieved.
- Other state measures (Victoria, Queensland, Western Australia) were not researched.

## Canada: the failure of AIDA, the Voluntary Code, the AI minister, the new strategy and legislation, and the Directive on Automated Decision-Making

### Takeaway
Canada's attempt at a horizontal AI statute (AIDA, part of Bill C-27) died on 6 January 2025 and the government has said it will not revive it. Under AI Minister Evan Solomon the approach is "light, tight, right": a national strategy ("AI for All", 4 June 2026) built on adoption, sovereign compute and trust, with regulation delivered through privacy reform (Bill C-36), online safety legislation (Bill C-34) and targeted measures, while the federal public service remains bound by the mandatory Directive on Automated Decision-Making. Independent analysts describe an industry-weighted process and a gap between sovereignty rhetoric and dependence on US technology.

### Cited Findings

#### AIDA and why it failed
- AIDA was tabled in June 2022 within Bill C-27, studied by the INDU committee from April 2023, amended by government proposals in November 2023, stalled through 2024, and died when Parliament was prorogued on 6 January 2025 after Prime Minister Trudeau's resignation. (Source: [Schwartz Reisman Institute, 11 Feb 2025](https://srinstitute.utoronto.ca/news/whats-next-for-aida); [Montreal AI Ethics Institute](https://montrealethics.ai/the-death-of-canadas-artificial-intelligence-and-data-act-what-happened-and-whats-next-for-ai-regulation-in-canada/))
- Content: obligations on developers and operators of "high-impact" systems (risk assessment and mitigation, record keeping, disclosure), monetary penalties and criminal offences, and a new AI and Data Commissioner. (Source: [Montreal AI Ethics Institute](https://montrealethics.ai/the-death-of-canadas-artificial-intelligence-and-data-act-what-happened-and-whats-next-for-ai-regulation-in-canada/))
- Peer-reviewed analysis (Brandusescu and Sieber, McGill, Data and Policy, 2025): no public consultation before tabling; afterwards ISED held more than 300 invite-only meetings, of which nine included civil society; ISED had a conflicting dual mandate to promote and to regulate AI; the bill was "explicitly tied to economic development"; it left out the public sector, workers' rights and creators. The authors recommend an independent regulator separate from the promotion mandate. (Source: [Data and Policy, Cambridge University Press, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/data-and-policy/article/missed-opportunities-in-ai-regulation-lessons-from-canadas-ai-and-data-act/5178DE82B270CD41FA3B7ECFC94BF810))

#### Voluntary Code of Conduct
- The Voluntary Code of Conduct on the Responsible Development and Management of Advanced Generative AI Systems was launched in September 2023. It sets six outcomes: accountability, safety, fairness and equity, transparency, human oversight and monitoring, validity and robustness. It distinguishes developers from managers of systems. The ISED page lists 47 signatory organisations (date of last addition not stated). (Source: [ISED Canada](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/en/voluntary-code-conduct-responsible-development-and-management-advanced-generative-ai-systems))

#### The AI minister, the strategy and new legislation
- Evan Solomon is Canada's first Minister of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Innovation. He has said he would not revive Bill C-27 as it was, describes the approach as "light, tight, right", and has said rules must not "choke off" innovators. A 30-day task force "sprint" and an online consultation with about 6,500 responses fed the strategy. (Source: [BetaKit, 2025](https://betakit.com/evan-solomon-teases-new-ai-laws-as-experts-warn-canada-is-behind-international-peers/))
- The strategy was delayed from late 2025 into 2026. (Source: [Global News, 2025](https://globalnews.ca/news/11558465/canada-artificial-intelligence-strategy-update-delay-minister/))
- 4 June 2026: "AI for All: Canada's National Artificial Intelligence Strategy" was released. Reported commitments: up to 250,000 jobs through AI adoption by 2031; a public supercomputer by 2031; 850 megawatts of compute capacity by 2030 scaling to 2.3 gigawatts; C$700 million more for the Compute Access Fund on top of more than C$2 billion already committed; a C$500 million Canadian Tech Growth Fund; C$500 million for the Regional AI Initiative; C$200 million for a health AI mission; C$50 million to expand the Canadian AI Safety Institute; C$30 million for CanCode. It contains no plan to reintroduce standalone AI legislation comparable to AIDA. (Source: [Legal 500 country commentary, June 2026, lead only; primary page not retrievable](https://www.legal500.com/developments/thought-leadership/canada-unveils-national-ai-strategy-key-commitments-regulatory-gaps-and-whats-missing/); primary URL: [ISED, AI for All](https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/en/canadas-national-artificial-intelligence-strategy-ai-all))
- Solomon's own description of the strategy (2 June 2026): "lots on trust, lots on empowering workers, lots on building Canada", with trust "absolutely vital", and "comprehensive legislation" to protect children, privacy and data to follow. (Source: [Canadian Press via CP24, 2 June 2026](https://www.cp24.com/news/canada/2026/06/02/federal-governments-new-ai-strategy-will-emphasize-trust-minister-says/))
- Bill C-36, the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, was introduced in June 2026 (first reading text on parl.ca). It is the first major private-sector privacy overhaul in over 25 years. AI-relevant provisions reported: personal information includes inferred information; transparency and explanation for automated decisions; a framework for de-identified data; information about under-18s treated as sensitive. (Source: [Al Jazeera, 10 July 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/10/canadas-bill-c-36-tackles-ai-privacy-is-it-enough); [Parliament of Canada, Bill C-36 first reading](https://www.parl.ca/DocumentViewer/en/45-1/bill/C-36/first-reading))
- Online safety: Bill C-34 was before the Standing Committee on Public Safety and National Security in mid-2026 (per the Legal 500 lead). By late September 2026 Canadian Press listed a "Safe Social Media Act", deepfake and non-consensual imagery legislation, rules on AI chatbots and children, and a completed consultation on AI transparency as introduced or under way. Exact bill status is not confirmed. (Source: [Canadian Press via CP24, 27 Sept 2026](https://www.cp24.com/news/canada/2026/09/27/ai-minister-says-system-deployment-needs-regulation-but-no-details-yet/))
- 27 September 2026: Solomon said AI system deployment needs regulation but gave no details: "I'm not going to say 'yes, this is what we're doing'". He said "safety is our number one concern", cited reports of AI agents doing "things that they weren't programmed to do", and said he had discussed with G7 and G20 counterparts slowing model releases, incident reporting and an international technology stability board. Canada and Germany committed C$300 million to Yoshua Bengio's LawZero. (Source: [Canadian Press via CP24, 27 Sept 2026](https://www.cp24.com/news/canada/2026/09/27/ai-minister-says-system-deployment-needs-regulation-but-no-details-yet/))
- The Canadian AI Safety Institute was launched in November 2024 as part of a C$2.4 billion federal AI package. (Source: [Schwartz Reisman Institute](https://srinstitute.utoronto.ca/news/whats-next-for-aida); [Wikipedia, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_artificial_intelligence))

#### Public-sector use: Directive on Automated Decision-Making
- The Treasury Board Directive is mandatory for federal institutions. Amendments in effect from 25 April 2023 extended scope to internal services (hiring, performance management, security screening), require the Algorithmic Impact Assessment to be published before launch, require peer review findings to be published, and strengthen bias testing, data governance and explanation. Compliance dates were 25 October 2023 for new systems and 25 April 2024 for existing ones. Reviews are now every two years. (Source: [Government of Canada, Treasury Board Secretariat](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/policies-standards/policy-service-digital-announcements/amendments-directive-automated-decision-making.html))
- Provincial layer: Ontario's Bill 194 imposes obligations on public sector entities to publish information about AI use and adopt accountability frameworks. (Source: [Schwartz Reisman Institute](https://srinstitute.utoronto.ca/news/whats-next-for-aida))

#### Official drivers
- Trust, worker empowerment, sovereignty and "building Canada", with productivity and affordability as the economic case. (Source: [Canadian Press via CP24, 2 June 2026](https://www.cp24.com/news/canada/2026/06/02/federal-governments-new-ai-strategy-will-emphasize-trust-minister-says/); [The Walrus, 15 Jan 2026](https://thewalrus.ca/evan-solomon-ai/))

#### Practice and independent accounts
- Kate Lunau (The Walrus, 15 January 2026): more than 120 civil society groups signed an October 2025 letter criticising the "prioritization of business and economic interests" and the absence of human rights and civil liberties groups on the task force. Ron Deibert of the Citizen Lab declined to take part. 85 per cent of Canadians want stronger regulation and 31 per cent trust AI. A Dais study found "no relationship between AI adoption and short-term productivity improvements" in Canadian firms surveyed in 2020 to 2021. Budget 2025 put C$925 million over five years into sovereign AI infrastructure. The article notes continued dependence on US platforms despite "sovereign cloud" language. (Source: [The Walrus, 15 Jan 2026](https://thewalrus.ca/evan-solomon-ai/))
- Privacy Commissioner Philippe Dufresne: "We need to make sure we have strong enforcement." Yoshua Bengio: "We don't just need regulation, we need international treaties." (Source: [BetaKit, 2025](https://betakit.com/evan-solomon-teases-new-ai-laws-as-experts-warn-canada-is-behind-international-peers/))
- On Bill C-36, Ignacio Cofone (Oxford) said it "will help, modestly, and less than the framing suggests" because harms arise from inference and decision-making, not only collection. (Source: [Al Jazeera, 10 July 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/10/canadas-bill-c-36-tackles-ai-privacy-is-it-enough))
- Gaps identified in the strategy by a legal commentary: no timelines or KPIs, nothing on worker displacement, policing use, environmental standards for data centres, or treatment of large AI companies. (Source: [Legal 500, June 2026, lead only](https://www.legal500.com/developments/thought-leadership/canada-unveils-national-ai-strategy-key-commitments-regulatory-gaps-and-whats-missing/))

### Inferences
- Policy elements in comparable form: legal form is strategy plus privacy and online-safety bills plus a voluntary code; no risk tiers in force (AIDA's "high-impact" category lapsed); developer and manager roles exist only in the voluntary code; frontier model oversight is through the AI Safety Institute and international diplomacy; public-sector use is the only area with mandatory algorithmic impact assessment; compute is a major spending line; children's safety and deepfakes are the legislative priorities.
- Canada and Australia followed the same arc within about a year of each other: a proposed horizontal law with a high-risk category, collapse of that proposal, then a strategy centred on adoption and compute, with binding action redirected to privacy and online harms. Canada's collapse was procedural (prorogation) on top of substantive opposition. Australia's was a policy choice.
- "Sovereignty" is a stronger stated driver in Canada than in Australia, linked to the trade conflict with the United States, yet practice still relies on US platforms. Canada also retains a louder frontier-safety voice (Bengio, LawZero funding, Solomon's September 2026 remarks) than Australia.

### Gaps
- The primary text of "AI for All" was not retrievable. Funding figures rest on a legal commentary and should be checked against the ISED page.
- Exact status, numbers and titles of the online safety and deepfake bills (C-34, "Safe Social Media Act") were not confirmed against parl.ca.
- No audit evidence was retrieved on compliance with the Directive on Automated Decision-Making (for example, how many systems have published AIAs).
- Whether additional firms signed the Voluntary Code after 2024, and whether it has any monitoring, is unknown.

## India: IndiaAI Mission, AI governance guidelines, deepfake labelling rules, the DPDP Act and the February 2026 AI Impact Summit

### Takeaway
India has chosen "Innovation over Restraint": the India AI Governance Guidelines reject a standalone AI law in favour of existing statutes, targeted amendments and new coordinating bodies, while the IndiaAI Mission funds compute, datasets and models. The binding exception is synthetic media: amended IT Rules requiring labelling of AI-generated content took effect on 20 February 2026. The AI Impact Summit (16 to 21 February 2026) reframed the summit series from safety to "impact" and drew more than US$200 billion of investment pledges, with the United States and China both signing the declaration.

### Cited Findings
- IndiaAI Mission: approved on 7 March 2024 with Rs 10,371.92 crore (about US$1.1 billion) over 2024 to 2029 across seven pillars. Compute receives Rs 4,563.36 crore. "Safe and Trusted AI" receives Rs 20.46 crore, about 0.2 per cent of the total. (Source: [Wikipedia, Artificial intelligence in India, lead only; figures match the Cabinet announcement but were not re-checked at PIB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_in_India))
- Compute and assets reported by government in February 2026: more than 38,000 GPUs onboarded against a target of 100,000; AIKosh with 9,500 datasets and 273 models; 570 AI Data Labs. A further 20,000 GPUs were announced at the summit. (Source: [Press Information Bureau, Feb 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2); [PIB summit outcomes](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2234343&reg=3&lang=1))
- India AI Governance Guidelines (MeitY; issued November 2025 per the assignment brief, described in a PIB release dated 15 February 2026): seven "sutras" (Trust is the Foundation; People First; Innovation over Restraint; Fairness and Equity; Accountability; Understandable by Design; Safety, Resilience and Sustainability) and six pillars (infrastructure, capacity building, policy and regulation, risk mitigation, accountability, institutions). (Source: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2))
- Legal form under the guidelines: build on existing laws, with "targeted legislative amendments" on classification, liability, data protection and copyright. Regulators are told to avoid "compliance-heavy requirements" unless necessary. New law is reserved for emerging risks in the long term. (Source: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2))
- Institutions: an AI Governance Group to coordinate policy, a Technology and Policy Expert Committee, and an AI Safety Institute for research, standards and technical guidance. (Source: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2))
- Risk approach: India-specific risk frameworks based on empirical evidence of harm, with attention to children and women. Short-term actions include a regulatory gap analysis, a master circular and groundwork for an AI incidents database. Medium-term actions include common standards (content authentication, fairness, cybersecurity), law amendments and pilot sandboxes in high-risk domains. (Source: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2))
- Deepfake and synthetic content rules: amended Information Technology Rules took effect on 20 February 2026. They impose intermediary obligations for AI and synthetically generated content, including labelling with embedded metadata. (Source: [Wikipedia, Regulation of artificial intelligence, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_artificial_intelligence))
- Data protection: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act received assent on 11 August 2023. Commencement is phased: 13 November 2025 (Data Protection Board and core institutional sections), 13 November 2026 (a second set of provisions), 13 May 2027 (remaining provisions). The Board is an adjudicator, not a rule-making regulator. The Act contains broad exemptions for state functions. Justice B.N. Srikrishna warned the law's state access powers risk an "Orwellian State". (Source: [Wikipedia, DPDP Act, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Personal_Data_Protection_Act,_2023))
- AI Impact Summit, New Delhi, 16 to 21 February 2026: more than 100 countries and 20 international organisations took part. Government outputs: AI Impact Summit Declaration (92 countries and organisations per PIB); New Delhi Frontier AI Impact Commitments signed by 13 frontier developers; voluntary guiding principles on resilient AI and on reskilling; a Charter for Democratic Diffusion of AI (22 signatories); Trusted AI Commons; AI governance guidance notes (22 countries). (Source: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2234343&reg=3&lang=1))
- Signatory counts conflict: PIB says 92, ORF says 91 countries and organisations (declaration text published 30 March 2026), ISAS says about 80 nations. All agree it is non-binding. (Source: [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2234343&reg=3&lang=1); [Observer Research Foundation](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/from-dialogue-to-deployment-strategic-outcomes-of-the-india-ai-impact-summit-2026); [ISAS, National University of Singapore, 25 Feb 2026](https://www.isas.nus.edu.sg/papers/india-ai-impact-summit-2026-a-technological-turning-point))
- The United States and China both signed, in contrast with the US refusal to sign the Paris declaration in 2025. Earlier counts: Bletchley 29, Seoul 11, Paris 60. (Source: [Observer Research Foundation](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/from-dialogue-to-deployment-strategic-outcomes-of-the-india-ai-impact-summit-2026))
- Investment pledges reported by government: more than US$200 billion in total, including Reliance US$110 billion over seven years, Adani US$100 billion by 2035, Google US$15 billion for an AI hub in Visakhapatnam, Lightspeed US$10 billion and General Catalyst US$5 billion. (Source: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2234343&reg=3&lang=1))
- The 13 frontier firms committed to multilingual evaluation and workforce impact analysis. (Source: [Observer Research Foundation](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/from-dialogue-to-deployment-strategic-outcomes-of-the-india-ai-impact-summit-2026))
- Next summit: Switzerland (Geneva) in 2027, with the UAE a possible host in 2028. (Source: [Wikipedia, AI Impact Summit, citing Hindustan Times, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Impact_Summit))

#### Official drivers
- AI as a development tool for agriculture, health, education and public service delivery, with "inclusivity, responsibility and sovereignty". India presented itself as a bridge between advanced and developing economies. (Source: [ISAS, 25 Feb 2026](https://www.isas.nus.edu.sg/papers/india-ai-impact-summit-2026-a-technological-turning-point))
- ORF describes India as "a middle voice that could create consensus" and the shift from "risk" to "impact" framing as what allowed broad sign-on. (Source: [Observer Research Foundation](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/from-dialogue-to-deployment-strategic-outcomes-of-the-india-ai-impact-summit-2026))

#### Practice and independent accounts
- Safety was subordinate to development and investment at the summit. ISAS: safety discussions "appeared subordinate to development narratives". TechPolicy.Press (as cited by Wikipedia) reported the summit gave "multinational corporations parity with sovereign governments" with "no equivalent high-level platform for civil society". (Source: [ISAS](https://www.isas.nus.edu.sg/papers/india-ai-impact-summit-2026-a-technological-turning-point); [Wikipedia, AI Impact Summit, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Impact_Summit))
- Implementation doubts: ISAS notes "infrastructure expansion must be matched by regulatory clarity" and skills must reach beyond the metropolitan centres. Bloomberg reported logistics failures. A university displayed a Chinese-made robot as indigenous. (Source: [ISAS](https://www.isas.nus.edu.sg/papers/india-ai-impact-summit-2026-a-technological-turning-point); [Wikipedia, AI Impact Summit, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Impact_Summit))
- The budget split in the IndiaAI Mission (compute Rs 4,563 crore against safety Rs 20 crore) shows where priority lies in spending terms. (Source: [Wikipedia, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_in_India))

### Inferences
- Policy elements in comparable form: legal form is guidelines plus delegated legislation on intermediaries plus a data protection statute in phased commencement; no risk tiers yet (framework promised); no prohibited uses; obligations fall on intermediaries and platforms more than on model developers; frontier developers are engaged through voluntary commitments; content labelling is the one hard rule; liability and copyright are flagged for amendment; enforcement is through MeitY's intermediary regime and the Data Protection Board; compute and sovereign models are central.
- India regulates speech-related harms of AI (deepfakes, platform content) through executive rule-making, which is fast and extends state leverage over platforms, while leaving economic uses of AI unregulated. That combination fits a driver mix of development goals, investment attraction and state control over the information environment.
- India's summit diplomacy shows middle-power positioning in practice: it gained US and Chinese signatures by removing safety commitments that either would resist.

### Gaps
- Details of the IT Rules amendment (draft in October 2025, notification date, label specification, the reported three-hour takedown window) could not be confirmed from MeitY or PIB in this session. Only the 20 February 2026 effective date and the metadata labelling requirement are sourced, and that from Wikipedia. Treat the three-hour takedown as unconfirmed.
- The original release date of the AI Governance Guidelines (5 November 2025 in most accounts) was not confirmed from a primary page. The PIB page retrieved is dated February 2026.
- DPDP Rules content, AI-relevant exemptions (publicly available data, research) and the content of the 13 November 2026 tranche need a primary check.
- No Reuters or Rest of World reporting on the summit or on enforcement practice was retrieved. Copyright and AI (the DPIIT committee process) was not researched.
- Whether the investment pledges are new money or restated plans is not established.

## Brazil: Bill 2338/2023 in the Chamber of Deputies, ANPD's role and the LGPD

### Takeaway
Brazil has the most EU-like bill in this group, but it is not law. PL 2338/2023 passed the Senate on 10 December 2024 and has sat in a Chamber of Deputies special committee since 2025. As at 2 September 2026 its status on the Chamber portal was still "awaiting the rapporteur's opinion", with no plenary vote. Until it passes, AI is governed mainly through the LGPD and its regulator, the ANPD, which the bill would make coordinator of a national AI governance system.

### Cited Findings
- Senate approval: 10 December 2024, rapporteur Senator Eduardo Gomes (PL-TO). The bill originated with Senator Rodrigo Pacheco. (Source: [Agência Senado, 10 Dec 2024](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara); [DIAP](https://www.diap.org.br/index.php/noticias/noticias/92249-pl-2338-23-votacao-do-projeto-sobre-inteligencia-artificial-esta-prevista-apenas-para-dezembro))
- Senate text, comparable elements. Risk classification: excessive-risk systems are prohibited (including autonomous weapons and systems exploiting vulnerable people). High-risk uses include autonomous vehicles, education and employment decisions, eligibility for public services, biometric emotion recognition, crime analysis and prediction, and medical diagnosis. (Source: [Agência Senado](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara))
- Generative AI: preliminary risk assessment before market release and identifiers for synthetic content. (Source: [Agência Senado](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara))
- Copyright: text mining allowed for research under conditions; commercial AI developers must remunerate rights holders; use of voice and image needs consent. (Source: [Agência Senado](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara))
- Governance and enforcement: the ANPD coordinates a National System for AI Regulation and Governance (SIA) with sectoral regulators. Sanctions reach R$50 million or 2 per cent of gross revenue per violation, plus suspension. (Source: [Agência Senado](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara))
- Workers: human oversight of automated discipline and dismissal decisions and retraining support were in the Senate text. (Source: [Agência Senado](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara))
- Concession made in the Senate: social media recommendation algorithms were removed from the high-risk list at the request of opposition senators, against the government's wishes. (Source: [Agência Senado](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara))
- Transition: most provisions would apply 730 days after publication; generative AI, prohibited uses and copyright rules after 180 days. (Source: [Agência Senado](https://www12.senado.leg.br/noticias/materias/2024/12/10/senado-aprova-regulamentacao-da-inteligencia-artificial-texto-vai-a-camara))
- Chamber of Deputies: received 17 March 2025. A special committee was formed, with Deputy Aguinaldo Ribeiro (PP-PB) as rapporteur from 20 May 2025. The work plan set ten thematic hearings, five regional seminars and an international seminar, with the report due 25 November 2025 and a vote in December 2025. (Source: [Câmara dos Deputados bill page](https://www.camara.leg.br/proposicoesWeb/fichadetramitacao?idProposicao=2487262); [DIAP](https://www.diap.org.br/index.php/noticias/noticias/92249-pl-2338-23-votacao-do-projeto-sobre-inteligencia-artificial-esta-prevista-apenas-para-dezembro))
- 19 December 2025: the vote was pushed to February 2026 by agreement between the presidencies of the two houses, citing lack of consensus. Open disputes: copyright and training data, the definition of unacceptable technologies, facial recognition exceptions for public security, labour safeguards removed during Chamber proceedings, and environmental effects. The Coalizão Direitos na Rede (49 organisations) demanded more transparency. The executive proposed a separate bill to create the SIA. The government also tried to link the data-centre tax regime Redata (MP 1.318/25) to the AI framework. (Source: [Desinformante, Dec 2025](https://desinformante.com.br/votacao-do-marco-da-ia-fica-para-2026-em-meio-a-impasses-politicos-e-criticas-ao-texto))
- 2026: the February vote did not happen. The Chamber portal records only the attachment of further AI bills (7 April 2026, May to June 2026, 2 September 2026) and shows the bill awaiting the rapporteur's opinion in the special committee, with priority status and no plenary vote. (Source: [Câmara dos Deputados bill page, read 3 Oct 2026](https://www.camara.leg.br/proposicoesWeb/fichadetramitacao?idProposicao=2487262))
- Brazil ranked 11th in the 2026 Global Index on Responsible AI, first in South and Central America. (Source: [Wikipedia, Regulation of artificial intelligence, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_of_artificial_intelligence))

### Inferences
- Policy elements in comparable form: legal form is a proposed comprehensive statute (rights-based and risk-based); risk tiers and prohibited uses on the EU pattern; duties on developers and deployers; generative AI and synthetic content labelling; copyright remuneration; civil liability; data protection authority as lead regulator within a sectoral system; penalties as a share of revenue; worker protections unusual among peers; data-centre incentives pursued separately through Redata.
- The official account is that Brazil is building a rights-protective framework. Practice in 2025 and 2026 is delay: each scheduled vote slipped, the October 2026 general election compresses the calendar, and the points of dispute (copyright, social media algorithms, facial recognition for policing, labour) are those where large platforms, security agencies and business have the most at stake.
- Brazil shows the same pairing seen elsewhere: a slow accountability statute alongside a fast-moving investment instrument (Redata) for data centres.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved explains the 2026 delay directly or confirms whether a rapporteur's substitute text was circulated. Law-firm and consultancy posts claiming a May 2026 vote could not be opened and are not relied on.
- ANPD's current legal status (its reported 2025 conversion into a regulatory agency), its AI-related enforcement record (for example the 2024 order against Meta's use of user data for training), its regulatory sandbox, and LGPD Article 20 (review of automated decisions) were not confirmed from primary sources in this session.
- The Brazilian AI Plan (PBIA) funding and the fate of Redata were not confirmed.
- Evidence of lobbying by technology firms in the Chamber is asserted in secondary commentary but not sourced here.

## UAE and Saudi Arabia: strategy-led and investment-led approach, sovereign compute, and any binding rules

### Takeaway
Neither Gulf state has an AI-specific law with general application. Both govern AI through national strategies, ethics principles and guidance, with binding force only in data protection, cybercrime and financial free-zone rules. The real policy is industrial: state-owned vehicles (G42 and MGX in Abu Dhabi, Humain under the Saudi PIF) building gigawatt-scale compute with US partners, under US export-control conditions that function as the most binding external constraint on either country's AI sector.

### Cited Findings

#### UAE
- No standalone federal AI statute. Non-binding instruments: National Strategy for AI 2031 (2018), Deepfake Guide (2021), AI Ethics Guide (December 2022), AI Adoption Guideline in Government Services (2023), UAE Charter for the Development and Use of AI (30 July 2024, 12 principles), a January 2026 white paper on "regulatory intelligence" launched at the World Economic Forum, and a Central Bank guidance note on AI and consumer protection (February 2026). (Source: [Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer AI tracker, updated 1 Apr 2026, lead only](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/reports/ai-tracker/uae); [White and Case tracker, Oct 2024, lead only](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/ai-watch-global-regulatory-tracker-uae))
- Binding rules that reach AI: the federal Personal Data Protection Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (the UAE Data Office is reported as not yet established), cybercrime and copyright laws, DIFC Data Protection Regulations (Regulation 10 on autonomous and semi-autonomous systems), ADGM data protection rules, Abu Dhabi Law No. 3 of 2024 creating the AI and Advanced Technology Council, and Federal Decree-Law No. 25 of 2018 allowing Cabinet to license innovative projects. (Source: [HSF Kramer tracker, lead only](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/reports/ai-tracker/uae); [White and Case tracker, lead only](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/ai-watch-global-regulatory-tracker-uae))
- Institutions: a Minister of State for AI, the UAE AI Office, the Abu Dhabi AI and Advanced Technology Council, and sector regulators. Public consultation on regulation is "not standard procedure in the UAE". (Source: [White and Case tracker](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/ai-watch-global-regulatory-tracker-uae); [Bird and Bird tracker, lead only](https://www.twobirds.com/en/capabilities/artificial-intelligence/ai-legal-services/ai-regulatory-horizon-tracker/uae))
- Sovereign compute and state ownership: G42 is chaired by Sheikh Tahnoun bin Zayed Al Nahyan, the national security adviser. Microsoft invested US$1.5 billion in April 2024. G42 agreed to phase out Huawei equipment (December 2023) and divested Chinese holdings (February 2024) after US pressure. MGX was launched in March 2024 by the Abu Dhabi government with G42 and Mubadala. (Source: [Wikipedia, G42, citing Reuters, FT, Bloomberg, NYT, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G42_(company)))
- Stargate UAE was announced in May 2025 with G42, OpenAI, Oracle, Nvidia, SoftBank and Cisco, with first operations expected in 2026. (Source: [Wikipedia, Stargate UAE, citing CNBC, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stargate_UAE))
- Unconfirmed against a primary source: in November 2025 the US authorised G42 to buy up to 35,000 Nvidia Blackwell-class chips, and a Bureau of Industry and Security rule effective 10 July 2026 (cited as 91 FR 43034) moved the UAE into Country Group A:5 and named approved recipients, with G42 and Core42 status expiring on 6 April 2027 unless conditions are met. Stargate UAE is described as 1 gigawatt within a 5 gigawatt campus, with the first 200 megawatts targeted for 2026 and no confirmed energisation at 31 July 2026. (Source: [vision2030.ai, 31 July 2026, an independent commentary site of unknown reliability, lead only](https://vision2030.ai/geopolitics/us-chip-export-controls-saudi-uae-divergence/); partly corroborated by [Wikipedia, G42](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G42_(company)))
- G42 launched a "Digital Embassies" framework for cloud governance in January 2026 and announced sovereign AI infrastructure partnerships abroad (Vietnam, India, United States, France, Italy) in 2026. (Source: [Wikipedia, G42, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G42_(company)))

#### Saudi Arabia
- "There are currently no AI Regulations that have the force of law in the Kingdom." The Saudi Data and AI Authority (SDAIA), which reports to the Prime Minister, issues non-binding instruments: AI Ethics Principles (September 2023), Generative AI Guidelines for government and for the public (January 2024), and an AI Adoption Framework (September 2024). (Source: [White and Case tracker, June 2024, lead only](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/ai-watch-global-regulatory-tracker-saudi-arabia); [HSF Kramer tracker, updated 12 Feb 2026, lead only](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/reports/ai-tracker/saudi-arabia))
- Binding rules that reach AI: the Personal Data Protection Law, the Anti-Cybercrime Law, the Essential Cybersecurity Controls (amended 2024) and evidence rules. Draft IP amendments on AI from April 2023 remained unenacted in February 2026. (Source: [HSF Kramer tracker, lead only](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/reports/ai-tracker/saudi-arabia))
- Sovereign compute: Humain was founded on 12 May 2025 by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman and is owned by the Public Investment Fund. It announced deals with Nvidia (18,000 chips in a first phase), AMD and Qualcomm in May 2025, plans "several gigawatts" of capacity, and develops the Arabic model ALLaM. (Source: [Wikipedia, Humain, citing Reuters, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humain))
- Unconfirmed against a primary source: on 19 November 2025 the US authorised Humain to buy up to 35,000 Blackwell-class chips subject to security and reporting conditions. As at 31 July 2026 Saudi Arabia had no equivalent of the UAE's published export rule, and Humain declined to disclose chip deliveries in June and July 2026. (Source: [vision2030.ai, 31 July 2026, lead only](https://vision2030.ai/geopolitics/us-chip-export-controls-saudi-uae-divergence/))

#### Official versus practice
- Official account: both states present AI as economic diversification and state modernisation, with ethics principles to assure responsible use. The UAE presents its regulatory model as agile "regulatory intelligence". (Source: [HSF Kramer UAE tracker](https://www.hsfkramer.com/insights/reports/ai-tracker/uae))
- Practice: the principal actors are state-owned or ruler-linked firms, so the state is promoter, owner, customer and regulator at once. The hard constraints come from Washington: divestment from Chinese hardware, chip caps, reporting requirements and entity-level approvals. (Source: [Wikipedia, G42, citing Reuters and NYT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G42_(company)))

### Inferences
- Policy elements in comparable form: legal form is strategy plus soft law; no risk tiers or prohibited uses; no developer or frontier duties; binding rules only via data protection, cybercrime, and free-zone regulation; no independent AI regulator; penalties only under adjacent laws; public-sector adoption is a goal in itself; compute and data centres are the core policy; international alignment is with US export-control terms.
- Drivers: economic diversification away from hydrocarbons, state capacity and prestige, and geopolitical positioning as the preferred third pole for US-aligned compute. Light regulation is part of the investment offer.
- The Gulf case adds a theme the other jurisdictions lack: AI governance by supply-chain conditionality, where another state's export rules do the regulating.
- The UAE has moved further than Saudi Arabia in converting US political agreements into a published, rule-based export status. If confirmed, that divergence is a useful example of how alignment with a patron state shapes domestic AI capacity.

### Gaps
- No Reuters, FT or government primary source was retrieved in this session for the chip authorisations, the July 2026 BIS rule, Stargate UAE capacity, or Humain deliveries. All need confirmation before use as fact.
- Reported items not confirmed: a draft Saudi "Global AI Hub Law" (2025 consultation), UAE use of AI in legislative drafting (Regulatory Intelligence Office, 2025), any UAE child digital safety or media law provisions on AI content, and Dubai or Abu Dhabi government AI strategies for 2025 to 2027.
- No independent analysis of surveillance uses of AI, labour conditions, or the absence of civil society input was retrieved.

## Africa: the AU Continental AI Strategy (2024), national strategies and draft laws

### Takeaway
The African Union's Continental AI Strategy (endorsed July 2024) is non-binding and frames AI as a development tool under Agenda 2063. By 2026 between 13 and 16 African states had national AI strategies and none had a binding AI statute. Kenya published an EU-style Artificial Intelligence Bill in 2026, Nigeria has bills pending, and South Africa withdrew its draft national AI policy on 26 April 2026 after it was found to contain fictitious AI-generated citations. Data protection authorities are the de facto AI regulators.

### Cited Findings

#### African Union
- The AU Executive Council endorsed the Continental AI Strategy at its 45th Ordinary Session in Accra on 18 to 19 July 2024. It promotes an "Africa-centric, development-focused approach to AI", ties AI to Agenda 2063 and the SDGs, and stresses job creation and preservation of African culture. (Source: [African Union](https://au.int/en/documents/20240809/continental-artificial-intelligence-strategy))
- Five focus areas: harnessing benefits, building capabilities, minimising risks, fostering cooperation, stimulating investment. Phase I runs 2025 to 2026 (governance frameworks, national strategies, capacity), with a mid-term review in 2027 and Phase II from 2028. It is non-binding: "There are no comprehensive, binding laws or regulations specifically governing AI across the entire African Union." (Source: [White and Case tracker, 4 Dec 2024, lead only](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/ai-watch-global-regulatory-tracker-african-union))
- The strategy contains fifteen policy recommendations and invokes Ubuntu and African values. (Source: [Africa Data Protection, Governance of AI in Africa, July 2026 edition](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf))
- Unconfirmed: an Africa Declaration on AI signed by 49 ministers in Kigali in April 2025 with a pledged US$60 billion Africa AI Fund, and a Smart Africa model AI policy framework announced in July 2025. (Source: [Techmoonshot, 26 May 2026, lead only](https://techmoonshot.com/2026/05/26/africa-ai-regulation-2026-a-country-by-country-map-of-whos-ahead-and-whos-stalling/))

#### Continental picture
- The OECD (April 2026 case study, from a Nairobi policy dialogue with officials of 12 African countries, co-organised with AFD and the UK FCDO) counts 13 national AI strategies adopted since 2018 and finds "common challenges in operationalising these strategies and translating policy objectives into effective governance and implementation". Its focus areas are agriculture, local language models and regulatory frameworks. (Source: [OECD, AI governance in Africa, 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-artificial-intelligence-case-studies_c517fcf5-en/ai-governance-in-africa_1ff55135-en.html))
- A trade source counts 16 of 54 countries with strategies or frameworks by April 2026. The counts conflict (13 versus 16), probably because of different definitions and dates. (Source: [Techmoonshot, lead only](https://techmoonshot.com/2026/05/26/africa-ai-regulation-2026-a-country-by-country-map-of-whos-ahead-and-whos-stalling/))
- "Most countries do not yet have a binding text" and "capacities for implementation, oversight and financing remain uneven". Three regulatory models are emerging: a single AI regulator (Kenya, Angola), distribution across existing regulators (Ghana), and principles-based (Nigeria). (Source: [Africa Data Protection, July 2026](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf))

#### Kenya
- National AI Strategy 2025 to 2030 published 27 March 2025. Non-binding. Pillars: AI digital infrastructure, data ecosystem, research and innovation. Aim: an African AI hub. (Source: [ICTLC, lead only](https://www.ictlc.com/the-rise-of-ai-legislation-in-africa-kenya-in-focus/?lang=en))
- The Artificial Intelligence Bill, 2026 was published on 19 February 2026 as a Senate Bill. It adopts four risk tiers on the EU model (unacceptable, high, limited, minimal), creates an independent Office of the Artificial Intelligence Commissioner with inspection, enforcement notice and fining powers, and a public register of high-risk systems. As at 30 April 2026 it had not been tabled. It needs passage by both houses. (Source: [Africa Data Protection, July 2026](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf); [Afriwise, reproducing Bowmans analysis, 30 Apr 2026, lead only](https://www.afriwise.com/blog/artificial-intelligence-bill-2026---proposed-too-soon))
- Critique of the Kenyan bill: transparency duties apply regardless of risk level, classification criteria are left to later regulations, and obligations overlap with data protection law. (Source: [Afriwise and Bowmans, lead only](https://www.afriwise.com/blog/artificial-intelligence-bill-2026---proposed-too-soon))

#### Nigeria
- A National AI Strategy with five pillars and thirty-four strategies. A private member's Control of Usage of AI Technology Bill, 2023. Senate Bill 731 to create a National Artificial Intelligence Commission passed first reading on 25 February 2025. (Source: [Africa Data Protection, July 2026](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf); [ICTLC, lead only](https://www.ictlc.com/the-rise-of-ai-legislation-in-africa-kenya-in-focus/?lang=en))

#### South Africa
- A National AI Policy Framework was issued in 2024 (October 2024 per one source; often cited as August 2024). A draft National AI Policy was published on 10 April 2026 and withdrawn on 26 April 2026 because it contained fictitious citations generated by AI. The policy must be redeveloped. In the meantime POPIA and the Information Regulator fill the gap. (Source: [Africa Data Protection, July 2026](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf); [Techmoonshot, lead only](https://techmoonshot.com/2026/05/26/africa-ai-regulation-2026-a-country-by-country-map-of-whos-ahead-and-whos-stalling/))

#### Rwanda and others
- Rwanda has a national AI policy (dated 2023 by one source and 2022 by another), with ethical oversight and risk assessment mechanisms, hosts the Centre for the Fourth Industrial Revolution, and runs an AI Scaling Hub with Gates Foundation support (US$7.5 million over three years, unconfirmed). A reported early 2026 partnership with Anthropic is unconfirmed. (Source: [White and Case tracker](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-our-thinking/ai-watch-global-regulatory-tracker-african-union); [Techmoonshot, lead only](https://techmoonshot.com/2026/05/26/africa-ai-regulation-2026-a-country-by-country-map-of-whos-ahead-and-whos-stalling/))
- Egypt has a second-edition National AI Strategy 2025 to 2030 and national guidelines for trustworthy AI with a readiness assessment tool. Mauritius has a strategy and non-binding FAIR guidelines with four risk levels. Ghana has a 2023 to 2033 strategy. (Source: [Africa Data Protection, July 2026](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf))

#### Official drivers versus practice
- Official: development goals (Agenda 2063, SDGs), jobs, cultural preservation, the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI as a common base, and regional leadership ambitions. (Source: [African Union](https://au.int/en/documents/20240809/continental-artificial-intelligence-strategy); [Africa Data Protection, July 2026](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf))
- Practice: strategies outnumber laws; implementation, oversight capacity and finance are uneven; interoperability between national frameworks and Africa's voice in global governance are unresolved; four countries (Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Rwanda) reportedly account for most AI investment (83 per cent, unconfirmed). (Source: [OECD, 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-artificial-intelligence-case-studies_c517fcf5-en/ai-governance-in-africa_1ff55135-en.html); [Africa Data Protection, July 2026](https://www.africadataprotection.org/Governance_of_AI_in_Africa_by_AfricaDataProtectionV2.pdf); [Techmoonshot, lead only](https://techmoonshot.com/2026/05/26/africa-ai-regulation-2026-a-country-by-country-map-of-whos-ahead-and-whos-stalling/))

### Inferences
- Policy elements in comparable form: legal form is continental soft law plus national strategies; Kenya's bill is the only text retrieved with risk tiers, prohibited uses, a dedicated regulator and a register; elsewhere data protection authorities enforce; no frontier model rules; skills, local-language models, agriculture and infrastructure dominate; external partners (OECD, AFD, FCDO, Gates Foundation, UNESCO) shape agendas.
- Where African states draft hard law they borrow the EU template (Kenya, Mauritius risk levels), which suggests regulatory diffusion and donor or partner alignment as drivers, in tension with the "Africa-centric" framing.
- The South African withdrawal is a concrete example of capacity limits in policy-making itself and of AI use inside government outrunning controls.

### Gaps
- The full OECD PDF and the AU strategy text were not readable, so funding figures, data-centre statistics and the detailed AU action plan are missing.
- Primary confirmation is needed for the Kigali declaration and fund, the Rwanda policy date, Nigeria's strategy status and bill progress in 2026, and the progress of Kenya's bill after April 2026.
- No peer-reviewed analysis was retrieved on drivers such as data sovereignty, dependence on foreign cloud providers, or the influence of Chinese and US vendors.

## Cross-jurisdiction: stated drivers versus drivers identified by independent analysts, and where practice diverges

### Takeaway
Across these jurisdictions the official account stresses productivity, trust, inclusion or development. The observed pattern is retreat from or delay of horizontal AI statutes (Australia, Canada, Brazil), substitution by strategies, compute and data-centre policy, and hard law only for child safety, deepfakes, privacy and government's own use. The most common divergence is that the safeguards promised as the reason no AI law is needed (privacy reform, ADM frameworks, gap analyses) lag well behind the investment measures.

### Cited Findings
- Productivity as stated driver: Australia's Productivity Commission estimate of about A$116 billion over a decade and its call to pause guardrails; New Zealand's NZ$76 billion claim sourced to Microsoft; Canada's 250,000 jobs target. (Source: [Productivity Commission](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf); [NZ strategy](https://www.beehive.govt.nz/sites/default/files/2025-07/New%20Zealand's%20AI%20Strategy%20-%20Investing%20with%20confidence.pdf); [Legal 500, lead only](https://www.legal500.com/developments/thought-leadership/canada-unveils-national-ai-strategy-key-commitments-regulatory-gaps-and-whats-missing/))
- Evidence against the productivity premise: a Dais study found no relationship between AI adoption and short-term productivity in Canadian firms; only 2 per cent of Canadian business leaders reported return on generative AI. The Productivity Commission itself notes wide uncertainty. (Source: [The Walrus](https://thewalrus.ca/evan-solomon-ai/); [Productivity Commission](https://assets.pc.gov.au/2025-12/data-digital_0.pdf))
- US pressure and alignment with a dominant partner: Husic's claim that Australia "blinked in the face of Donald Trump" (denied by Ayres); the Gulf states' acceptance of US export conditions and divestment from Chinese hardware; the US signing India's declaration once the safety framing was removed. (Source: [ABC News, 8 June 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-06-08/federal-government-ai-policy-retreat-data-centres-four-corners/106753596); [Wikipedia, G42, lead only](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G42_(company)); [ORF](https://www.orfonline.org/expert-speak/from-dialogue-to-deployment-strategic-outcomes-of-the-india-ai-impact-summit-2026))
- Investment attraction: "open for business" (Australia), Invest New Zealand, Canada's compute fund, India's US$200 billion pledges, Brazil's Redata, Gulf sovereign vehicles. (Source: [Australian ministerial release](https://www.minister.industry.gov.au/ministers/timayres/media-releases/new-agreement-ai-collaboration-anthropic); [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2234343&reg=3&lang=1); [Desinformante](https://desinformante.com.br/votacao-do-marco-da-ia-fica-para-2026-em-meio-a-impasses-politicos-e-criticas-ao-texto))
- Public-sector scandal as driver: Robodebt drove Australia's ADM consultation and the DTA policy, yet the framework law is still absent and a further unlawful automation case was found in August 2025. ### Inferences
- Candidate policy themes that work across all seven jurisdictions: (1) legal form (statute, delegated rules, mandatory administrative policy, soft law); (2) risk classification and prohibited uses; (3) allocation of duties between developers, deployers and platforms; (4) frontier model oversight (safety institutes, voluntary commitments, export conditions); (5) synthetic content and deepfakes; (6) child safety and companion chatbots; (7) privacy and automated decision-making; (8) copyright and training data; (9) government's own use; (10) regulator design and penalties; (11) compute, data centres and energy; (12) skills and labour protections; (13) international alignment.
- Candidate driver themes: productivity and growth narratives; competition for investment and compute; alignment with or pressure from the United States; domestic constituencies with concentrated interests (creators, unions, child-safety advocates) versus diffuse ones; institutional design (promotion and regulation housed in one ministry); public-sector failures; sovereignty and prestige; development and inclusion; regulatory diffusion from the EU; state control of information.
- Two regularities stand out. First, safeguards for citizens are sequenced after investment measures everywhere. Second, the public sector is regulated more tightly than the private sector in the light-touch Anglophone states, while in India, the Gulf and much of Africa the state is the main AI promoter and is lightly constrained.
- Hard law tends to appear where the regulated party is foreign and the protected party is sympathetic and organised (children, creators), which explains Australia's and Canada's firmness on online safety and Australia's on copyright alongside their retreat on general AI accountability.

### Gaps
- These cross-cutting inferences rest on a limited source base for India, Brazil, the Gulf and Africa. Peer-reviewed comparative work and OECD.AI observatory entries were not retrieved and should be consulted before the themes are finalised.
- Evidence on lobbying is mostly positional (who said what publicly), not documentary.
