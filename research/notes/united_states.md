# United States: AI policy and regulation at federal and state level, as at October 2026

Research date: 3 October 2026. All claims are dated. Source quality is flagged where it falls below the bar. "Law-firm summary" means the fact is a description of a primary legal text taken from a law-firm client alert and not checked against the primary text in this session. "Unconfirmed" means only a low-quality or secondary source was found.

Comparability summary for the report writer (each row is supported in the sections below):

| Theme | United States position as at October 2026 |
|:-|:-|
| Legal form | No comprehensive federal AI statute. Federal policy runs through executive orders, OMB memos, agency use of existing law, export control regulations and voluntary agreements. Binding AI-specific rules are mostly state statutes. |
| Scope | Federal: government use and procurement, exports, frontier model security (voluntary). States: sector and harm specific laws (frontier model transparency, automated decisions, chatbots, deepfakes, employment, health). |

| Liability | No federal AI liability regime. White House framework opposes developer liability for third-party misuse. Some state chatbot laws create private rights of action. |
| Enforcement and penalties | State attorneys general are the main enforcers. Penalties range from USD 500 per violation (California employment) to USD 1 million to USD 3 million (frontier laws). FTC uses section 5 of the FTC Act. |

## Key Question 1: What is federal policy as at 2026?

### Takeaway
As at October 2026 there is no general federal AI statute. Federal policy is a deregulatory, "dominance" oriented executive programme (EO 14179, the July 2025 AI Action Plan, EO 14365 on state laws) combined with growing national security intervention in frontier models (voluntary pre-release access under a 2 June 2026 order, and a 12 June 2026 export control letter that forced Anthropic to switch off two models for 19 days).

### Cited Findings

Executive orders and strategy

- EO 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence", was signed in the first week of the Trump administration (January 2025). The White House Office of Science and Technology Policy describes it as rescinding the Biden AI executive order (EO 14110) and refocusing policy on economic competitiveness, national security and freedom from ideological bias. Source: [White House OSTP, "Science and Technology Highlights: Year One", January 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf)
- America's AI Action Plan was released on 23 July 2025. It has three pillars (accelerating AI innovation, building American AI infrastructure, leading in international diplomacy and security) and lists more than 90 federal policy actions. Source: [White House OSTP](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf)
- Three executive orders were signed with the Plan on 23 July 2025: EO 14318 "Accelerating Federal Permitting of Data Center Infrastructure", EO 14319 "Preventing Woke AI in the Federal Government", and EO 14320 "Promoting the Export of the American AI Technology Stack". Source: [White House OSTP](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf)
- Other orders: EO 14277 "Advancing Artificial Intelligence Education for American Youth" (23 April 2025). EO 14363 "Launching the Genesis Mission" (24 November 2025), which mobilises the Department of Energy's 17 national laboratories and aims to double the productivity of federally funded research within a decade, with more than USD 320 million in initial investments announced. The American AI Exports Program was established on 21 October 2025. Source: [White House OSTP](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf); [White House, "Launching the Genesis Mission"](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/11/launching-the-genesis-mission/)
- Official rationale, quoted: "America is in a race to achieve global dominance in AI, and the nation that wins will shape the industries, set the standards, and secure the strategic advantages of the 21st century." Source: [White House OSTP](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf)
- EO 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence", was signed on 11 December 2025 (90 FR, 16 December 2025). Stated policy: to "sustain and enhance the United States' global AI dominance through a minimally burdensome national policy framework for AI". Operative parts: (s 3) the Attorney General must set up an AI Litigation Task Force within 30 days to challenge state AI laws on Commerce Clause, pre-emption or other grounds. (s 4) Commerce must within 90 days identify "onerous" state laws, including laws that require models to "alter their truthful outputs". (s 5) Broadband (BEAD) non-deployment funds and discretionary grants may be conditioned on states not having such laws. (s 6) The FCC is to start a proceeding on a federal AI reporting and disclosure standard that pre-empts conflicting state rules. (s 7) The FTC is to issue a policy statement on when state laws that require altered outputs are pre-empted by the FTC Act's ban on deception. (s 8) A legislative recommendation for a federal framework is to be prepared, which should not pre-empt state laws on child safety, data centre infrastructure, or state government procurement and use of AI. Source: [Federal Register, EO 14365, 16 December 2025](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-16/html/2025-23092.htm)
- The White House released its four-page "National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" legislative recommendations on 20 March 2026. It was prepared by OSTP Director Michael Kratsios and Special Adviser for AI and Crypto David Sacks. Seven pillars: protecting children and empowering parents; safeguarding communities; respecting intellectual property and supporting creators; preventing censorship and protecting free speech; enabling innovation and US AI dominance; educating an AI-ready workforce; and a federal framework with targeted pre-emption of state AI laws. It opposes a new AI regulator and favours existing agencies and "industry-led standards". It would bar states from regulating AI model development and from penalising developers for third-party misuse, while preserving generally applicable state laws, zoning for data centres and state procurement. On copyright it defers to the courts. On child safety it calls for "commercially reasonable, privacy protective, age assurance requirements" and warns against open-ended liability. Source: [Roll Call, 20 March 2026](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/20/white-house-ai-framework-calls-for-preemption-of-state-laws/); [Holland & Knight summary, March 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/white-house-releases-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial)
- EO "Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security" was signed on 2 June 2026. It creates a voluntary framework under which developers give the government access to frontier models 30 days before public release, and may share early access with critical infrastructure operators. It orders a "classified benchmarking process to assess the advanced cyber capabilities of AI models". The NSA Director determines which models are designated "covered frontier models". It directs the Attorney General to prioritise prosecution of AI-enabled computer crime and sets up a voluntary vulnerability discovery and patching mechanism. The order states that it "is not creating a governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement". Source: [JURIST, June 2026](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/06/trump-signs-executive-order-calling-for-government-access-to-frontier-ai-models/); [Crowell & Moring, June 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/executive-order-creates-voluntary-regulatory-regime-of-frontier-ai-models)

Federal agency use and procurement (OMB)

- OMB memos M-25-21 (agency use) and M-25-22 (acquisition) were issued on 3 April 2025. They replace the Biden-era M-24-10 and M-24-18. Agencies must retain or designate a Chief AI Officer within 60 days and set up AI Governance Boards within 90 days. "High-impact AI" is defined as AI whose output is a principal basis for decisions affecting civil rights, liberties or privacy, access to education, housing, insurance, credit or employment, critical government services, health and safety, critical infrastructure, or strategic assets. Seven minimum practices apply to high-impact AI: pre-deployment testing, AI impact assessment, ongoing monitoring, operator training, human oversight, appeals or remedies, and consultation with end users and the public. Compliance was due within 365 days. M-25-22 applies to solicitations issued after 30 September 2025, tells agencies to maximise use of US-produced AI, to avoid vendor lock-in, and bars vendors from training public models on non-public agency data without consent. Source: [Wiley, April 2025 (law-firm summary)](https://www.wiley.law/alert-Trump-Administration-Revamps-Guidance-on-Federal-Use-and-Procurement-of-AI)
- OMB memo M-26-04, "Increasing Public Trust in Artificial Intelligence Through Unbiased AI Principles" (December 2025), implements EO 14319 by requiring "unbiased AI principles" in federal procurement of large language models. The Department of Energy issued an acquisition letter implementing it on 8 May 2026. Source: [OMB M-26-04 (primary PDF)](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/M-26-04-Increasing-Public-Trust-in-Artificial-Intelligence-Through-Unbiased-AI-Principles-1.pdf); [DOE Acquisition Letter 2026-05](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2026-05/AL%202026-05%20Unbiased%20AI%20Principles%20M-26-04_5.8.26.pdf)

NIST and the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI)

- The Biden-era US AI Safety Institute at NIST was rebranded in 2025 as the Center for AI Standards and Innovation. Source: [Nextgov/FCW, June 2026](https://www.nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/06/lawmakers-propose-ai-framework-would-preempt-state-laws-3-years/413975/)
- On 6 May 2026 NIST announced that CAISI had signed frontier AI testing agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft and xAI. The agreements allow pre-release and post-deployment evaluation, testing in classified environments, and access to models "with reduced or removed safeguards". Earlier partnerships were "renegotiated to align with directives from Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and the White House's AI Action Plan". CAISI reported more than 40 evaluations completed. Source: [ExecutiveGov, May 2026](https://www.executivegov.com/articles/caisi-ai-testing-google-deepmind-microsoft-xai)
- After the 2 June 2026 order, CAISI was reported to have stopped publishing its evaluations of frontier models. Senator Ted Budd (Republican, North Carolina) wrote on 26 June 2026 asking that CAISI be allowed to publish, noting the order gives the NSA Director the decision on when assessments are shared. Source: [Office of Senator Budd, 30 June 2026](https://www.budd.senate.gov/2026/06/30/budd-calls-for-caisi-to-resume-publishing-research-on-frontier-ai-models/)
- CAISI's funding is about USD 15 million a year. The draft Great American AI Act would raise it to USD 100 million a year through 2029. Source: [Tech Policy Press, June 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/unpacking-the-great-american-artificial-intelligence-act-of-2026/)
- The NIST AI Risk Management Framework remains voluntary. It has legal effect indirectly: Texas TRAIGA gives an affirmative defence to organisations that comply with it. Source: [American Bar Association, Business Law Today, July 2025](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/business_law/resources/business-law-today/2025-july/texas-enters-ai-sandbox-with-traiga-implications-business-trials/)
- Unconfirmed: CAISI Director Chris Fall resigned on 20 July 2026 and the body had no permanent director for 67 days as at 28 September 2026, with NIST's director acting. In September 2026 the Office of the National Cyber Director reportedly told OpenAI and Anthropic to withhold frontier models from the UK AI Security Institute until US review was complete (attributed to Politico and Bloomberg). Source: [TechTimes, 28 September 2026 (low-quality aggregator, not verified at Politico or Bloomberg)](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/328095/20260928/white-house-bars-uk-ai-safety-institute-frontier-ai-testing-caisi-us-body-has-no-director.htm)

FTC and other enforcement under existing law

- The FTC enforces against AI-related deception under section 5 of the FTC Act. Examples listed by one tracker: DoNotPay (February 2025, USD 193,000 redress), Evolv Technologies (November 2024), IntelliVision (December 2024), Workado (complaint April 2025, consent order August 2025). The Consumer Reviews Rule (16 CFR Part 465, in effect 21 October 2024) bans AI-generated fake reviews. In December 2025 the FTC set aside its consent order against Rytr, citing the AI Action Plan and undue burden on AI innovation. Source: [AI Policy Desk, 2026 (secondary tracker, individual cases not checked at ftc.gov)](https://www.aipolicydesk.com/blog/ftc-ai-enforcement-actions-2026)
- Shortly after 14 May 2026 the FTC issued a proposed "Policy Statement Concerning the Suppression of Accuracy in Artificial Intelligence Systems", as directed by EO 14365. It says some state AI laws, including Colorado's, "could create deception concerns under Section 5 of the FTC Act". Comments closed 31 July 2026. It is not binding and does not itself pre-empt state law. Source: [McDermott Will & Schulte, 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-narrows-its-ai-law-as-the-ftc-signals-a-new-federal-approach/)
- The DOJ AI Litigation Task Force was created under EO 14365. Its first court action was on 24 April 2026, when DOJ moved to intervene in xAI's suit against the Colorado AI Act, arguing the Act breaches the Equal Protection Clause because disparate-impact liability "compels discrimination". Source: [Jenner & Block, April 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/doj-joins-xai-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorado-ai-act); [DOJ memorandum establishing the Task Force](https://www.justice.gov/ag/media/1422986/dl?inline=)
- The Commerce evaluation of "onerous" state laws was due on 11 March 2026. As at late March 2026 it had not been publicly released. Source: [Holland & Knight, March 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/white-house-releases-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial); [S&P Global Market Intelligence, 5 March 2026](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/articles/2026/3/companies-face-compliance-limbo-as-trump-administration-targets-state-ai-laws-99326115)
- The Department of Defense designated Anthropic a "supply chain risk". Anthropic sued in March 2026, saying the label followed its refusal to supply the military without assurances against mass surveillance and autonomous weapons use. In August 2026 US District Judge Rita Lin ruled against the government, finding the action was "based on a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic for its 'arrogance' in criticizing the government". On 25 September 2026 the DC Circuit upheld the designation 2 to 1, rejecting First Amendment and due process claims. Judge Henderson dissented. Source: [ABC News, September 2026](https://abcnews.com/Business/anthropic-appeals-court-declines-block-pentagon-blacklisting/story?id=136755690); [Al Jazeera, 1 July 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/1/us-lifts-restrictions-on-powerful-ai-models-fable-mythos-anthropic-says)

Export controls on chips and models

- The Biden administration's interim final rule of 13 January 2025 (the "AI Diffusion Rule") created three country tiers, caps on chip exports, and a new control on closed model weights (ECCN 4E091) for models trained with more than 10^26 operations. Compliance was to start on 15 May 2025. Source: [Freshfields, January 2025 (law-firm summary)](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/a-fresh-take/ai-models-chips-and-data-centers-targeted-by-expansive-us-export-control-rule-102jw79)
- The Trump administration announced in May 2025 that it would rescind the AI Diffusion Rule. Formal removal from the Export Administration Regulations was still pending in January 2026. Source: [Baker McKenzie Sanctions and Export Controls blog, January 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://sanctionsnews.bakermckenzie.com/bis-revises-license-review-policy-for-advanced-computing-commodities-ai-semiconductors-to-china-and-macau-when-exported-from-the-united-states/)
- A BIS final rule effective 15 January 2026 moved licence review for certain advanced chips to China and Macau from presumption of denial to case by case review. It covers the Nvidia H200, AMD MI325X and equivalents (total processing performance below 21,000). Conditions include certification that US orders are not delayed, US third-party testing, a cap on China shipments at 50 per cent of US volumes, know-your-customer checks and remote access limits. At the same time a presidential proclamation imposed a 25 per cent duty on certain advanced chips. Source: [Baker McKenzie, January 2026](https://sanctionsnews.bakermckenzie.com/bis-revises-license-review-policy-for-advanced-computing-commodities-ai-semiconductors-to-china-and-macau-when-exported-from-the-united-states/)
- June 2026 model control: on 12 June 2026 BIS sent Anthropic a letter requiring an export licence for "any foreign persons, whether inside or outside of the United States" to access its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models. The letter cited the Export Control Reform Act and, reportedly, EAR section 744.22 (military-intelligence end use). The stated reason was national security concern about the models' ability to find cyber vulnerabilities after a partner (reported as Amazon) showed a jailbreak. Anthropic said it had to "abruptly disable Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for all our customers". CSIS notes uncertainty about BIS's legal authority to act this way. Source: [CSIS, June 2026](https://www.csis.org/analysis/department-commerce-restricted-access-anthropics-latest-models-what-comes-next)
- Lawfare describes the instrument as probably an "is informed" letter, which imposes a licence requirement without notice and comment rulemaking, and calls the effect "a global kill switch" because the company could not filter users by nationality. It reports David Sacks saying a "highly credible trusted partner" showed "the operability of a cyber weapon", that the administration asked Anthropic to fix or withdraw the models and Anthropic refused, and that Anthropic called the jailbreak "narrow" and said the issue was present in competing models. Source: [Lawfare, June 2026](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/a-kill-switch-for-frontier-ai)
- Commerce lifted the controls by letter from Secretary Lutnick. Access for critical infrastructure operators was approved on 27 June 2026 and general restoration began on 2 July 2026. Conditions: Anthropic must "proactively detect and address security risks", work with government on forthcoming model standards, and report "malicious activity". Source: [Al Jazeera, 1 July 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/1/us-lifts-restrictions-on-powerful-ai-models-fable-mythos-anthropic-says)

Compute, energy and industrial policy

- Infrastructure measures claimed by the White House: NEPA reforms and streamlined permitting for data centres (EO 14318), TSMC's USD 100 billion Arizona expansion (March 2025) and Micron's USD 200 billion memory investment (June 2025). Source: [White House OSTP](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf)

### Inferences
- Federal policy has two tracks that pull in different directions. The economic track removes or blocks rules on private AI development. The security track has moved toward direct state control of frontier models (classified benchmarking, pre-release access, a one-off export control letter, a supply chain risk designation). The second track is exercised through discretionary executive tools, not through general rules.
- The federal government regulates its own use of AI (OMB memos) more concretely than it regulates private use. For comparison purposes the US "high-impact AI" category is a public-sector construct only.
- Procurement is used as a values lever (the "unbiased AI principles" and the Anthropic designation), which is a form of regulation by contract.
- The June 2026 episode shows that export control law can function as a de facto deployment licence for frontier models, even though the 2 June order disclaims any licensing regime.

## Key Question 2: What have states enacted, and what is the status of federal pre-emption or moratorium attempts?

### Takeaway
States are the primary source of binding AI rules. By 1 July 2026, 29 states had enacted 109 AI laws in the 2026 session, with companion chatbot and child safety laws the most active area, and three states (California, New York, Illinois) now regulate frontier model developers. Every federal attempt at pre-emption has so far failed in Congress (99 to 1 Senate vote in July 2025; discussion draft only in 2026), so pre-emption is pursued through executive pressure and litigation, which contributed to Colorado repealing and replacing its AI Act in May 2026.

### Cited Findings

Scale and pattern

- State AI-related laws enacted rose from fewer than 10 in 2020 to 150 in 2025. Cumulative leaders 2016 to 2025: California 62, Maryland 28, Virginia 25, Utah 24. Stanford describes state legislatures as the primary venue for AI policy making given federal uncertainty. Source: [Stanford HAI, AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)
- As at 1 July 2026: 109 AI laws and 28 data centre laws enacted in 2026 across 29 states; 14 companion chatbot laws enacted from more than 100 bills; 6 laws restricting AI use by health insurers; 31 per cent of enacted AI laws had bipartisan sponsorship. For the first time both Republican-led and Democratic-led states enacted restrictive data centre laws (ratepayer protection, audits, tax incentive rollbacks). Source: [Tech Policy Press, July 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/where-state-ai-legislation-stands-half-way-into-2026/)

Colorado (automated decisions)

- The Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) was signed in May 2024. It required developers and deployers of "high-risk" AI systems to use reasonable care to prevent algorithmic discrimination in consequential decisions. Its start date moved from 1 February 2026 to 30 June 2026. Source: [Jenner & Block, April 2026](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/doj-joins-xai-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorado-ai-act)
- xAI sued on 9 April 2026 in the US District Court for the District of Colorado (x.AI LLC v. Weiser), alleging First Amendment, Commerce Clause, vagueness and Equal Protection violations. DOJ moved to intervene on 24 April 2026. On 28 April 2026 a magistrate judge granted a joint motion staying enforcement until Colorado adopted rules or new legislation. Source: [Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/doj-joins-xai-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorado-ai-act); [HR Dive, April 2026](https://www.hrdive.com/news/colorado-ai-bias-law-unconstitutional-elon-musks-xai/817258/)
- On 14 May 2026 Governor Polis signed SB 26-189, which repeals the Colorado AI Act and replaces it with an automated decision-making technology (ADMT) law effective 1 January 2027. Developers must give deployers a statement of intended and known harmful uses, training data categories, known limitations and guidance on human review. Deployers must give clear notice before using covered ADMT in a consequential decision, give a plain-language explanation within 30 days of an adverse outcome, and offer meaningful human review where commercially reasonable. Records must be kept for three years. Enforcement is exclusive to the Attorney General under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act, with no private right of action and a 60-day cure period that sunsets on 1 January 2030. Liability is apportioned by relative fault. The duty of care to prevent algorithmic discrimination and the impact assessment regime are gone. Attorney General rulemaking comments run from 11 August to at least 26 October 2026. Source: [Davis Polk, 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-landmark-ai-act-publishes-draft-rules-public); [Colorado Attorney General rulemaking page](https://coag.gov/ai/)

Frontier model laws (California, New York, Illinois)

- California SB 53 (Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act) took effect on 1 January 2026. It applies to frontier models trained with more than 10^26 operations. "Large frontier developers" are those with more than USD 500 million annual gross revenue. Duties: publish a frontier AI framework, publish transparency reports before deployment, report critical safety incidents (within 15 days) to the Office of Emergency Services, protect whistleblowers. Enforcement by the Attorney General. Penalties up to USD 1 million per violation. Source: [Morrison Foerster, April 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/260403-new-york-amends-the-raise-act-to-align-more-closely); [Stanford HAI, AI Index 2026](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)
- New York's RAISE Act was signed on 19 December 2025 and amended in March 2026 to align with SB 53. The amendment removed the earlier bar on deploying models that pose an "unreasonable risk of critical harm". It uses the same 10^26 and USD 500 million thresholds. Incident reporting is within 72 hours (24 hours for imminent danger). An oversight office sits in the Department of Financial Services. Penalties are up to USD 1 million for a first violation and USD 3 million for later ones. Effective 1 January 2027. ### Inferences
- State law has converged on a few templates that the report can treat as US "models": (1) frontier developer transparency with 10^26 and USD 500 million thresholds (California, New York, Illinois, and the federal draft); (2) notice, explanation and human review for automated consequential decisions (Colorado's replacement law, California employment law); (3) companion chatbot and child safety duties; (4) synthetic content disclosure; (5) intent-based prohibitions plus sandbox (Texas).
- The comprehensive, risk-based, EU-style model (the original Colorado AI Act) has been abandoned in the US without ever taking effect. The duty to prevent algorithmic discrimination was the element removed.
- Enforcement design is consistently light: attorney general only, cure periods, no private right of action. The exception is child safety chatbot law, where private actions appear (California SB 1119).
- Pre-emption is now framed federally as a line between "development" (to be federal) and "deployment and use" (left to states). Both the White House framework and the bipartisan draft use this line.
- Frontier regulation is becoming more intrusive at state level over 2025 to 2026 (transparency in 2025, mandatory audits in Illinois in 2026, embedded verifiers and a shut-off mechanism proposed by California in September 2026).

## Key Question 3: What do copyright litigation and US Copyright Office guidance establish about training data and outputs?

### Takeaway
There is no legislative rule on AI training. The law is being made by courts and is not settled: two 2025 district rulings found training on lawfully acquired books to be fair use, while the first appellate ruling (Third Circuit, 30 September 2026) rejected fair use for training a competing non-generative product. The largest outcome in money terms is a USD 1.5 billion settlement driven by pirated source copies, not by training itself.

### Cited Findings
- Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence: on 30 September 2026 a Third Circuit panel (Judge Montgomery-Reeves writing, Judges Restrepo and Bove concurring) affirmed that ROSS's use of Westlaw headnotes to train a competing legal research tool was not fair use. The use was "minimally transformative at best" and harmed both the direct market and a potential market for licensing AI training data. ROSS said it will seek Supreme Court review. The case was filed in 2020 and decided for Thomson Reuters on partial summary judgment in 2025. Source: [Courthouse News, 30 September 2026](https://www.courthousenews.com/ai-training-of-copyrighted-material-not-fair-use-third-circuit/)
- Analysts describe this as the first US appellate finding on fair use for AI training and note it does not resolve generative AI questions. Source: [MLex, 2026 (headline)](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/intellectual-property/articles/2531633); [Ballard Spahr, October 2026 (law-firm commentary, headline)](https://www.ballardspahr.com/insights/alerts-and-articles/2026/10/third-circuit-addresses-fair-use-in-ai-training-but-leaves-generative-ai-questions-unresolved)
- Bartz v. Anthropic: in June 2025 Judge Alsup held that training on and digitising lawfully purchased books was fair use but that keeping pirated copies in a central library was not. A USD 1.5 billion class settlement (about USD 3,000 per book, more than 400,000 works) received final approval on 22 July 2026 from Judge Martínez-Olguín. JURIST calls it the first settlement among dozens of AI copyright suits. Source: [JURIST, July 2026](https://www.jurist.org/news/2026/07/judge-approves-record-1-5-billion-settlement-involving-anthropic/)
- Kadrey v. Meta: summary judgment for Meta on fair use in 2025. As at 18 June 2026 plaintiffs were seeking interlocutory appeal to the Ninth Circuit on whether fair use can excuse acquisition by piracy, a point on which it conflicts with Bartz. Source: [McKool Smith litigation tracker, 18 June 2026 (law-firm tracker)](https://www.mckoolsmith.com/newsroom-ailitigation-62)
- New York Times v. OpenAI and Microsoft (SDNY multidistrict litigation 25-md-03143, Judge Stein): motion to dismiss partly denied in March 2025. Cross-motions for summary judgment were filed on 4 September 2026, with replies due 6 November 2026. No fair use ruling yet. Source: [AI Lawsuit Tracker, 27 September 2026 (secondary tracker)](https://ailawsuittracker.com/cases/new-york-times-v-openai/)
- Secondary liability claims have narrowed after the Supreme Court's Cox v. Sony decision. Concord Music v. Anthropic plaintiffs voluntarily dismissed secondary infringement claims. New suits continue, including Elsevier and others v. Meta (SDNY, 2026) alleging "systematic torrenting". Music label cases against Suno and Udio and studio cases against Midjourney remain pending. Source: [McKool Smith tracker, 18 June 2026](https://www.mckoolsmith.com/newsroom-ailitigation-62)
- US Copyright Office: Part 3 of its AI report (generative AI training) was issued as a pre-publication version on 9 May 2025. It says copying entire works for training generally weighs against fair use, though this may be justified where necessary, and it stresses market harm including "market dilution". It has not been formally published. Source: [US Copyright Office, Part 3 pre-publication version](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf); [Lowenstein Sandler, 2026 (law-firm commentary)](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/scotus-ruling-re-opens-the-door-on-the-copyright-office-s-ai-training-report-ai)
- The administration tried to remove Register of Copyrights Shira Perlmutter after the report. On 30 June 2026 the Supreme Court denied the government's application in Blanche v. Perlmutter (No. 25A478), leaving her in office. Source: [Lowenstein Sandler, 2026](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/scotus-ruling-re-opens-the-door-on-the-copyright-office-s-ai-training-report-ai)
- Executive position: the March 2026 White House framework declines to legislate on fair use, defers to the courts, and suggests exploring collective licensing. Source: [Roll Call, 20 March 2026](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/20/white-house-ai-framework-calls-for-preemption-of-state-laws/)
- State level: California AB 2013 requires training data summaries (see Key Question 2). Source: [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-04-24-state-ai-laws-where-are-they-now)

### Inferences
- The US approach to training data is ex post adjudication, not ex ante rule. This contrasts with jurisdictions that have statutory text and data mining exceptions or opt-out schemes.
- The emerging judicial line turns on (a) whether source copies were lawfully acquired and (b) whether the AI product substitutes for the original in its market. Neither question has a Supreme Court answer.
- Financial exposure has so far come from piracy of inputs, not from the act of training. That gives developers an incentive to license or buy inputs, which is a market-led route to the same result that a licensing statute would produce.
- The executive branch is not neutral in practice: it attempted to remove the official whose office published a report unfavourable to developers, while officially deferring to courts.

### Gaps
- Copyright Office positions on outputs were not re-verified this session. Background knowledge (lead only): Part 2 of the report (January 2025) said purely AI-generated material is not copyrightable and that human authorship is required, and the DC Circuit affirmed this in Thaler v. Perlmutter (March 2025). Whether the Supreme Court acted on Thaler was not checked.
- The full Third Circuit opinion was not read. The treatment of factor 3 and the exact limits stated for generative AI are not recorded here.
- Status of Getty v. Stability AI (US), Disney v. Midjourney and the music cases beyond "pending" was not obtained.
- I found no federal bill on AI and copyright that has advanced. Status of the NO FAKES Act is unknown.

## Key Question 4: What drivers are stated officially, and what do independent analysts say actually drove outcomes?

### Takeaway
The official federal drivers are AI "dominance" over China, national and economic security, innovation, and protection of speech from "ideological bias". States cite child safety, consumer and worker protection, and federal inaction. Independent analysis points to industry money and lobbying, partisan control, federalism and bipartisan state resistance, and specific incidents (chatbot harms to minors, the 2026 frontier model security incidents) as the forces that determined outcomes.

### Cited Findings

Stated drivers (official account)

- Dominance and competition: EO 14365 says US AI leadership "will promote United States national and economic security and dominance across many domains" and sets a policy of a "minimally burdensome national policy framework". Source: [Federal Register, EO 14365](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-16/html/2025-23092.htm)
- Race framing: "America is in a race to achieve global dominance in AI". Source: [White House OSTP, January 2026](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf)

EO 14365 targets state laws that require models to "alter their truthful outputs". The March 2026 framework has a pillar on "preventing censorship and protecting free speech". Source: [White House OSTP](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/WHOSTP-2025-Wins.pdf); [Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-16/html/2025-23092.htm); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/white-house-releases-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial)

Source: [CRN Asia, 2026 (trade press, headline only)](https://www.crnasia.com/news/2026/components-and-peripherals/trump-greenlights-nvidia-h200-chip-sales-to-china-after-mont)

### Inferences
- Candidate driver themes for cross-jurisdiction comparison, with the US reading: geopolitical competition (very strong, official); industrial and investment interests (very strong, observed through super PACs, hearing witnesses and procurement); constitutional structure and federalism (decisive for outcomes); partisan control (determines direction at both levels); free speech and culture-war framing (distinctive to the US); salient incidents (shift policy quickly, especially child harm and security events); public and bipartisan concern about children and energy costs (drives state law in both red and blue states); courts (set copyright and speech limits).
- Industry is not a single bloc. Venture capital and some developers fund deregulation and pre-emption. At least one large developer funds pro-regulation groups. Existing regulated industries are less visible in the sources.
- The China competition argument is used on both sides of the same question: to loosen rules on developers and chip sales, and to tighten state control over frontier model access.

### Gaps
- FEC filings were not checked. Super PAC totals come from Wikipedia and The Next Web (which cites CNBC), because NPR and CNBC pages could not be fetched. Treat the dollar figures as indicative.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests causes of the 2025 to 2026 federal outcomes. The causal reading rests on think-tank and news analysis.
- Public opinion data (for example, polling on AI regulation or data centres) was not collected.
- Congressional Research Service and CSET Georgetown products were not retrieved in this session.
- Effect of the November 2026 midterm elections is unknown as at this date.

## Key Question 5: Where does practice diverge from the official account?

### Takeaway
The main divergences are: a "light-touch, no licensing" federal stance alongside the most forceful intervention in commercial AI deployment to date; "deference to states on child safety and general laws" alongside litigation and funding threats aimed at state laws; an innovation and openness narrative alongside reduced transparency of the federal testing body; and a China-competition rationale alongside loosened chip sales to China.

### Cited Findings
- No licensing in principle, licensing in effect. The 2 June 2026 order says it "is not creating a governmental licensing, preclearance, or permitting requirement". Ten days later BIS imposed a licence requirement on foreign-person access to two models, which forced a global shutdown. Lawfare calls this "a global kill switch", notes it was done without rulemaking, and CSIS notes uncertainty about legal authority. Source: [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/executive-order-creates-voluntary-regulatory-regime-of-frontier-ai-models); [Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/a-kill-switch-for-frontier-ai); [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/department-commerce-restricted-access-anthropics-latest-models-what-comes-next)
- Voluntary in form, conditional in practice. Restoration of Anthropic's models came with conditions set by letter (security risk detection, cooperation on model standards, reporting malicious activity). Source: [Al Jazeera, 1 July 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/7/1/us-lifts-restrictions-on-powerful-ai-models-fable-mythos-anthropic-says)
- Uneven treatment of firms. Anthropic said the vulnerability was also present in competing models. A district court found the Defense Department's supply chain designation was retaliatory. The DC Circuit nonetheless upheld the designation on 25 September 2026. Source: [Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/a-kill-switch-for-frontier-ai); [ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Business/anthropic-appeals-court-declines-block-pentagon-blacklisting/story?id=136755690)
- Free speech framing versus procurement content rules. The administration frames state bias rules as compelled alteration of "truthful outputs", while its own procurement policy (EO 14319, M-26-04) sets content-related "unbiased AI principles" for models sold to government. Source: [Federal Register, EO 14365](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-16/html/2025-23092.htm); [OMB M-26-04](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/M-26-04-Increasing-Public-Trust-in-Artificial-Intelligence-Through-Unbiased-AI-Principles-1.pdf)
- Pre-emption by pressure, not by law. Congress has passed no pre-emption statute. The executive order itself cannot pre-empt state law. Yet Colorado's Act was stayed and then repealed after a company suit joined by DOJ, and the FTC issued a proposed statement that "would not preempt Colorado law" but signals section 5 exposure. Source: [GW Regulatory Studies Center](https://regulatorystudies.columbian.gwu.edu/ai-regulation-and-federalism-what-moratorium-wasnt-debate-revealed); [HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/colorado-ai-bias-law-unconstitutional-elon-musks-xai/817258/); [McDermott](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/colorado-narrows-its-ai-law-as-the-ftc-signals-a-new-federal-approach/)
- Announced enforcement versus actual enforcement. The Commerce list of "onerous" laws was due 11 March 2026 and had not been published by late March. The Task Force's only confirmed court action is one intervention. One commentator's headline in early 2026 was that the Task Force "has filed nothing" (opinion source, used as a lead only). Source: [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/03/white-house-releases-a-national-policy-framework-for-artificial); [Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/doj-joins-xai-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorado-ai-act)
- Federal deterrence has not stopped states. After EO 14365, 29 states enacted 109 AI laws in the first half of 2026, Illinois added mandatory frontier audits, and California added 13 or more AI laws on 30 September 2026. Source: [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/where-state-ai-legislation-stands-half-way-into-2026/); [Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/illinois-imposes-transparency-and-safety-obligations-on-frontier-ai-systems); [Governor of California](https://www.gov.ca.gov/2026/09/30/californias-nation-leading-ai-framework-just-got-stronger-governor-newsom-signs-more-first-in-the-nation-worker-protections-and-more/)
- State laws are weaker in operation than in headline. Colorado's flagship Act never took effect. New York removed its deployment prohibition before commencement. Utah narrowed its disclosure duties. Cooley notes limited enforcement to date in Utah and limited guidance in California. Source: [Cooley, April 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-04-24-state-ai-laws-where-are-they-now)
- Standards and openness versus secrecy. CAISI was presented as a standards and innovation body, yet after June 2026 it reportedly stopped publishing frontier model evaluations and benchmarking became classified. A Republican senator objected. Source: [Office of Senator Budd](https://www.budd.senate.gov/2026/06/30/budd-calls-for-caisi-to-resume-publishing-research-on-frontier-ai-models/)
- China competition versus chip sales. The stated aim is to beat China, yet BIS moved H200-class chip exports to China from presumed denial to case by case review in January 2026, paired with a 25 per cent duty. Source: [Baker McKenzie](https://sanctionsnews.bakermckenzie.com/bis-revises-license-review-policy-for-advanced-computing-commodities-ai-semiconductors-to-china-and-macau-when-exported-from-the-united-states/)
- Deference to courts on copyright versus the attempt to remove the Register of Copyrights after the training report. Source: [Lowenstein Sandler](https://www.lowenstein.com/news-insights/publications/client-alerts/scotus-ruling-re-opens-the-door-on-the-copyright-office-s-ai-training-report-ai); [Roll Call](https://rollcall.com/2026/03/20/white-house-ai-framework-calls-for-preemption-of-state-laws/)
- FTC consumer protection in name, retreat in a specific case. The FTC set aside its Rytr order in December 2025 on innovation grounds. Source: [AI Policy Desk (secondary tracker)](https://www.aipolicydesk.com/blog/ftc-ai-enforcement-actions-2026)
- Company practice ran ahead of law. Unconfirmed reports say OpenAI limited a GPT-5.6 series to "trusted partners" at government request and Anthropic released Mythos 5.1 only to US-vetted organisations on 1 September 2026. Source: [TechTimes, 28 September 2026 (low-quality, unconfirmed)](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/328095/20260928/white-house-bars-uk-ai-safety-institute-frontier-ai-testing-caisi-us-body-has-no-director.htm)
- Political spending tactics: Wikipedia, citing Tom's Hardware, Wired and Bloomberg, reports that a Leading the Future linked outlet used AI agents "posing as human journalists" (April 2026), paid influencers through a dark money campaign, and drew an FEC complaint (May 2026). Not verified at the original sources. Source: [Wikipedia, "Leading the Future" (tertiary)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_the_Future)

### Inferences
- In the US the gap between formal law and effective control is wide. The most consequential acts of 2026 (the model shutdown, the Colorado repeal, the supply chain designation) happened through letters, litigation positions and procurement decisions, not through statutes or rules. A comparison framework that counts only statutes will understate US federal control of frontier AI and overstate US state regulation of ordinary AI use.
- "Voluntary" federal mechanisms operate under a credible threat of coercion after June 2026, so their voluntariness should be treated as formal only.
- The official federal account presents states as the source of fragmentation. In practice states have converged on shared thresholds and templates, and the federal draft bill borrows them.
- The official account stresses rules that apply equally. Observed practice includes firm-specific actions that courts have partly found to be retaliatory.

### Gaps
- No systematic data on state enforcement actions under AI-specific laws was found. I found no reported penalty under SB 53, TRAIGA or the Utah Act as at October 2026, and cannot confirm that none exist.
- Federal agency compliance with OMB M-25-21 (inventories, high-impact AI practices by April 2026) was not assessed. No GAO or inspector general review was retrieved.
- Whether CAISI has resumed publication since June 2026 is unknown.
- Reports about UK institute access and OpenAI's restricted release need confirmation at Politico, Bloomberg or Reuters.
- The outcome of xAI's Ninth Circuit appeal on California AB 2013 is unknown.
