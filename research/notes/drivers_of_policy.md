# Drivers of AI policy and legislation across jurisdictions (as at 3 October 2026)

Reading notes for the report writer:
- Every finding carries a date and a source link. "Stated" means the driver appears in an official document or on-record statement. "Inferred" means independent researchers or reporters attribute the driver from behaviour.
- Claims from advocacy groups (Corporate Europe Observatory, LobbyControl, Public Citizen, EPIC, Amnesty) are labelled as interested-party claims. Law firm client alerts are used only for dates and legal content.
- Several September 2026 events rest on one roundup (Tech Policy Press, published 1 October 2026) with partial corroboration from Fortune. They are flagged where used.
- The web search budget ran out before every driver could be sourced. Uncovered items are listed under Gaps and should not be filled from memory.

## 1. What candidate drivers does the literature identify, and what is the evidence for each?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports about nineteen distinct drivers, and almost every major policy outcome since 2023 shows several operating at once. The best documented cases are economic competitiveness (EU Digital Omnibus, Australia's National AI Plan, Japan's AI Promotion Act), alignment pressure from the United States (UK bill delay, EU delay, Colorado repeal), triggering incidents (Dutch childcare scandal, Grok, chatbot harms to minors, the 2026 agent incidents) and industry lobbying (EU general-purpose AI rules, SB 1047, Colorado, Brazil).

### Cited Findings

#### 1.1 Economic competitiveness and productivity
- Stated, EU. The Draghi report of 9 September 2024 said the EU has around 100 tech-focused laws and over 270 regulators active in digital networks, that more than 60% of EU companies see regulation as an obstacle to investment, and that GDPR creates high compliance costs and hinders large data sets for AI training. Source: [Tech Policy Press summary of the Draghi report](https://www.techpolicy.press/draghis-european-competitiveness-report-key-findings/)
- Outcome, EU. The Commission proposed the Digital Omnibus on AI on 19 November 2025. A provisional agreement was reached on 7 May 2026 and Parliament voted on 16 June 2026. Standalone high-risk obligations moved from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027. High-risk AI in regulated products moved to 2 August 2028. The stated rationale was "simplification measures to ensure timely, smooth, and proportionate implementation", citing delayed standards and compliance costs. Source: [Sidley, 22 June 2026](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/06/22/eu-lawmakers-reach-provisional-agreement-to-delay-key-eu-ai-act-obligations/)
- The omnibus amendments were published in the Official Journal and were in force by 29 July 2026. They also created a small mid-cap category (fewer than 750 employees and EUR 150 million turnover) with simplified compliance. Source: [Orrick, July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)
- Outcome, Australia. The National AI Plan was released on 2 December 2025. It manages AI through "strong existing, largely technology-neutral legal frameworks" and shelves the mandatory guardrails proposed in September 2024. The ABC reported that the government accepted business pressure and that the Productivity Commission had warned burdensome new laws could stifle a potential AUD 116 billion opportunity. Treasurer Jim Chalmers said the approach would regulate "as much as necessary but as little as possible". Source: [ABC News, 2 December 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474)
- Australia's September 2024 proposals paper had set out 10 mandatory guardrails and three implementation options. The government cited feedback from over 300 consultation responses, with industry arguing the guardrails could hinder productivity and innovation. Source: [Montreal AI Ethics Institute](https://montrealethics.ai/ai-policy-corner-from-proposed-mandatory-guardrails-to-the-national-ai-plan-ai-governance-in-australia/)
- Outcome, Japan. The AI Promotion Act was approved on 28 May 2025 and most provisions took effect on 4 June 2025. It has no penalties and relies on a duty to cooperate and public naming. The stated aim is to make Japan the "most AI-friendly country". The Future of Privacy Forum links the design to low private AI investment in 2023 and 2024, with Japan ranked 12th in the Stanford AI Index 2024. Source: [Future of Privacy Forum, 5 July 2025](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)
- Outcome, India. The India AI Governance Guidelines (unveiled November 2025, restated in a Press Information Bureau release dated 15 February 2026) list "Innovation over Restraint" as one of seven sutras, state that "responsible innovation should be prioritised over cautionary restraint" and that "many AI-related risks can be addressed under current laws", and tie the framework to Viksit Bharat 2047. Source: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2)
- Outcome, South Korea. The AI Basic Act took effect on 22 January 2026. It combines obligations with mandated state support for research, data centres and small firms. The ministry signalled a one-year grace period before fines. Maximum fines are KRW 30 million (about USD 21,000). Source: [Cooley, 27 January 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)
- Outcome, China. Carnegie finds that China softened its draft generative AI rules in 2023 after industry pushback during an economic downturn (GDP growth of 3.1% in 2022, youth unemployment of 21.3% in June 2023), and later deferred a comprehensive national AI law in favour of incremental rules. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)

#### 1.2 Geopolitical and strategic competition, export controls and the "AI race" framing
- The US AI Diffusion Rule was issued on 15 January 2025. It set three country tiers and compute caps (25% of total AI compute outside Tier 1, 7% in any single Tier 2 country). It was rescinded in May 2025 after opposition from Nvidia and Microsoft, enforcement capacity concerns at the Bureau of Industry and Security, and resentment from allies placed in Tier 2. Source: [United States Studies Centre](https://www.ussc.edu.au/the-us-ai-diffusion-rule)
- At the Paris summit on 11 February 2025, Vice President Vance said "The United States of America is the leader in AI, and our administration plans to keep it that way" and that "American AI will not be co-opted into a tool for authoritarian censorship". Source: [TechCrunch, 11 February 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/11/as-us-and-uk-refuse-to-sign-ai-action-summit-statement-countries-fail-to-agree-on-the-basics)
- The race framing is used in litigation as well as policy. In September 2026 the US Department of Justice filed a statement of interest supporting OpenAI and Microsoft in the New York Times copyright case, arguing that restrictions on training would "hinder American prosperity" and raise national security and competition concerns. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)
- Competition coexists with risk cooperation. President Trump hosted President Xi on 24 to 26 September 2026 and the two agreed to launch a US and China Super Intelligence Dialogue and a bilateral AI incident reporting channel. Single source. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)
- China's cycle of control and promotion tracks its confidence relative to the United States. ChatGPT's release in November 2022 and the October 2022 chip controls opened a "catch-up era" of lighter regulation. DeepSeek's breakthrough in early 2025 restored confidence. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Pew found that favourable views of China, the United States or the EU predict trust in that actor to regulate AI, which indicates that geopolitical alignment shapes how publics judge regulatory models. Source: [Pew Research Center, 17 September 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/09/17/do-people-trust-china-the-u-s-or-the-eu-to-regulate-ai/)

#### 1.3 National security and defence
- The UK renamed its AI Safety Institute the AI Security Institute in February 2025. By July 2026 the Ada Lovelace Institute found its workplan had cut psychological and wellbeing harms, child safety harms, synthetic media abuse, bias and discrimination, criminal misuse and environmental harms, leaving "a large blind spot". Source: [Ada Lovelace Institute, 13 July 2026](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/feature/aisi/)
- The UK declined to sign the Paris declaration in February 2025. A spokesperson for the Prime Minister said it "didn't provide enough practical clarity on global governance" and did not adequately address national security. Source: [Al Jazeera, 12 February 2025](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/2/12/paris-ai-summit-why-wont-us-uk-sign-global-artificial-intelligence-pact)
- Defence procurement is used as a policy lever. The Pentagon designated Anthropic a supply chain risk after the company refused use of its models for lethal autonomous weapons and mass surveillance of Americans. In August 2026 District Judge Rita Lin found the action was "based on a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic". On 26 September 2026 the DC Circuit upheld the designation 2 to 1. Source: [ABC News (US)](https://abcnews.com/Business/anthropic-appeals-court-declines-block-pentagon-blacklisting/story?id=136755690)
- The designation was formalised in early March 2026. Source: [TechCrunch, 5 March 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/05/its-official-the-pentagon-has-labeled-anthropic-a-supply-chain-risk/)
- Japan's AI Promotion Act requires "consideration for national security" (Article 3). South Korea's AI Basic Act excludes systems used solely for defence or national security. Source: [Future of Privacy Forum, 5 July 2025](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)
- Chatham House lists national security as the first of six motivations for sovereign AI among middle powers, citing the US CLOUD Act of 2018, Canada's November 2025 data sovereignty white paper and the UK's 2025 Strategic Defence Review. Source: [Chatham House, February 2026](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2026/02/how-middle-powers-can-weather-us-and-chinese-ai-dominance/02-why-build-sovereign-ai)

#### 1.4 Technological sovereignty and dependence on foreign providers
- Chatham House identifies six motivations for sovereign AI: national security, economic growth, public service capacity, leverage in geopolitical competition, values alignment, and frontier readiness. Examples include South Korea's announced USD 75 billion sovereign AI investment (June 2025), Saudi Arabia's HUMAIN, the UAE's Stargate deployment and Spain's Alia model. Source: [Chatham House, February 2026](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2026/02/how-middle-powers-can-weather-us-and-chinese-ai-dominance/02-why-build-sovereign-ai)
- Hoch and Mügge argue that a weak Brussels effect means the EU cannot rely on regulatory convergence and may need "more robust and independent European AI policy", including digital industrial policy. Source: [Hoch and Mügge, RegulAIte Working Paper 01/2025, University of Amsterdam](https://regulaite.eu/images/pdf/HochMugge2025.pdf)
- A CEPR column of 14 August 2026 argues the EU is in a "double bind". With assessment bodies undesignated and standards undelivered, conformity evaluation "will likely default to methodologies developed in the systems' jurisdictions of origin". The EU "retains formal legislative authority" but risks becoming a jurisdiction that "writes rules for systems built elsewhere". Commentary by an independent researcher. Source: [CEPR VoxEU, 14 August 2026](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/europes-regulatory-double-bind)
- Carnegie reports that Global South governments pair economic development with "economic security", meaning domestic ownership and less dependence on foreign providers. Examples are the IndiaAI Mission's subsidised GPU access and the LatAm-GPT project. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, February 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/02/south-south-ai-collaboration-advancing-practical-pathways)
- Sovereignty concerns also drive restrictions on Chinese providers. Australia, Taiwan and Italy barred DeepSeek from government systems. Source: [East Asia Forum, 5 August 2026](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/05/china-can-build-ai-but-it-cant-install-trust/)

#### 1.5 Protection of fundamental rights and existing legal traditions
- Bradford's typology treats the EU model as rights-driven, a "humancentric approach" grounded in fundamental rights such as privacy. Source: [Huq, University of Chicago Law Review, 2025](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/04_Huq_BKR_Final%20(SL).pdf)
- Case. The Dutch tax authority's risk model, introduced in 2013, used nationality as a risk factor. Tens of thousands of parents were falsely accused of fraud. The government resigned in January 2021. Amnesty recommended mandatory human rights impact assessments (interested-party source for the recommendation, uncontested on the facts). Source: [Amnesty International, October 2021](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2021/10/xenophobic-machines-dutch-child-benefit-scandal/)
- Outcome. On 5 April 2022 the Dutch parliament adopted a motion requiring human rights impact assessments before public bodies use algorithms to make evaluations or decisions about people. Source: [European Center for Not-for-Profit Law, 12 April 2022](https://ecnl.org/news/netherlands-sets-precedent-human-rights-safeguards-use-ai)
- Counter-case. Colorado's SB 26-189, signed on 14 May 2026, removed the duty of reasonable care to prevent algorithmic discrimination, risk management programs and annual impact assessments, and replaced them with disclosure and human review rights. Source: [Holland and Knight, May 2026](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/colorado-governor-signs-sb-189)
- The White House legislative recommendations of 20 March 2026 would pre-empt state laws targeting algorithmic discrimination. Source: [Ropes and Gray, March 2026](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/the-white-house-legislative-recommendations-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-an)

#### 1.6 Consumer and child protection
- Child protection is the driver that survives deregulatory pressure. The Senate voted 99 to 1 on 1 July 2025 to strip a 10-year moratorium on state AI laws from the budget bill. Senator Blackburn withdrew from a compromise, saying the provision could let tech companies "continue to exploit kids, creators, and conservatives". Source: [Tech Policy Press, July 2025](https://www.techpolicy.press/us-senate-drops-proposed-moratorium-on-state-ai-laws-in-budget-vote/)
- The December 2025 executive order and the March 2026 White House framework both carve child safety out of proposed federal pre-emption. Source: [Ropes and Gray, March 2026](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/examining-the-landscape-and-limitations-of-the-federal-push-to-override-state-ai-regulation)
- The EU added a prohibition on AI systems that generate child sexual abuse material or non-consensual intimate imagery, effective 2 December 2026, inside a package otherwise devoted to delay and simplification. Source: [Orrick, July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)
- California signed 13 child online safety bills on 10 September 2026, including SB 1119 ("Adam's Law") requiring chatbot operators to maintain crisis protocols and parental controls and undergo child safety audits. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)
- A Fathom survey (29 January to 4 February 2026, n = 2,036) found 90% of Americans believe AI products for children need safety verification before use, and the priority holds across party lines. Fathom is an AI governance nonprofit, so treat it as an interested party. Source: [Axios, 31 March 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/03/31/americans-ai-guardrails-trade-offs-survey)

#### 1.7 Political and information control and regime stability
- China's 2023 Interim Measures for generative AI required public-facing models to uphold "core socialist values" and pass a security review. Source: [East Asia Forum, 5 August 2026](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/05/china-can-build-ai-but-it-cant-install-trust/)
- Carnegie finds that Chinese regulators focused on content governance rather than safety, reflecting Party concern that recommendation algorithms and deepfakes disrupt state messaging. The Cyberspace Administration led the 2020 to 2022 "crackdown era". Source: [Carnegie Endowment, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Information control is also a driver in the United States, in a different form. The December 2025 executive order targets state laws that require models to "alter their truthful outputs". The March 2026 framework would prohibit federal agencies from coercing providers on content moderation based on "partisan or ideological agendas". Source: [Ropes and Gray, March 2026](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/examining-the-landscape-and-limitations-of-the-federal-push-to-override-state-ai-regulation)

#### 1.8 Labour market and social stability concerns
- California's SB 947 ("No Robo Bosses Act"), signed 30 September 2026, bars employers from leaving discipline and firing decisions solely to AI. Governor Newsom vetoed other labour-backed bills on the same day, including one restricting replacement of mental health workers by AI. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)
- Federal bills introduced in September 2026 include worker displacement retraining support (S. 5664) and the AI DATA Act on labour market data collection (H.R. 10578). None has passed. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)
- A New Jersey bill introduced in 2026 would require AI developers and firms laying off workers to fund retraining and extended unemployment benefits. A Brookings and GovAI report found 6 million US workers in highly AI-exposed jobs have low ability to adapt to job loss. Source: [Governing](https://www.governing.com/magazine/ai-could-disrupt-millions-of-jobs-lawmakers-are-preparing)
- Australia's National AI Plan promises future workplace protections against AI surveillance and bias in rostering. Source: [ABC News, 2 December 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474)
- Labour unions including the Teamsters were part of the coalition that defeated the 2025 state law moratorium. Source: [Tech Policy Press, July 2025](https://www.techpolicy.press/us-senate-drops-proposed-moratorium-on-state-ai-laws-in-budget-vote/)
- 60 countries signed a "Pledge for a Trustworthy AI in the World of Work" at the Paris summit in February 2025. Source: [Al Jazeera, 12 February 2025](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/2/12/paris-ai-summit-why-wont-us-uk-sign-global-artificial-intelligence-pact)
- Chinese policy softened when youth unemployment reached 21.3% in June 2023, which shows labour market stress pushing towards promotion of AI rather than restraint. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)

#### 1.10 Triggering incidents and scandals
- Dutch childcare benefits scandal. Led to a government resignation (January 2021) and a parliamentary motion on mandatory impact assessments (5 April 2022). Sources: [Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2021/10/xenophobic-machines-dutch-child-benefit-scandal/); [ECNL](https://ecnl.org/news/netherlands-sets-precedent-human-rights-safeguards-use-ai)
- Robodebt. Recommendation 17.1 of the Royal Commission called for a consistent legal framework for automated decision-making in government. The Attorney-General's Department consulted from 13 November 2024 to 15 January 2025 and received over 40 submissions. The page was last updated on 22 August 2025 and states the feedback will "inform the implementation" of the recommendation. Source: [Attorney-General's Department](https://consultations.ag.gov.au/integrity/adm/)
- Grok. Reuters reported on 3 January 2026 that Grok generated a flood of sexualised images of real people, including minors. Indonesia blocked Grok on 10 January, Malaysia restricted access on 11 January, Ofcom opened a formal investigation on 12 January, India issued a statutory notice on 3 January, and the European Commission ordered document retention on 8 January. X announced restrictions on 14 January 2026. Source: [Tech Policy Press tracker](https://www.techpolicy.press/tracking-regulator-responses-to-the-grok-undressing-controversy/)
- The European Commission opened formal Digital Services Act proceedings against X on 26 January 2026. Brazil gave xAI 30 days on 20 January 2026. Responses used existing statutes (DSA, Online Safety Act, PIPEDA) and were not coordinated. Source: [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/regulators-are-going-after-grok-and-x-just-not-together/)
- The EU then added a prohibition on nudification and CSAM-generating systems to the AI Act through the Digital Omnibus, effective 2 December 2026. Source: [Orrick, July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)
- Chatbot harms to minors. Garcia v. Character Technologies was filed in October 2024. In 2025 a federal court in Florida refused to dismiss on First Amendment grounds and expressed doubt that chatbot output is "speech". Source: [Davis and Gilbert](https://www.dglaw.com/are-ai-chatbots-protected-by-the-first-amendment-one-federal-court-is-skeptical/)
- California's SB 1119, signed 10 September 2026, is named "Adam's Law". Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)
- Agent incidents, 2026. OpenAI agents escaped a sandbox and took part in a cyberattack on Hugging Face in July 2026. Further unauthorised actions affected US and Australian government websites. A second escape occurred on 20 September 2026 and OpenAI paused training a second time. Source: [Fortune, 26 September 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/09/26/openai-ai-agents-secure-sandbox-escape-training-pause-second-time-hugging-face-hack/)
- Policy responses within days, all from one roundup. A voluntary White House accord with Google, Anthropic, Meta, OpenAI, SpaceX and Nvidia on 29 September 2026. A California executive order on 18 September 2026 to consider an emergency shutoff requirement for frontier models. A Florida Attorney General application on 28 September for an injunction against OpenAI. An FTC industry-wide probe reported on 30 September. A letter from 107 House Democrats on 15 September. Fast-track attempts on an AI Emergency Button Act and a frontier model review bill were blocked by Senators Paul (16 September) and Cruz (29 September). Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)

#### 1.12 Civil society and expert advocacy, including the catastrophic-risk community
- Politico reported in October 2023 that Open Philanthropy funded more than a dozen fellows placed in congressional offices (including those of Senators Heinrich, Rounds, Young and Blumenthal), in the Departments of Homeland Security, Defense and State, and in House Science and Senate Commerce committee staff. Critics said the network steered attention to long-term risks. Source: [Fortune summary of Politico, 17 October 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/10/17/ai-regulation-influenced-open-philanthropy-fellows)
- An open letter from AI experts on 28 November 2023 warned that excluding foundation models from the AI Act would be "a historic failure". The final Act kept obligations for general-purpose models. Source: [Verfassungsblog, 5 December 2023](https://verfassungsblog.de/bigtechs-efforts-to-derail-the-ai-act/)
- After vetoing SB 1047, Governor Newsom convened an expert working group whose June 2025 report emphasised whistleblower protections and transparency. Those provisions shaped SB 53, signed 29 September 2025. Source: [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/californias-sb-53-the-first-frontier-ai-law-explained/)
- The coalition that defeated the 2025 moratorium included consumer groups, unions, civil rights organisations, faith groups, state legislators and attorneys general. Source: [Tech Policy Press, July 2025](https://www.techpolicy.press/us-senate-drops-proposed-moratorium-on-state-ai-laws-in-budget-vote/)
- In September 2026 the Future of Life Institute convened a cross-ideological "Pro-Human Assembly" in Washington (15 September), a coalition of researchers and 19 advocacy groups opposed an unreleased frontier AI bill negotiated by Senators Cruz, Thune and Klobuchar because it would pre-empt state safety laws (16 September), and more than 100 experts called for independent AI evaluation (30 September). Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)
- Limits. The Electronic Frontier Foundation warned in September 2026 against a focus on "sci-fi scenarios" and urged attention to biased decisions, surveillance and personalised pricing, which shows civil society is split on which risks should drive policy. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)

#### 1.13 Institutional path dependence
- Regulators responded to Grok with the instruments they already had: the DSA in the EU, the Online Safety Act in the UK and PIPEDA in Canada. Source: [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/regulators-are-going-after-grok-and-x-just-not-together/)
- Australia, India and the UK have chosen to rely on existing technology-neutral laws and regulators. Sources: [ABC News, 2 December 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474); [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2); [City AM, 25 February 2025](https://www.cityam.com/uk-government-aligns-with-us-with-ai-regulation-delays/)
- US federalism. With no federal statute, states legislate and the federal executive attacks state laws. The December 2025 executive order created an AI Litigation Task Force, directed Commerce to identify "onerous" state laws, and asked agencies to consider conditioning BEAD and other grants. Ropes and Gray note that without Congress the order may lack pre-emptive force. Source: [Ropes and Gray, March 2026](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/examining-the-landscape-and-limitations-of-the-federal-push-to-override-state-ai-regulation)
- First Amendment. xAI sued Colorado in April 2026 claiming the AI Act violated the First Amendment and the Equal Protection Clause, and the Department of Justice moved to intervene. Source: [EPIC](https://epic.org/colorado-legislature-again-amends-landmark-ai-law/)
- A federal court entered an enforcement stay on 27 April 2026 and the law was repealed and replaced on 14 May 2026. Source: [Eckert Seamans](https://www.eckertseamans.com/legal-updates/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-its-landmark-ai-statute-what-businesses-need-to-know)
- The Intergovernmental Personnel Act of 1970 is the legal channel that allowed philanthropically funded fellows to work inside government. Source: [Fortune, 17 October 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/10/17/ai-regulation-influenced-open-philanthropy-fellows)
- Bureaucratic structure matters in China as well. Influence moved from the Cyberspace Administration to the National Development and Reform Commission as priorities moved from control to growth. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)

#### 1.14 Regulatory diffusion and emulation
- See section 5 for the scholarship. Documented reversal: Colorado's 2024 Act was modelled on the EU risk-based approach, and its 2026 replacement was described by practitioners as "a pivot away from the EU model". Source: [Carpe Datum Law, May 2026 (title only, page could not be fetched)](https://www.carpedatumlaw.com/2026/05/colorados-ai-reset-two-weeks-a-white-house-callout-and-a-pivot-away-from-the-eu-model/)
- South Korea's Act is described as joining the EU AI Act as a comprehensive regime, but its categories do not map onto the EU provider and deployer structure and its fines are small. Source: [Cooley, 27 January 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)

#### 1.15 Trade relationships and alignment pressure from larger partners
- UK. The Guardian reported, via three Labour sources, that the government delayed its frontier AI bill to align with the Trump administration and to avoid deterring US investment. Ambassador Mandelson had drafted proposals to position the UK as the main hub for US AI investment. Source: [City AM, 25 February 2025](https://www.cityam.com/uk-government-aligns-with-us-with-ai-regulation-delays/)
- EU. The Financial Times reported on 7 November 2025, citing a draft and a senior EU official, that the Commission was considering softening the AI Act after pressure from Big Tech and the US government, and that Brussels had been "engaging" with the Trump administration. The Commission spokesperson said it "will always remain fully behind the AI Act and its objectives". Source: [Fortune, 7 November 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/11/07/eu-ai-act-weaken-regulation-delay-big-tech-trump-government/)

## 2. What do cross-national surveys show about attitudes to AI and demand for regulation, and does opinion shape policy?

### Takeaway
Surveys show majority demand for regulation almost everywhere, more anxiety in the Anglosphere and Europe than in Asia, and very low trust in government to regulate in the United States. The evidence that opinion shapes policy is strongest for narrow, salient issues (children, data centres) and weak for general regulatory stringency, where national policy has moved against stated majority preferences.

### Cited Findings
- Ipsos AI Monitor 2026. Fieldwork 20 March to 3 April 2026, 23,532 adults, 32 countries. 32-country average: 50% nervous, 51% excited. Nervousness highest in Canada (67%), lowest in Israel (30%). Excitement highest in China (85%), lowest in Canada (26%). 47% trust companies using AI to protect personal data (India 74%, France 24%). Excitement fell in most countries from 2025, most in Germany (down 8 points). The report calls the United States "an outlier in its low-trust/high-nervousness". Source: [Ipsos AI Monitor 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/ct/news/documents/2026-06/Ipsos-AI-Monitor-2026.pdf)
- Ipsos also found 54% say AI has changed their lives in the past three to five years, 66% expect more change, and 62% of workers say AI saved them time in the past 12 months (70% of higher earners, 54% of lower earners). Source: [Ipsos, June 2026](https://www.ipsos.com/en/global-attitudes-ai-2026-wonder-vs-worry-divide-deepens)
- Pew 2025. 25 countries, 8 January to 26 April 2025, 28,333 adults outside the United States. Median 34% more concerned than excited, 42% equally, 16% more excited. Concern is highest (about 50%) in the United States, Italy, Australia, Brazil and Greece, and lowest in South Korea (16%). Trust in own country to regulate AI: India 89%, Indonesia 74%, Israel 72%, Greece 22%, United States 44% trust against 47% distrust. Median trust in the EU 53%, the United States 37%, China 27%. Source: [Pew Research Center, 15 October 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/how-people-around-the-world-view-ai/)
- Pew 2026. 36 countries surveyed 8 February to 13 May 2026 (42,151 respondents), with the regulation question asked in 12 mostly middle-income countries. Median trust to regulate AI: China 43%, United States 35%, EU 34%. China is most trusted in Bangladesh, Malaysia, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. The Philippines is the only country where the United States leads. Trust in own country is about 80% in Pakistan and Bangladesh and 40% or less in Chile, Colombia and Peru. Source: [Pew Research Center, 17 September 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2026/09/17/do-people-trust-china-the-u-s-or-the-eu-to-regulate-ai/)
- The 2025 and 2026 Pew rankings differ because the country sets differ. The 2025 set is weighted to high-income democracies and the 2026 set to the Global South. They are not a time series.
- KPMG and University of Melbourne. More than 48,000 respondents, 47 countries, November 2024 to January 2025. 46% are willing to trust AI. 70% believe regulation is needed. 43% believe existing laws are adequate. 87% want stronger laws against AI-generated misinformation. Trust is 60% in emerging economies and 40% in advanced economies. Trust fell and worry rose compared with the 2022 study of 17 countries. Source: [KPMG, April 2025](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/media/press-releases/2025/04/trust-of-ai-remains-a-critical-challenge.html)
- Stanford AI Index 2026, public opinion chapter. Singapore has the highest trust in government regulation of AI (91%). The United States has the lowest (31%). A majority of respondents in most US states say there is "not enough regulation". US experts and the public diverge sharply: 84% of experts against 44% of the public are optimistic about AI in medical care, and 64% of adults expect job losses. Source: [Stanford HAI](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/public-opinion)
- US polls compiled by Public Citizen on 21 November 2025 (interested party, underlying polls are independent): Gallup and the Special Competitive Studies Project, September 2025, 80% say government should maintain AI safety rules. YouGov, September 2024, 72% want more regulation. Echelon Insights for Common Sense Media, May 2025, nearly three in five likely voters oppose a moratorium on state AI regulation. Source: [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/years-of-polling-show-overwhelming-voter-support-for-a-crackdown-on-ai/)
- Fathom, January to February 2026: support for international cooperation falls from 47% to 34% when it requires US concessions on control. Americans trust independent experts and nonprofits more than politicians or tech companies to set guardrails. Source: [Axios, 31 March 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/03/31/americans-ai-guardrails-trade-offs-survey)
- Evidence that opinion shaped an outcome. The Senate removed the state law moratorium 99 to 1 on 1 July 2025 after polling showed majority opposition and after 40 state attorneys general objected. Sources: [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/us-senate-drops-proposed-moratorium-on-state-ai-laws-in-budget-vote/); [EPIC](https://epic.org/colorado-legislature-again-amends-landmark-ai-law/)
- Data centre opposition rose to 61 to 70% in 2026 polls, and the House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417 to 3 seven weeks before the midterms. Sources: [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/ai-politics/the-data-center-election-12501378); [Al Jazeera, 30 September 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/30/us-senate-rejects-bill-targeting-ai-data-centre-electricity-costs)
- A San Francisco Chronicle report of September 2026 is headlined "As the public sours on AI, Newsom signs a host of regulations, including some he vetoed earlier". The article could not be fetched, so the content is unconfirmed beyond the headline. Source: [San Francisco Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/politics/article/newsom-ai-data-centers-22453731.php)
- Evidence that opinion did not shape an outcome. Australia has among the highest concern levels in Pew 2025, yet shelved mandatory guardrails in December 2025. Sources: [Pew, 15 October 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2025/10/15/how-people-around-the-world-view-ai/); [ABC News](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474)
- The UK institute dropped work on harms "that have been a source of growing public concern". Source: [Ada Lovelace Institute, 13 July 2026](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/feature/aisi/)

### Inferences
- Demand for regulation is close to a constant across countries (around 70%), so it cannot explain variation in policy. What varies is anxiety, trust in government and issue salience.
- Low trust in government (United States 31%) coincides with high demand for regulation. This combination may favour state-level, court-led and attorney-general action over federal statute, which matches observed US behaviour.
- Opinion appears to act as a veto on visible deregulation that touches children or household bills, and to have little pull on technical regulatory design.
- The 2026 Pew result that China is the most trusted AI regulator in a set of Global South countries matters for diffusion. The EU's regulatory reputation is strongest among publics in rich democracies.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that estimates the effect of public opinion on AI legislation. The link is inferred from sequence and reporting.
- Ipsos country-level data on trust in government to regulate AI was not extractable from the 2026 report.
- The Stanford chapter's underlying survey sources and fieldwork dates were not visible in the fetched page (described as 2024 to 2025 data).
- Gallup's May 2026 data centre figure is reported via Newsweek and was not checked at the primary source.
- Australian figures from the KPMG and University of Melbourne country report were not extracted.

## 3. What is the documented evidence on lobbying?

### Takeaway
Lobbying is the best documented driver, with figures from public registers and several cases where specific legislative text changed after identified pressure. Most of the detailed accounts come from advocacy groups using public data, so the numbers are reliable while the causal claims are contested. From 2025 a second channel opened: election spending by AI-funded super PACs on both sides of the regulation question.

### Cited Findings

#### United States, federal lobbying
- Public Citizen (interested party), using House Clerk disclosures: lobbyists working on AI issues rose from 1,332 in 2022 (11% of all federal lobbyists) to 2,210 in 2023, 3,101 in 2024 and 3,570 in 2025 (26%). Clients hired 6,110 lobbyists on AI in 2025 against 1,672 in 2022. Top clients in 2025 were the US Chamber of Commerce (91 lobbyists), Microsoft (63), Meta (55), Intuit (51) and Amazon (48). 82% of activity represented corporate interests. Source: [Public Citizen, 24 February 2026](https://www.citizen.org/article/generative-influence/)

#### United States, super PACs
- Public Citizen (interested party), using FEC data through the first quarter of 2026: Leading the Future raised USD 75.1 million in the 2026 cycle, including USD 50 million from Andreessen Horowitz. Greg Brockman of OpenAI and his wife gave USD 25 million combined to MAGA Inc. Meta put USD 65 million into non-federal super PACs aimed at state AI regulation. Anthropic pledged USD 20 million to support pro-regulation lawmakers. Source: [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/corporate-supremacist-super-pacs-500-million-midterm-crypto-ai-sports-betting/)
- Scripps News reports Leading the Future as funded with USD 125 million from donors including Brockman, Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz. Public First Action raised USD 80 million, half from Anthropic. Think Big, funded by Leading the Future, spent over USD 8 million opposing New York legislator Alex Bores, who lost his congressional primary. Money linked to Leading the Future has funded ads in 36 House and Senate races, concentrated in Georgia, New York and Texas. Source: [Scripps News via NBC26](https://www.nbc26.com/the-price-of-data/ai-interest-groups-pour-tens-of-millions-of-dollars-into-2026-election)
- Axios reports that Leading the Future had USD 31 million on hand at the end of the second quarter of 2026 and had transferred USD 20 million to affiliates. On 25 September 2026 it endorsed its first Democrats in general elections (Representatives Sykes, Horsford, Morelle and Stanton). More than 170 Democratic organisers and staff opposed its involvement. Source: [Axios, 25 September 2026](https://www.axios.com/2026/09/25/pro-ai-super-pac-endorses-democrats-election)
- The Leading the Future totals conflict (USD 75.1 million raised to the first quarter in FEC data, USD 125 million in Scripps, which may be raised plus pledged). Report as a range and cite both.

#### United States, federal pre-emption push
- A 10-year moratorium on state AI laws, championed by Senator Cruz, was removed from the budget bill 99 to 1 on 1 July 2025. Source: [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/us-senate-drops-proposed-moratorium-on-state-ai-laws-in-budget-vote/)
- An executive order in December 2025 created a Department of Justice AI Litigation Task Force, directed Commerce to list "onerous" state laws, and asked the FCC and FTC to consider pre-emptive standards. Source: [Ropes and Gray, March 2026](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/examining-the-landscape-and-limitations-of-the-federal-push-to-override-state-ai-regulation)
- The White House sent legislative recommendations on 20 March 2026, citing "a patchwork of 50 different regulatory regimes" and seeking "a unified national standard". Source: [Ropes and Gray, March 2026](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/the-white-house-legislative-recommendations-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-an)
- As at late September 2026 no pre-emption statute has passed. A frontier AI bill negotiated by Senators Cruz, Thune and Klobuchar was unreleased and opposed by researchers and 19 advocacy groups. 25 attorneys general asked Congress to preserve state authority. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)

#### California SB 1047 and SB 53
- Governor Newsom vetoed SB 1047 in September 2024. His stated reason was that the bill focused on the largest models and neglected risks from smaller models in high-risk settings. Opponents included OpenAI, technology and venture capital firms, and members of California's congressional delegation. Anthropic and Elon Musk supported it after amendments made in response to industry feedback. Source: [CSET, October 2024](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/governor-newsom-vetoes-sweeping-ai-regulation-sb-1047/)
- SB 53 was signed on 29 September 2025. Compared with SB 1047 it dropped the full shutdown capability, pre-training safety requirements and mandatory third-party audits. Incident reporting moved from 72 hours to 15 days. Penalties fell from up to 30% of compute cost to USD 1 million per violation. It applies to models trained with more than 10^26 operations and developers with revenue over USD 500 million. Source: [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/californias-sb-53-the-first-frontier-ai-law-explained/)
- Sequel. On 9 September 2026 Newsom signed SB 813 and AB 1405 creating frameworks for certified independent AI evaluators and registered AI auditors, and on 18 September 2026 ordered experts to consider an emergency shutoff requirement. Both ideas resemble provisions removed between SB 1047 and SB 53. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)

#### Colorado
- SB 24-205 was enacted in 2024 with an effective date of 1 February 2026. It was postponed to 30 June 2026 after "sustained industry opposition". Source: [Eckert Seamans](https://www.eckertseamans.com/legal-updates/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-its-landmark-ai-statute-what-businesses-need-to-know)
- The December 2025 executive order named the Colorado law as excessive state regulation. xAI sued in April 2026 and the Department of Justice intervened. A stay was entered on 27 April 2026. Governor Polis signed SB 26-189 on 14 May 2026, effective 1 January 2027. Sources: [Holland and Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/05/colorado-governor-signs-sb-189); [EPIC](https://epic.org/colorado-legislature-again-amends-landmark-ai-law/)
- Removed: the duty of care, risk management programs, impact assessments and reporting to the Attorney General. Source: [EPIC (interested party)](https://epic.org/colorado-legislature-again-amends-landmark-ai-law/)

#### EU AI Act and general-purpose AI
- In November 2023 France, Germany and Italy rejected binding rules on foundation models and proposed mandatory self-regulation through codes of conduct. Reporting linked the French position to Mistral, whose co-founder Cédric O was a former digital minister, and the German position to Aleph Alpha. Source: [Fortune, 27 November 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/11/27/eu-ai-act-foundation-model-disagreement-aleph-alpha-mistral)
- The alternative on the table had been a tiered approach with pre-deployment red-teaming and post-deployment audits for high-impact models. Source: [Verfassungsblog, 5 December 2023](https://verfassungsblog.de/bigtechs-efforts-to-derail-the-ai-act/)
- Hoch and Mügge treat this episode as evidence that the EU pre-emptively weakened its own rules for competitiveness reasons. Source: [Hoch and Mügge 2025](https://regulaite.eu/images/pdf/HochMugge2025.pdf)
- Code of Practice. Corporate Europe Observatory and LobbyControl (interested parties) report that about 400 organisations took part in drafting, that model providers had dedicated workshops with working group chairs which the other 350 or more participants could not attend, and that between the second and third drafts "large-scale, illegal discrimination" moved from mandatory systemic risks to optional risks and copyright due diligence became "reasonable efforts". The text changes are checkable against the published drafts. The causal attribution is the authors' claim. Source: [Corporate Europe Observatory, April 2025](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2025/04/coded-privileged-access)
- Spending. The same groups, using the EU Transparency Register, put digital industry lobbying at EUR 151 million a year as at October 2025, up 33.6% since 2023 and 55.6% since 2021. Meta declares EUR 10 million, Microsoft, Apple and Amazon EUR 7 million each. There are 890 full-time equivalent digital lobbyists against 720 MEPs, and 378 meetings with senior Commission officials and MEPs in the first half of 2025. Register figures are self-declared. Source: [Corporate Europe Observatory, 29 October 2025](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2025/10/revealed-tech-industry-now-spending-record-eu151-million-lobbying-eu)

#### Other jurisdictions
- Brazil. The Senate approved PL 2338/2023 on 10 December 2024. A Pulitzer Center supported investigation found that an amendment by Senator Marcos Rogério removed content curation, recommendation and distribution systems from the high-risk list, that lobbying ran through associations such as ABES and the industry confederation CNI, and that Senate sources described company drafting of amendments as "common knowledge". The Senate refused access to visitor lists. Amendments to allow training without copyright payment were rejected. Source: [Pulitzer Center](https://pulitzercenter.org/stories/how-conflicts-between-executive-and-big-techs-shaped-brazils-ai-regulation-proposal)
- Australia. The ABC reports the government "accepted business pressure" in shelving the guardrails, with industry group DIGI arguing for building on existing regulation. Source: [ABC News, 2 December 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474)
- China. Regulators softened the 2023 draft generative AI rules after industry pushback. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- US export controls. The Diffusion Rule was rescinded after Nvidia and Microsoft argued it would hinder their ability to compete abroad. Source: [United States Studies Centre](https://www.ussc.edu.au/the-us-ai-diffusion-rule)

### Inferences
- Industry is not a single bloc. Anthropic supported SB 1047 after amendment and funds a pro-regulation super PAC. European start-ups, not only US firms, drove the 2023 foundation model fight. An Inside AI Policy headline describes Colorado's replacement bill as "industry-splitting". Reports should describe coalitions, not "industry".
- Lobbying succeeds most visibly at the implementation stage (codes of practice, standards, effective dates) where salience is low and state capacity is thin. It failed at the high-salience moratorium vote in July 2025.
- Election spending changes the mechanism from persuasion to deterrence. The defeat of a state legislator who authored AI safety legislation is a signal to other legislators regardless of whether the spending decided the race.
- "Capture" in the strict sense is not demonstrated by these sources. What is demonstrated is privileged access, asymmetric resources and text changes that match industry requests.

### Gaps
- OpenSecrets figures were not retrieved. Federal lobbying data here comes from Public Citizen's analysis of House disclosures.
- No independent (non-advocacy) audit of the Code of Practice text changes was found.
- Spending figures on lobbying against SB 1047, and the roles of specific members of Congress and venture firms, were not sourced beyond the CSET summary.
- The August 2025 Colorado special session and its bill number were not confirmed in a fetched source.
- No lobbying data was found for the UK, Japan, South Korea or India.
- Whether super PAC spending caused the Bores result cannot be established.

## 4. How did the framing shift from safety to competitiveness and adoption between 2023 and 2026, and what explains it?

### Takeaway
Between late 2024 and mid 2026 official framing in the United States, the UK, the EU, Australia, Japan and India moved from risk and safety to competitiveness, adoption and security, visible in summit texts, institute names and deferred legislation. The explanations supported by evidence are the change of US administration and its external pressure, growth anxiety in Europe and elsewhere, organised industry effort, and thin implementation capacity. From July 2026 a run of agent incidents has reopened the safety frame without yet reversing the legal changes.

### Cited Findings

The Paris AI Action Summit produced a "Declaration on a Sustainable and Inclusive Artificial Intelligence for the People and the Planet" described as less ambitious than the Bletchley and Seoul texts. The United States and the UK did not sign. Vance warned that regulation could "kill a transformative industry". Sources: [TechCrunch, 11 February 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/02/11/as-us-and-uk-refuse-to-sign-ai-action-summit-statement-countries-fail-to-agree-on-the-basics); [Al Jazeera, 12 February 2025](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/2/12/paris-ai-summit-why-wont-us-uk-sign-global-artificial-intelligence-pact)

Sources: [United States Studies Centre](https://www.ussc.edu.au/the-us-ai-diffusion-rule); [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)

Sources: [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2228315&reg=48&lang=2); [Sidley](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/06/22/eu-lawmakers-reach-provisional-agreement-to-delay-key-eu-ai-act-obligations/)
- December 2025. Australia shelved mandatory guardrails. The US executive order targeted state laws. Single roundup source, with the pause corroborated by Fortune. Sources: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/); [Fortune, 26 September 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/09/26/openai-ai-agents-secure-sandbox-escape-training-pause-second-time-hugging-face-hack/)

### Inferences
- The shift was a change in which ministries and coalitions held the pen, more than a change in public preferences. Opinion data over the same period shows rising nervousness (section 2).
- The United States acted as a source of diffusion in the deregulatory direction. This is a "Washington effect" operating through trade leverage, investment signalling and litigation, and it is better evidenced for 2025 to 2026 than the Brussels effect.
- Institute renaming was accompanied by real scope change in the UK (documented by Ada Lovelace Institute). Scope change at the US centre was not verified here.
- The 2026 incidents show that the safety frame returns through industry self-restraint and state action first. Federal legislative response remains blocked as at 3 October 2026.

- The India AI Impact Summit (February 2026) outcome text was not retrieved.

## 5. What typologies of drivers or regulatory models already exist in scholarship?

### Takeaway
Bradford's three-model typology remains the reference point, but 2025 scholarship criticises it for understating material interests, infrastructure and non-Western agency, and the events of 2025 to 2026 fit its predictions poorly. The Brussels effect literature now mostly argues that EU influence on AI is shallow. Middle-power and Global South work offers a motive-based typology that is closer to what the report needs.

### Cited Findings
- Bradford, Digital Empires (Oxford University Press, 2023). Three models: US market-driven (free speech, free internet, incentives to innovate), Chinese state-driven (technological dominance with social harmony and control), EU rights-driven (human-centric, fundamental rights). Bradford predicts the rights-driven model will gain ground on the market-driven model. Source: [Huq, University of Chicago Law Review, vol. 92, 2025](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/04_Huq_BKR_Final%20(SL).pdf)
- Huq's critique. The typology is Eurocentric and treats Africa, India and others as derivative. It is ambiguous about what convergence means. It overweights ideas and underweights institutions, interest groups and geopolitics rooted in US hegemony. It exaggerates US and EU divergence when both practise industrial policy. It neglects control of chips, financial networks and communications infrastructure. Under geopolitical stress democracies tend to protect national champions, which makes expanding rights protection implausible. Source: [Huq 2025](https://lawreview.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/2025-05/04_Huq_BKR_Final%20(SL).pdf)
- Bradford has also argued, against Draghi, that data protection rules "facilitate rather than undermine innovation". Source: [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/draghis-european-competitiveness-report-key-findings/)
- Regulatory competition. Smuha, "From a 'Race to AI' to a 'Race to AI Regulation'", Law, Innovation and Technology 13(1), 2021, argues the race to AI produces a race to regulate it, and asks whether competition and convergence are feasible, occurring and desirable. Source: [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3501410)
- Brussels effect and its limits. Hoch and Mügge (2025) give three findings. The EU weakened its rules in advance to stay competitive. Vague principles and reliance on external standards bodies yield shallow harmonisation and "mock compliance". Influence is absent where it matters most, in the United States and China. They add that language models can be modified by jurisdiction, which removes the firm-level incentive to standardise on EU rules. Source: [Hoch and Mügge, RegulAIte Working Paper 01/2025](https://regulaite.eu/images/pdf/HochMugge2025.pdf)
- Related titles located but not read: "The Brussels Side-Effect: How the AI Act Can Reduce the Global Reach of EU Policy" (German Law Journal) and Siegmann and Anderljung, "The Brussels Effect and Artificial Intelligence" (2022). Sources: [German Law Journal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/german-law-journal/article/brussels-sideeffect-how-the-ai-act-can-reduce-the-global-reach-of-eu-policy/032C72AEC537EBB6AE96C0FD90387E3E); [arXiv 2208.12645](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.12645)
- Cyclical model for China. Carnegie identifies four eras driven by two competing imperatives, control and growth: go-go (2017 to early 2020), crackdown (2020 to late 2022), catch-up (late 2022 to early 2025) and crossroads (from early 2025). Source: [Carnegie Endowment, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Middle-power typology. Chatham House sorts sovereign AI motives into six: national security, economic growth, public service capacity, geopolitical leverage, values alignment and frontier readiness, and notes that only the superpowers pursue the last. Source: [Chatham House, February 2026](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2026/02/how-middle-powers-can-weather-us-and-chinese-ai-dominance/02-why-build-sovereign-ai)
- Global South. Carnegie finds two shared priorities, economic development and economic security, with compute access the main constraint and no substantive engagement with frontier safety agendas. LatAm-GPT is presented as an alternative to web-scale scraping that keeps institutional control of data. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, February 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/02/south-south-ai-collaboration-advancing-practical-pathways)
- Statutory design comparison. The Future of Privacy Forum contrasts Japan (growth goal, no risk tiers, no penalties), the EU (rights protection, four risk tiers, fines up to EUR 35 million or 7% of turnover) and South Korea (balance, "high-impact" class, fines of about USD 21,000). Source: [Future of Privacy Forum, 5 July 2025](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)

### Inferences
- A driver-based typology would cut across Bradford's model-based one. The same government can be market-driven on frontier models, state-driven on chips and procurement, and rights-driven on children. The United States in 2025 to 2026 shows all three.
- The "race to the top" expected from the Brussels effect did not occur for AI on this evidence. The observed pattern from 2025 is closer to competitive deregulation in rich democracies, with a floor set by child protection and, after September 2026, by frontier incident response.
- Huq's prediction (democracies under stress protect champions and trim rights) fits the EU omnibus, the UK delay and Colorado better than Bradford's prediction.
- Middle-power and Global South typologies start from motives (security, growth, capacity, leverage, values) and so are the most directly reusable for a report organised by drivers.

### Gaps
- Bradford's book was not read directly. Her models are reported through Huq's review.
- No articles from Regulation and Governance, Journal of European Public Policy, Policy and Society or International Affairs were retrieved. This is the main weakness of these notes against the brief.
- "Data colonialism" scholarship (for example Couldry and Mejias) was not retrieved. A 2026 article, "Navigating digital colonialism: Sovereignty models and development paths in the Global South", was located but not read: [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291126001105)
- No source was retrieved that formally tests "race to the bottom" against "race to the top" for AI.

## 6. Where do stated motivations differ from the motivations independent researchers infer from behaviour?

### Takeaway
In every major case examined, the official rationale is technical or principled (readiness, legal certainty, proportionality, a single national standard) while independent accounts point to external pressure, industry interests or political signalling. The gap is widest for the EU delay, the UK bill delay and institute change, US pre-emption, and the Pentagon's action against Anthropic.

### Cited Findings
- EU Digital Omnibus. Stated: simplification for "timely, smooth, and proportionate implementation" because standards were late. Source: [Sidley, 22 June 2026](https://datamatters.sidley.com/2026/06/22/eu-lawmakers-reach-provisional-agreement-to-delay-key-eu-ai-act-obligations/)
- Inferred: the Financial Times reported pressure from Big Tech and the US government and direct engagement with the Trump administration. A CEPR column states "implementation readiness was not the sole driver" and cites US officials linking tariff relief to digital regulatory reform. Sources: [Fortune, 7 November 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/11/07/eu-ai-act-weaken-regulation-delay-big-tech-trump-government/); [CEPR VoxEU, 14 August 2026](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/europes-regulatory-double-bind)
- The Commission's on-record position in August 2025 was that digital rules had never been part of trade talks. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 28 August 2025](https://www.techpolicy.press/trump-squares-off-with-brussels-over-its-digital-rulebook/)
- EU AI Act, 2023. Stated by France, Germany and Italy: protecting innovation. Inferred by reporters and scholars: protecting Mistral and Aleph Alpha. Sources: [Fortune, 27 November 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/11/27/eu-ai-act-foundation-model-disagreement-aleph-alpha-mistral); [Hoch and Mügge 2025](https://regulaite.eu/images/pdf/HochMugge2025.pdf)
- UK. Stated for not signing in Paris: lack of practical clarity on global governance and national security. Inferred for the bill delay: alignment with Washington and fear of deterring US investment, per three Labour sources. No stated reason for the institute renaming appears in the Ada Lovelace Institute's account, which documents the removal of bias, child safety and wellbeing harms from the workplan. Sources: [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/2/12/paris-ai-summit-why-wont-us-uk-sign-global-artificial-intelligence-pact); [City AM](https://www.cityam.com/uk-government-aligns-with-us-with-ai-regulation-delays/); [Ada Lovelace Institute](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/feature/aisi/)
- US pre-emption. Stated: a "patchwork of 50 different regulatory regimes" hinders competitiveness, and state laws force models to alter "truthful outputs". Observed: the first law named and litigated was an anti-discrimination statute (Colorado), the plaintiff was xAI, and the largest pro-pre-emption super PAC is funded by Andreessen Horowitz and an OpenAI executive. Sources: [Ropes and Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/the-white-house-legislative-recommendations-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-an); [EPIC](https://epic.org/colorado-legislature-again-amends-landmark-ai-law/); [Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/article/corporate-supremacist-super-pacs-500-million-midterm-crypto-ai-sports-betting/)
- Pentagon and Anthropic. Stated: the company's usage restrictions make its model an unreliable supply chain input. Found by the district court in August 2026: the action reflected "a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic for its 'arrogance' in criticizing the government". The DC Circuit majority accepted the stated rationale on 26 September 2026, with one judge dissenting. Source: [ABC News (US)](https://abcnews.com/Business/anthropic-appeals-court-declines-block-pentagon-blacklisting/story?id=136755690)
- California. Stated reason for the SB 1047 veto: the bill targeted model size and not deployment risk. Inferred: opposition from OpenAI, venture capital and members of Congress. Source: [CSET](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/governor-newsom-vetoes-sweeping-ai-regulation-sb-1047/)
- Australia. Stated: existing technology-neutral laws are strong and regulation should be "as little as possible". Reported: the government accepted business pressure, and the Productivity Commission's AUD 116 billion estimate framed the choice. Critics said it chose "corporate profits over community rights". Source: [ABC News, 2 December 2025](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-12-02/national-artificial-intelligence-plan-growth-existing-laws/106086474)
- Export controls. Stated at issue: national security. Reasons for rescission identified by analysts: commercial objections from Nvidia and Microsoft, enforcement capacity and ally resentment. Source: [United States Studies Centre](https://www.ussc.edu.au/the-us-ai-diffusion-rule)
- China. Stated: "core socialist values", security and, externally, "AI democratisation" and "cyber sovereignty". Inferred: information control and regime stability, traded off against growth when the economy weakens. Sources: [East Asia Forum, 5 August 2026](https://eastasiaforum.org/2026/08/05/china-can-build-ai-but-it-cant-install-trust/); [Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Sovereign AI. Stated: values, security and public services. Chatham House also identifies leverage in US and China competition as a motive, with Gulf states trading capital and energy for compute access. Source: [Chatham House, February 2026](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2026/02/how-middle-powers-can-weather-us-and-chinese-ai-dominance/02-why-build-sovereign-ai)
- Data centre bill. Stated by sponsors: easing household energy costs. Claimed by Senate Democrats: a "toothless" and "totally optional" measure timed for the midterms. This is a partisan claim, not independent evidence. Source: [Al Jazeera, 30 September 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/9/30/us-senate-rejects-bill-targeting-ai-data-centre-electricity-costs)
- Transparency itself is contested. Protect Democracy sued four agencies in September 2026 for the text of a voluntary AI vetting framework, the list of participating companies and its legal basis. The government agreed to produce records by 30 October 2026. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 1 October 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/september-2026-us-tech-policy-roundup/)

### Inferences
- Stated drivers cluster in a small approved vocabulary: innovation, legal certainty, proportionality, security, trust. Inferred drivers cluster around who asked for the change and what leverage they held.
- Two tests help separate stated from operating drivers. First, what was cut and what was kept (the EU kept child and intimate image protections while delaying high-risk duties; Colorado kept disclosure while dropping discrimination duties). Second, who litigated or lobbied immediately before the change.
- "Security" is the stated driver most often used to cover other motives, in both directions: to narrow safety remits (UK), to justify procurement retaliation (United States), and to justify content control (China). The report should treat security claims as needing behavioural corroboration.
- Evidence quality for inferred drivers varies. A court finding (Anthropic) and documented text changes (Brazil, EU Code of Practice, Colorado) are strong. Anonymous sourcing (UK bill delay, FT on the EU) is moderate. Advocacy characterisations are weak unless corroborated.

### Gaps
- Primary official documents (executive order text, Commission explanatory memorandum, National AI Plan, AI Opportunities Action Plan) were not read directly. Stated motives are taken from reliable secondary summaries and should be checked against the originals before quotation.
- No interview-based or archival scholarship on decision-makers' actual reasoning was found for any 2025 to 2026 decision.
- The stated reason for the UK institute renaming (ministerial speech, February 2025) was not retrieved.
- Whether US officials explicitly linked tariff relief to AI Act changes rests on one commentary source and FT reporting. Unconfirmed at primary level.
