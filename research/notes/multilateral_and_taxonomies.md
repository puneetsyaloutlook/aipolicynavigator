# Multilateral AI governance instruments and comparative taxonomies of national AI policy (as at October 2026)

Reader note on method. Research was done on 3 October 2026. Every finding below carries a link. Most pages were read through an automated page reader that summarises content, so exact wording and exact counts should be checked against the linked primary source before being quoted verbatim. Where sources conflict, both figures are given. Items recalled from background knowledge and not re-verified in this session are confined to the Gaps lists and marked "unconfirmed in this session". Each instrument is recorded in two parts where evidence allowed: (1) the official or institutional account, and (2) observed behaviour of states and firms.

## 1. What do the main multilateral instruments contain, and who has adopted them?

### Takeaway
Almost every multilateral AI instrument in force at October 2026 is voluntary and non-binding, and the instruments overlap heavily in content: a values list (rights, fairness, transparency, safety, accountability) plus an enabling list (R&D, skills, infrastructure, cooperation). The only binding treaty is the Council of Europe Framework Convention. Between 2023 and 2026 the summit series moved from "safety" (Bletchley) to "action" (Paris) to "impact" (New Delhi), and by mid 2026 three competing institutional tracks were visible: the UN Panel and Dialogue, the US-led Pax Silica, and the China-led World AI Cooperation Organization.

### Cited Findings

#### OECD AI Principles and the OECD definition of an AI system
Official account:
- The OECD Recommendation on AI was adopted in May 2019 and updated in May 2024. It has five values-based principles: (1) inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being; (2) human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy; (3) transparency and explainability; (4) robustness, security and safety; (5) accountability. [OECD.AI principles overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)
- It has five recommendations for policymakers: (1) investing in AI research and development; (2) fostering an inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem; (3) shaping an enabling interoperable governance and policy environment for AI; (4) building human capacity and preparing for labour market transition; (5) international cooperation for trustworthy AI. [OECD.AI principles overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)
- The OECD definition of an AI system reads: "a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments." [OECD.AI principles overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)
- OECD.AI lists 47 adherents (countries and entities) as at the date of access, 3 October 2026. [OECD.AI principles overview](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)
Observed behaviour:
- The G20 adopted non-binding G20 AI Principles at Osaka in 2019 that were drawn from the OECD Recommendation, which extended the OECD text to non-OECD members including China, India and Russia. [CAIDP G20 resource page](https://www.caidp.org/resources/g20/)
- The OECD national policy database held over 800 AI policy initiatives from 69 countries plus the EU at the date of access, which shows the Principles are used as an organising frame for national reporting. [OECD.AI survey guidance](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-survey-guidance)
- The IAPP tracker (January 2026) observes that most jurisdictions start with a national strategy or ethics policy and not legislation, and that jurisdictions coordinate through UNESCO, the OECD and the Council of Europe. [IAPP Global AI Law and Policy Tracker PDF](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltd4dd5b2d705252bc/blt34a8e3844fb44942/global_ai_law_policy_tracker.pdf)

#### UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (2021)
Official account:
- Adopted in November 2021 (23 November 2021) by UNESCO's 193 member states. UNESCO describes it as the first global standard on AI ethics. [UNESCO Recommendation page](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics); [UNESCO UK key facts PDF](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/14137/unesco_recommendation_on_the_ethics_of_artificial_intelligence_-_key_facts.pdf)
- Four values: human rights and human dignity; living in peaceful, just and interconnected societies; ensuring diversity and inclusiveness; environment and ecosystem flourishing. [UNESCO Recommendation page](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics)
- Ten principles: proportionality and do no harm; safety and security; right to privacy and data protection; multi-stakeholder and adaptive governance and collaboration; responsibility and accountability; transparency and explainability; human oversight and determination; sustainability; awareness and literacy; fairness and non-discrimination. [UNESCO Recommendation page](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics)
- It has eleven policy action areas and two implementation tools, the Readiness Assessment Methodology (RAM) and the Ethical Impact Assessment (EIA). [UNESCO UK key facts PDF](https://unesco.org.uk/site/assets/files/14137/unesco_recommendation_on_the_ethics_of_artificial_intelligence_-_key_facts.pdf)
Observed behaviour:
- UNESCO's observatory reports that 55 national reports have been produced through the RAM (date of access 3 October 2026). That is roughly 28 per cent of the 193 adopting states five years after adoption. [UNESCO Global AI Ethics and Governance Observatory](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en)

#### G7 Hiroshima AI Process: guiding principles, code of conduct, reporting framework
Official account:
- The Hiroshima AI Process began in 2023 under the Japanese G7 presidency and produced International Guiding Principles and an International Code of Conduct for Organisations Developing Advanced AI Systems. Japan's process site records these as agreed by G7 digital and tech ministers and endorsed by leaders in December 2023. [Japan MIC Hiroshima AI Process site](https://www.soumu.go.jp/hiroshimaaiprocess/en/index.html)
- The OECD-hosted voluntary reporting framework for the code of conduct launched on 7 February 2025. [OECD.AI HAIP reporting framework overview](https://oecd.ai/en/transparency/overview)
- Version 2.0 of the reporting framework, described as streamlined to help small and medium enterprises take part, was launched in May 2026 at a Tech7 event during the G7 Digital and Tech Ministerial under the French presidency. It is open to a wider range of organisations that develop, deploy or provide advanced AI systems. [OECD press release, May 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2026/05/oecd-launches-streamlined-hiroshima-ai-process-reporting-framework-to-help-small-and-medium-sized-enterprises-participate.html); [OECD.AI HAIP overview](https://oecd.ai/en/transparency/overview)
- The submission deadline for inclusion in the next OECD analytical review was 30 September 2026. [OECD.AI HAIP overview](https://oecd.ai/en/transparency/overview)
Observed behaviour:
- On 24 April 2025 the OECD published the first submissions from 19 organisations: AI21 Labs, Anthropic, Data Privacy and AI, Fayston Preparatory School, Fujitsu, Google, KDDI, KYP.ai, MGOIT, Microsoft, NEC, NTT, OpenAI, Palo Alto Research/West Lake, Preferred Networks, Rakuten, Salesforce, SoftBank and TELUS. [OECD.AI blog, initial submissions](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/initial-submissions-g7-hiroshima-ai-process-reporting-framework)
- The OECD overview page states that the first round resulted in 25 reports. The two figures (19 and 25) differ, probably because of later additions to the first cycle. [OECD.AI HAIP overview](https://oecd.ai/en/transparency/overview)
- Participation is concentrated in Japanese and US firms. Several large frontier developers (for example Meta, Amazon, xAI, and all Chinese developers) do not appear in the 19 names published in April 2025. [OECD.AI blog, initial submissions](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/initial-submissions-g7-hiroshima-ai-process-reporting-framework)
- The Hiroshima Process Friends Group continued to add members in 2026, including the Republic of Moldova in August 2026 and firms such as Cohere. [Japan MIC Hiroshima AI Process site](https://www.soumu.go.jp/hiroshimaaiprocess/en/index.html)

#### G20
Official account:
- 2019 Osaka: non-binding G20 AI Principles drawn from the OECD Recommendation, with a "human-centred approach to AI". 2020 Riyadh and 2021 Rome reaffirmed and advanced implementation. 2023 New Delhi leaders' declaration carried AI provisions (paragraph 61). 2024 Brazil focused its digital economy working group on AI for sustainable development and reduction of inequality. 2025 South Africa set up a Task Force on AI, Data Governance and Innovation for Sustainable Development and launched an AI for Africa initiative. [CAIDP G20 resource page](https://www.caidp.org/resources/g20/); [G20 Task Force chair's statement, 30 September 2025](https://www.g20.utoronto.ca/2025/250930-ai-taskforce.html)
Observed behaviour:
- The 2025 task force outcome was issued as a chair's statement and not as an agreed ministerial declaration, which indicates lack of full consensus. [G20 Task Force chair's statement](https://www.g20.utoronto.ca/2025/250930-ai-taskforce.html)
- The Atlantic Council argued in 2025 that the G20 was moving forward on AI governance and that the United States risked being left out. This is analyst opinion and is recorded as such. [Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/new-atlanticist/the-g20-is-moving-forward-on-global-ai-governance-and-the-us-risks-being-left-out/)

#### UN: Global Digital Compact, Scientific Panel, Global Dialogue
Official account:
- The Global Digital Compact (opened for endorsement September 2024) commits under its AI objective to establish an international scientific panel on AI, establish a global AI policy dialogue, develop AI capacity-building partnerships, and consider options for a Global Fund on AI. A high-level review is scheduled for Q3 2027. [UN Global Digital Compact page](https://www.un.org/global-digital-compact/en)
- General Assembly resolution A/RES/79/325, adopted by consensus on 26 August 2025, established the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and the Global Dialogue on AI Governance. [UN Panel FAQ](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en/faq); [UN Global Dialogue FAQ](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq)
- The Panel has 40 members (19 women, 21 men) from all five UN regions, serving in a personal capacity. They were selected from more than 2,600 applications from over 140 countries and appointed by the General Assembly in February 2026. At the first meeting on 3 March 2026 the Panel elected Yoshua Bengio (Canada) and Maria Ressa (Philippines) as co-chairs. [UN Panel FAQ](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en/faq)
- The Panel issued its Preliminary Report on 1 July 2026. Its central warning is that current safeguards cannot keep pace with the growth of AI capabilities. It covers seven domains: AI science, advances and trajectories; societal applications; economic implications; security, systems and environmental implications; human rights, information and democracy; cultural flourishing, autonomy and child safety; management, governance and reliability. [UN Panel Preliminary Report page](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en/preliminary-report)
- The first Global Dialogue was held in Geneva on 6 and 7 July 2026 with over 3,000 participants from 163 countries and 59 side events. Co-chairs were Egriselda López (El Salvador) and Rein Tammsaar (Estonia). It used four thematic clusters: AI opportunities and implications; bridging AI divides; safe, secure and trustworthy AI and interoperability; human rights, transparency, accountability and oversight. The second Dialogue is set for 3 and 4 May 2027 in New York. [UN Global Dialogue FAQ](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq)
- The Dialogue's mandate resolution lists seven thematic areas: safe AI, capacity-building, social, economic and ethical implications, governance interoperability, human rights protection, transparency and oversight, and open-source initiatives. [UN Global Dialogue FAQ](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq)
Observed behaviour:
- The Dialogue is explicitly "not a negotiating forum". Its output was a co-chairs' summary of convergences and not an agreed text. [UN Global Dialogue FAQ](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq)
- CSIS (2026) describes the United States as having a "substantial absence" from UN AI dialogue processes. Carnegie records the US stating at New Delhi its "strong disagreement with any mechanism aimed at global governance of AI". [CSIS, State of AI Global Governance](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit); [Carnegie, Perspectives from India's AI Impact Summit, 30 April 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)
- The New Delhi Declaration (February 2026) made no reference to the UN Scientific Panel or the forthcoming Geneva Dialogue. [DFRLab, 4 March 2026](https://dfrlab.org/2026/03/04/what-the-ai-impact-summit-reveals/)

#### AI summit series
Bletchley Park, 1 and 2 November 2023 (AI Safety Summit):
- The Bletchley Declaration was agreed by 28 countries plus the EU: Australia, Brazil, Canada, Chile, China, France, Germany, India, Indonesia, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Kenya, Saudi Arabia, Netherlands, Nigeria, Philippines, Republic of Korea, Rwanda, Singapore, Spain, Switzerland, Türkiye, Ukraine, UAE, UK, US, and the EU. New Zealand joined in October 2024. [GOV.UK Bletchley Declaration](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-safety-summit-2023-the-bletchley-declaration/the-bletchley-declaration-by-countries-attending-the-ai-safety-summit-1-2-november-2023)
- Content: focus on "frontier AI"; recognition of "potential for serious, even catastrophic, harm"; commitment to "risk-based policies" suited to national circumstances with a "pro-innovation and proportionate governance and regulatory approach"; developers bear a "particularly strong responsibility" for safety testing and evaluation; an internationally inclusive network of scientific research on frontier AI safety. China and the US both signed. [GOV.UK Bletchley Declaration](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-safety-summit-2023-the-bletchley-declaration/the-bletchley-declaration-by-countries-attending-the-ai-safety-summit-1-2-november-2023)
Seoul, 21 and 22 May 2024 (AI Seoul Summit):
- The leaders' Seoul Declaration was adopted by only 11 parties: the EU, US, UK, Australia, Canada, Germany, France, Italy, Japan, Republic of Korea and Singapore. The broader Seoul Ministerial Statement was signed by 27 countries plus the EU. China signed neither according to the lists reproduced. (Source is Wikipedia, so treat as a lead and confirm against the Korean or UK government pages.) [Wikipedia, AI Seoul Summit 2024](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_Seoul_Summit_2024)
- Sixteen companies agreed to the Frontier AI Safety Commitments at Seoul, and four more joined later. [METR, Common Elements of Frontier AI Safety Policies, December 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-12-09-common-elements-of-frontier-ai-safety-policies/)
- CSIS describes Seoul as broadening the frame to "safety, innovation, and inclusivity". [CSIS, State of AI Global Governance](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)
Paris, 10 and 11 February 2025 (AI Action Summit):
- More than 1,000 participants from more than 100 countries. The "Statement on Inclusive and Sustainable Artificial Intelligence for People and the Planet" was signed by 61 countries and regional blocs according to CSIS (the EPC gives 60). China and India signed. The United States and the United Kingdom declined. [CSIS, France's AI Action Summit](https://www.csis.org/analysis/frances-ai-action-summit); [EPC, The Paris Summit](https://www.epc.eu/publication/The-Paris-Summit-Au-Revoir-global-AI-Safety-61ea68/); [Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/science-technology/ai-action-summit-india-china-over-50-other-countries-sign-declaration-on-inclusive-sustainable-ai)
- Stated reasons: the UK cited insufficient "practical clarity on global governance" and national security concerns. The US objected to the emphasis on multilateralism, inclusivity and environmental language. [CSIS, France's AI Action Summit](https://www.csis.org/analysis/frances-ai-action-summit)
- Content: digital divide, labour market impacts, environmental sustainability and international cooperation. Deliverables included a public interest AI platform and observatories on work. The EPC notes the statement contained "no substantial commitments to AI safety". [CSIS, France's AI Action Summit](https://www.csis.org/analysis/frances-ai-action-summit); [EPC](https://www.epc.eu/publication/The-Paris-Summit-Au-Revoir-global-AI-Safety-61ea68/)
- Investment announcements dominated: about EUR 109 billion in private AI infrastructure investment in France and an EU AI Champions pledge of EUR 150 billion. Sources differ on the EU public component (CSIS: InvestAI EUR 50 billion; EPC: EUR 58 billion). [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/frances-ai-action-summit); [EPC](https://www.epc.eu/publication/The-Paris-Summit-Au-Revoir-global-AI-Safety-61ea68/)
New Delhi, 16 to 20 February 2026, leaders' segment 18 and 19 February (India AI Impact Summit):
- The New Delhi Declaration on AI Impact was endorsed by 88 countries and international organisations as at 21 February 2026 and by 91 as at 24 February 2026 after Bangladesh, Costa Rica and Guatemala joined (Government of India figures). Other counts in circulation: 89 (DFRLab, 4 March 2026) and 92 (Brookings; Carnegie, 30 April 2026). The differences reflect late endorsements and whether organisations are counted. [PIB, Government of India, 24 February 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2232005&reg=3&lang=1); [DFRLab](https://dfrlab.org/2026/03/04/what-the-ai-impact-summit-reveals/); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/takeaways-from-the-india-ai-impact-summit/)
- Unlike Paris, the major powers all signed: the US, China, the UK, Russia, all G7 members and the EU. [Carnegie, 30 April 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/takeaways-from-the-india-ai-impact-summit/)
- Content: three organising themes (People, Planet, Progress) and seven working groups or "chakras": democratising AI resources (democratic diffusion); economic growth and social good (Global AI Impact Commons); secure and trusted AI (Trusted AI Commons); science (International Network of AI for Science Institutions); access for social empowerment; human capital; resilience, innovation and efficiency. All pledges are framed as voluntary and non-binding, with language such as "taking note of" and "recognising". [Drishti IAS summary of the declaration](https://www.drishtiias.com/daily-updates/daily-news-analysis/new-delhi-declaration-on-ai-impact); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)
- Carnegie's word count of "safety" in the four declarations: Bletchley 15, Seoul 14, Paris 6, New Delhi 0. New Delhi used "secure and trusted AI" instead. [Carnegie, 30 April 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)
- Brookings reads the same text less starkly: the declaration says "secure, trustworthy and robust AI is foundational to building trust and maximizing societal and economic benefits", so safety is repositioned and not abandoned. [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/takeaways-from-the-india-ai-impact-summit/)
- Company side: 13 model developers (including Amazon, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic and Sarvam AI) signed "New Delhi Frontier AI Impact Commitments" with two commitments, transparency on real-world AI usage and multilingual testing. A Charter for Democratic Diffusion of AI was endorsed by 25 countries and organisations. More than USD 200 billion in investment announcements were made. [Carnegie, 30 April 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)
- The US used the summit to announce an American AI Exports Program. [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/takeaways-from-the-india-ai-impact-summit/)
- Next hosts: Switzerland (Geneva) in 2027, then the UAE in 2028. [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/takeaways-from-the-india-ai-impact-summit/); [Wikipedia, India AI Impact Summit 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India_AI_Impact_Summit_2026)

#### International Network of AI Safety Institutes
Official account:
- The network was launched in November 2024. Founding members listed by the Stanford AI Index 2025: Australia, Canada, the EU, France, Japan, Kenya, Republic of Korea, Singapore, the UK and the US (chair). [Stanford AI Index 2025, chapter 6](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai_ai-index-report-2025_chapter6_final.pdf)
- On 9 December 2025 the network was renamed the International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science. The stated reason was a "re-focus" on long-term priorities around testing and evaluation methods. The UK AI Security Institute acts as coordinator. [MLex, December 2025](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2419745)
Observed behaviour:
- The word "safety" has been removed from the UK institute (AI Security Institute, February 2025), the US institute (Center for AI Standards and Innovation, June 2025) and the network itself (December 2025). [Wikipedia, AI safety institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_safety_institute); [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2419745)
- CSIS (2026) reports that the network "continues to function" but that political momentum has moved away from safety as the organising principle. [CSIS, State of AI Global Governance](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)
- China is not a member of the network in any list found. [Stanford AI Index 2025, chapter 6](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai_ai-index-report-2025_chapter6_final.pdf)

#### International AI Safety Report
- The second edition, the International AI Safety Report 2026, was published on 3 February 2026, chaired by Yoshua Bengio, written by over 100 experts, with an Expert Advisory Panel nominated by more than 30 countries and international organisations. It is organised around three questions: what general-purpose AI can do, what risks it poses, and what risk management approaches exist. Risks are grouped as malicious use, malfunctions, and systemic risks. It was timed to inform the New Delhi summit. [Inside Privacy (Covington), February 2026](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/international-ai-safety-report-2026-examines-ai-capabilities-risks-and-safeguards/); [Report PDF](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/international-ai-safety-report-2026.pdf)
- Observed behaviour: the 2025 edition was released before Paris, yet the Paris statement contained no substantial safety commitments. The 2026 edition was presented at New Delhi, where the declaration did not use the word "safety". [EPC](https://www.epc.eu/publication/The-Paris-Summit-Au-Revoir-global-AI-Safety-61ea68/); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)
- Bengio now co-chairs both this report and the UN Scientific Panel, which creates two parallel scientific assessment tracks with the same lead. [UN Panel FAQ](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en/faq)

#### ISO/IEC 42001 and related standards
- ISO/IEC 42001 is the AI management system (AIMS) standard against which organisations can be certified. ISO/IEC 42006, published July 2025, sets requirements for bodies that audit and certify AI management systems under ISO/IEC 42001, and works with ISO/IEC 17021-1. [ISO, ISO/IEC 42006:2025](https://www.iso.org/standard/42006)
- Observed behaviour: Roberts et al. (International Affairs, 2024) note that ISO, IEC, IEEE and ITU are all active in AI standards, which "enables companies to 'forum shop'". [Roberts et al., preprint](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf)

#### Council of Europe Framework Convention (added because it is the only binding multilateral instrument)
- The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (CETS 225) is supported by HUDERIA, a risk and impact assessment methodology. The EU ratified on 15 May 2026. Moldova, Canada and Israel reported ratification work under way. As at June 2026 the Council's steering committee was still working "with a view to achieving early entry into force", so entry into force had not been confirmed. [Council of Europe CDNET report, 8 June 2026](https://rm.coe.int/cdnet-2026-r1-en/48802c5989)

#### ASEAN Guide on AI Governance and Ethics
Official account:
- The ASEAN Guide (2024) sets seven guiding principles: transparency and explainability; fairness and equity; security and safety; human-centricity; privacy and data governance; accountability and integrity; robustness and reliability. [Expanded ASEAN Guide, Generative AI](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Expanded-ASEAN-Guide-on-AI-Governance-and-Ethics-Generative-AI.pdf)
- The Expanded Guide on generative AI (published on asean.org in January 2025) lists six generative AI risks (mistakes and anthropomorphism; factual inaccuracy and disinformation; deepfakes, impersonation and fraud; intellectual property infringement; privacy and confidentiality; embedded bias) and nine policy dimensions: accountability; data; trusted development and deployment; incident reporting; testing and assurance; security; content provenance; safety and alignment R&D; AI for public good. [Expanded ASEAN Guide](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Expanded-ASEAN-Guide-on-AI-Governance-and-Ethics-Generative-AI.pdf)
- It is explicitly voluntary: member states "are recommended to apply, on a voluntary basis" and nothing in it changes legal obligations. [Expanded ASEAN Guide](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Expanded-ASEAN-Guide-on-AI-Governance-and-Ethics-Generative-AI.pdf)
- The nine dimensions closely match Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI, which the IAPP tracker lists as a leading voluntary framework. [IAPP tracker PDF](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltd4dd5b2d705252bc/blt34a8e3844fb44942/global_ai_law_policy_tracker.pdf)

#### China: Global AI Governance Initiative, Action Plan and the World AI Cooperation Organization
Official account:
- China issued the Global AI Governance Initiative in 2023 (declaratory principles), sponsored a UN General Assembly resolution on AI capacity-building in 2024, announced a 13-point Global AI Governance Action Plan on 26 July 2025, and proposed a world AI cooperation organisation in 2025. [CSIS, State of AI Global Governance](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit); [Stanford AI Index 2026, chapter 8](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)
- The World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization (WAICO) was founded in Shanghai on 16 July 2026 with 29 founding members. It is an intergovernmental body outside the UN system headquartered in Shanghai. Commitments announced include 5,000 AI training places for developing countries over five years, regional cooperation centres with ASEAN, the Arab League, the African Union, CELAC, the SCO and BRICS, and deployment of the "Mazu" early-warning system in 30 countries. [Caixin Global, 17 July 2026](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-07-17/china-launches-shanghai-based-ai-governance-body-with-29-founding-nations-102465524.html); [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)
- Named founding members across sources: China, Russia, Kazakhstan, Pakistan, Indonesia, Laos, Brazil, Venezuela, Ethiopia and Cameroon. [Caixin Global](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-07-17/china-launches-shanghai-based-ai-governance-body-with-29-founding-nations-102465524.html); [CGTN, 18 July 2026](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2026-07-18/29-countries-join-World-AI-Cooperation-Organization-in-Shanghai-1OSeZzywfx6/p.html)
Observed behaviour:
- CSIS describes WAICO's membership as overwhelmingly low and middle income states in the Global South, and contrasts it with the US-led Pax Silica (launched 11 December 2025 with 11 signatories, 24 by June 2026, mostly high-income US security partners). Kazakhstan is the only state identified as joining both (Pax Silica in June 2026, WAICO in July 2026). [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)
- CSIS notes China has domestic regulatory frameworks to offer abroad while the US lacks "a coherent national set of regulations" to export. [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)

### Inferences
- Status spectrum for the theme set. Binding treaty: Council of Europe convention only. Intergovernmental soft law: OECD, UNESCO, G20, GDC, ASEAN Guide. Political declarations: the four summit texts. Firm-facing voluntary codes: Hiroshima code of conduct, Seoul frontier commitments, New Delhi frontier impact commitments. Technical standards: ISO/IEC 42001 family. Scientific assessment: International AI Safety Report and the UN Panel. Any comparison of national policy should record which of these a jurisdiction has joined as a distinct "international alignment" theme.
- Signing patterns are a usable indicator of national posture. Useful markers: signed Bletchley (2023), signed Seoul leaders' declaration (2024), signed Paris (2025), signed New Delhi (2026), OECD adherent, HAIP Friends Group, AISI network member, CoE convention signatory or ratifier, Pax Silica, WAICO.
- The breadth of signatures rose (29, then 11 at leaders' level in Seoul, then about 61, then about 90) as the specificity and safety content of the texts fell. The widest text (New Delhi) is the least demanding.
- By mid 2026 the landscape has three institutional poles (UN, US-led, China-led) plus the summit series and the OECD/G7 track. This supports treating "geopolitical alignment" as a driver of national policy separate from the content themes.

## 2. Which principles and policy elements recur across the instruments, and what empirical studies have measured convergence?

### Takeaway
Empirical studies from 2019 onward find strong convergence on a small core (transparency, fairness, safety or non-maleficence, accountability, privacy), with human oversight and sustainability appearing less often. The same studies warn that agreement on words hides disagreement on meaning, priority and implementation. The intergovernmental instruments reviewed in section 1 show the same core plus a second, enabling layer (R&D, skills, infrastructure, international cooperation) that the ethics-guideline studies do not capture.

### Cited Findings
- Jobin, Ienca and Vayena (Nature Machine Intelligence, 2019) analysed 84 AI ethics documents, 88 per cent of them published after 2016. Eleven principle clusters with document counts: transparency 73 of 84; justice and fairness 68; non-maleficence 60; responsibility 60; privacy 47; beneficence 41; freedom and autonomy 34; trust 28; sustainability 14; dignity 13; solidarity 6. [Jobin et al., arXiv version](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)
- Jobin et al. conclude that convergence is emerging around five principles (transparency, justice and fairness, non-maleficence, responsibility, privacy) but with substantive divergence on "how ethical principles are interpreted; why they are deemed important; what issue, domain or actors they pertain to; and how they should be implemented". [Jobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)
- Jobin et al. on who writes the principles: USA 20 documents (23.8 per cent), UK 14 (16.7 per cent), Japan 4; no independent representation from Africa, South America or Central Asia. Issuers: private companies 22.6 per cent, government agencies 21.4 per cent, academic institutions 10.7 per cent, intergovernmental organisations 9.5 per cent. [Jobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)
- Fjeld et al. (Berkman Klein Center, 2020) analysed 36 prominent principles documents and found eight themes with coverage: fairness and non-discrimination 100 per cent; privacy 97 per cent; accountability 97 per cent; transparency and explainability 94 per cent; safety and security 81 per cent; professional responsibility 78 per cent; human control of technology 69 per cent; promotion of human values 69 per cent. 64 per cent of documents referenced human rights and five adopted international human rights law as their framework. [Fjeld et al., ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339138141_Principled_Artificial_Intelligence_Mapping_Consensus_in_Ethical_and_Rights-Based_Approaches_to_Principles_for_AI)
- Fjeld et al. identify a "normative core" and note that "more recent documents tend to cover all eight of these themes", while cautioning that principles must be read in cultural, linguistic, geographic and organisational context and are insufficient without policies, regulation and professional practice. [Fjeld et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339138141_Principled_Artificial_Intelligence_Mapping_Consensus_in_Ethical_and_Rights-Based_Approaches_to_Principles_for_AI)
- Corrêa et al. (Patterns, vol 4 issue 10, October 2023), "Worldwide AI ethics", reviewed 200 guidelines and governance policies from public bodies, academia, companies and civil society and identified "at least 17 resonating principles". An open database accompanies the paper. [Corrêa et al., arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.11922)
- Principles recurring across the intergovernmental texts read for section 1:
  - Transparency and explainability: OECD principle 3; UNESCO principle 6; ASEAN principle 1; Fjeld 94 per cent; Jobin 73 of 84. [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles); [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics); [ASEAN](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Expanded-ASEAN-Guide-on-AI-Governance-and-Ethics-Generative-AI.pdf)
  - Fairness and non-discrimination: OECD principle 2; UNESCO principle 10; ASEAN principle 2; G20 2019. [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles); [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics); [CAIDP G20](https://www.caidp.org/resources/g20/)
  - Robustness, safety and security: OECD principle 4; UNESCO principle 2; ASEAN principles 3 and 7; Bletchley Declaration. [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles); [GOV.UK Bletchley](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-safety-summit-2023-the-bletchley-declaration/the-bletchley-declaration-by-countries-attending-the-ai-safety-summit-1-2-november-2023)
  - Accountability and responsibility: OECD principle 5; UNESCO principle 5; ASEAN principle 6. [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles); [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics)
  - Privacy and data governance: OECD principle 2; UNESCO principle 3; ASEAN principle 5. [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles); [ASEAN](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Expanded-ASEAN-Guide-on-AI-Governance-and-Ethics-Generative-AI.pdf)
  - Human rights, human-centricity and human oversight: OECD principle 2; UNESCO value 1 and principle 7; ASEAN principle 4; G20 "human-centred" framing; the Council of Europe convention in binding form. [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics); [CoE CDNET report](https://rm.coe.int/cdnet-2026-r1-en/48802c5989)
  - Sustainability and environment: OECD principle 1; UNESCO value 4 and principle 8; Paris statement; New Delhi "resilience, innovation and efficiency" pillar. Low in Jobin (14 of 84). [UNESCO](https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics); [CSIS Paris](https://www.csis.org/analysis/frances-ai-action-summit); [Jobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)
  - Inclusion, capacity-building and bridging divides: OECD principle 1 and recommendation 4; UNESCO value 3 and principle 9; UN Dialogue cluster 2; Paris statement; New Delhi pillars 1, 5 and 6. [UN Global Dialogue FAQ](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq); [Drishti IAS](https://www.drishtiias.com/daily-updates/daily-news-analysis/new-delhi-declaration-on-ai-impact)
  - Enabling policy (R&D investment, ecosystem and infrastructure, skills and labour transition, international cooperation, interoperable governance): OECD recommendations 1 to 5; UN Dialogue themes; New Delhi pillars. [OECD.AI](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)
- Operational governance mechanisms that recur in the newer, firm-facing instruments: risk assessment, vulnerability management, governance frameworks and incident reporting (HAIP reporting areas); capability thresholds, model evaluations, model weight security, deployment safeguards, halting conditions and accountability mechanisms (frontier safety policies); incident reporting, testing and assurance, content provenance (ASEAN expanded guide). [OECD.AI blog](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/initial-submissions-g7-hiroshima-ai-process-reporting-framework); [METR, December 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-12-09-common-elements-of-frontier-ai-safety-policies/); [ASEAN](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Expanded-ASEAN-Guide-on-AI-Governance-and-Ethics-Generative-AI.pdf)

### Inferences
- A defensible common theme set has two layers. Layer A (normative core, measured by Jobin, Fjeld and Corrêa and present in OECD, UNESCO, ASEAN and G20): transparency and explainability; fairness and non-discrimination; safety, security and robustness; accountability and liability; privacy and data governance; human oversight and human rights; sustainability. Layer B (enabling and structural policy, present in the OECD recommendations, the UN Dialogue themes and the Paris and New Delhi texts): R&D and public investment; compute and infrastructure; skills and labour transition; inclusion and access; international cooperation and interoperability; institutional arrangements.
- A third layer has emerged since 2023 and is absent from the 2019 and 2020 studies: frontier or general-purpose model governance (evaluations, thresholds, incident reporting, content provenance, model security) and sovereignty (compute, data localisation, national models). The theme set should include these explicitly or it will be anchored to a pre-generative-AI consensus.
- The principle studies sampled documents mostly from the US, UK and EU and mostly before 2020. "Convergence" in those studies is convergence among a narrow set of issuers.
- Recurrence of a word across instruments is weak evidence of shared policy. Carnegie's word count on "safety" shows how quickly a term can be dropped from a consensus text while the underlying concept is relabelled.

### Gaps
- Corrêa et al.'s list of 17 principles with frequencies was not retrieved (PubMed Central blocked). The open database linked from the arXiv record should hold it.
- No study was found that quantitatively codes the intergovernmental instruments of 2023 to 2026 (summit declarations, GDC, HAIP code, ASEAN Guide) against a common principle list. The crosswalk above is the researcher's own reading.
- Mittelstadt (2019), "Principles alone cannot guarantee ethical AI", and Hagendorff (2020) are standard citations on the limits of principlism but were not read in this session.

## 3. What taxonomies do existing comparative trackers and studies use to classify national AI policy?

### Takeaway
Existing taxonomies classify along different axes that should not be merged: by instrument type (OECD.AI), by legal status and approach (IAPP), by governance content tags (AGORA), by risk domain, actor and lifecycle (MIT and CSET mapping), by measurable activity (Stanford AI Index), by regulatory design dimensions (CEIMIA) and by political-economic model (Bradford). A sound theme set should state which axis each theme sits on.

### Cited Findings

#### OECD.AI policy observatory (national AI policies database)
- The database groups 25 policy instrument types into four categories: (1) Governance; (2) Financial support; (3) AI enablers and other incentives; (4) Guidance and regulation. Example instrument types include "national strategies, agendas and plans" and "emerging AI-related regulation". [OECD.AI survey guidance](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-survey-guidance)
- The survey has three themes: AI governance; national AI policies; regulatory and legislative initiatives to promote trustworthy AI. Each initiative can be mapped to the OECD AI Principles and to policy areas. [OECD.AI survey guidance](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-survey-guidance)
- Scale at date of access: over 800 initiatives from 69 countries plus the EU, supplied by national contact points. [OECD.AI survey guidance](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-survey-guidance)

#### Stanford HAI AI Index, policy and governance chapter
- AI Index 2025 (chapter 6) measures: AI-related laws passed (114 to 116 geographic areas), mentions of AI in legislative proceedings (75 areas), US federal and state legislation, US federal regulations and issuing agencies, deepfake laws, AI safety institutes, and public investment (contracts and grants). [Stanford AI Index 2025, chapter 6](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/hai_ai-index-report-2025_chapter6_final.pdf)
- AI Index 2026 (chapter 8) is organised around: national AI strategies; AI sovereignty (infrastructure, data, models, applications, talent); legislative activity; congressional witness testimony; US federal regulations; public investment. It also carries a timeline of major policy events for 2025. Data sources include Oxford Insights, Epoch AI, Digital Policy Alert, Brookings, and procurement databases. [Stanford AI Index 2026, chapter 8](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)
- The 2026 edition adds "AI sovereignty" as a measured dimension, with indicators such as state-backed GPU clusters by region and cumulative data localisation measures. [Stanford AI Index 2026, chapter 8](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)

#### IAPP Global AI Law and Policy Tracker
- Last updated January 2026. It covers 34 jurisdictions across six continents. Each jurisdiction is described under four headings: specific AI governance law or policy; relevant authorities; other relevant laws and policies; wider AI context. [IAPP tracker PDF](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltd4dd5b2d705252bc/blt34a8e3844fb44942/global_ai_law_policy_tracker.pdf)
- Its framing of approaches: comprehensive legislation (EU AI Act, risk-based; South Korea's AI Basic Act); use-case or sector-specific regulation (China); voluntary frameworks (Australia's Voluntary AI Safety Standard, Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework); national strategy combined with ethics guidance (most jurisdictions). It states "there is no standard approach". [IAPP tracker PDF](https://assets.contentstack.io/v3/assets/bltd4dd5b2d705252bc/blt34a8e3844fb44942/global_ai_law_policy_tracker.pdf)

#### AGORA (AI Governance and Regulatory Archive), CSET and the Emerging Technology Observatory
- AGORA codes documents with 77 thematic tags in five domains: (1) risk factors governed, adapted from the NIST AI Risk Management Framework; (2) harms addressed, based on CSET's AI incident harm categories; (3) governance strategies; (4) incentives for compliance; (5) application domains. Example tags: bias (risk factor); harm to physical health or safety (harm); evaluation, disclosure, auditing (strategies); access to business opportunities, subsidies, fines (incentives); transportation, agriculture, government military (applications). [ETO AGORA dataset documentation](https://eto.tech/dataset-docs/agora-dataset/); [Arnold, Schiff et al., Introducing AGORA](https://danielschiff.com/papers/introducing-agora.pdf)
- Coding density: median 8 codes per instrument, mean 10.2, range 0 to 43. [Introducing AGORA](https://danielschiff.com/papers/introducing-agora.pdf)
- Coverage caveat stated by the maintainers: the dataset "skews toward U.S. law and policy"; US federal documents since 2020 are collected systematically, state and non-US documents ad hoc. Dataset version cited in 2026 is 1.32.0. [ETO AGORA dataset documentation](https://eto.tech/dataset-docs/agora-dataset/)

#### MIT AI Risk Initiative and CSET, "Mapping the AI Governance Landscape" (October 2025, updated April 2026)
- Applies several taxonomies to AGORA documents (950 plus in the pilot, over 1,000 in the 9 April 2026 update): AI risk domain (MIT AI Risk Taxonomy, 24 subdomains); AI actors by role (proposer, target, enforcer, monitor; developers, deployers, governance bodies, users, infrastructure providers, affected stakeholders); AI lifecycle stage (OECD and NIST definitions); legislative status (hard law, soft law); sectors governed (NAICS); AI system technical scope. [MIT AI Risk, pilot test and update](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-pilot-test-and-update); [CSET, April 2026 update](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-april-2026-update/)
- Findings: most covered risk subdomains are governance failure, AI system security vulnerabilities and attacks, and lack of capability or robustness. Least covered are AI welfare and rights, multi-agent risks, and economic and cultural devaluation of human effort. Most covered sectors are public administration, scientific R&D and national security. Least covered are accommodation and food services, arts and recreation, and real estate. [MIT AI Risk, pilot test and update](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-pilot-test-and-update)

#### CEIMIA, "A Comparative Framework for AI Regulatory Policy" (Roberts, Ziosi, Osborne, Saouma; February 2023)
- Compares Canada, China, the EU, the UK and the US on seven dimensions: definition of AI; key aims; scope and focal areas; approach to risk; regulatory requirements; monitoring and enforcement; flexibility and revisions. Presented as a heuristic that can be extended to more jurisdictions. [CEIMIA framework PDF](https://ceimia.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/a-comparative-framework-for-ai-regulatory-policy.pdf)

#### Bradford, Digital Empires (Oxford University Press, 2023)
- Three models of digital regulation: the American market-driven model (free markets, free speech, innovation incentives, limited state oversight); the Chinese state-driven model (state authority over platforms, technology in service of political control and stability, fast legislation and enforcement); the European rights-driven model (individual rights and democratic safeguards, asymmetric obligations on large firms, for example GDPR, DMA, DSA). [Tech Policy Press interview with Bradford](https://www.techpolicy.press/digital-empires-a-conversation-with-anu-bradford/); [OUP book page](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/digital-empires-9780197649268?cc=us&lang=en)
- Bradford frames two contests: a "horizontal" battle between states (chiefly US and China) and a "vertical" battle between governments and technology companies. She treats AI and semiconductors as central to the horizontal battle. [Tech Policy Press interview](https://www.techpolicy.press/digital-empires-a-conversation-with-anu-bradford/)

#### Other comparative framings found
- Roberts, Hine, Taddeo and Floridi (International Affairs, 2024) characterise the three main approaches as "the EU foregrounding new regulations, the US taking a more laissez-faire approach, and China relying on a hybrid approach". [Roberts et al., preprint](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf)
- UNESCO's Readiness Assessment Methodology is itself a comparative instrument, producing country reports on preparedness to implement AI ethically (55 produced). [UNESCO observatory](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en)
- Oxford Insights' Government AI Readiness Index 2025 assesses 195 governments. Its method was revised for 2025 to recognise that governments also "enable AI adoption across sectors, promote innovation, and safeguard against risks", not only use AI in public services. [Oxford Insights, Government AI Readiness Index 2025](https://oxfordinsights.com/ai-readiness/government-ai-readiness-index-2025/)
- Brookings (Engler, 2023) compares the EU and US approaches along risk management lines. Title and existence confirmed in search results only; content not read. [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-eu-and-us-diverge-on-ai-regulation-a-transatlantic-comparison-and-steps-to-alignment/)

### Inferences
- Suggested crosswalk of axes for the report's theme set:
  - Axis 1, regulatory model or posture (Bradford; Roberts et al.; IAPP): market-driven, state-driven, rights-driven; and within that comprehensive horizontal law, sector or use-case rules, voluntary guidance, strategy only.
  - Axis 2, instrument type (OECD.AI): governance; financial support; enablers and incentives; guidance and regulation.
  - Axis 3, regulatory design (CEIMIA): definition, aims, scope, approach to risk, requirements, enforcement, flexibility.
  - Axis 4, substantive content (AGORA; MIT risk domains; the principle studies): risk factors, harms, governance strategies, compliance incentives, application domains.
  - Axis 5, capacity and sovereignty (Stanford 2026; Oxford Insights): strategy, public investment, compute, data, talent.
  - Axis 6, international alignment (section 1): which instruments a jurisdiction has joined.
- The labels "risk-based", "rights-based", "principles-based" and "sector-led" used in the brief are in common use but no single authoritative source that defines all of them as a closed typology was found in this session. IAPP uses "risk-based" for the EU AI Act and "voluntary" for Australia and Singapore; Bradford supplies "market", "state" and "rights". The report should say that these labels are a synthesis, and that real jurisdictions mix them (for example China combines binding use-case rules with state-led industrial policy; the UK is principles-based and regulator-led).
- CSET and MIT's finding that public administration and national security are the most governed sectors, and that "governance failure" is the most addressed risk, suggests that national AI policy is largely government governing its own use and process. A theme on "government use of AI" should be separate from "regulation of private actors".

### Gaps
- The full list of AGORA's 77 tags was not retrievable from the documentation pages or the paper. The codebook is referenced at eto.tech and should be consulted directly if the report needs the complete tag list.
- The 25 OECD.AI instrument types were not listed on the page retrieved. Only the four categories are confirmed.
- Bradford's AI-specific writing after 2023 was not retrieved. The Digital Empires typology is drawn from an interview and the publisher page.
- No Carnegie or Brookings paper presenting a formal multi-country typology of AI regulatory models was read. The Brookings transatlantic comparison was identified but not opened.
- The Stanford 2026 chapter summary came through a summarising reader and may have misread charts. Its structure is reliable; individual figures need checking against the PDF.
- Digital Policy Alert and the Global Index on Responsible AI are other trackers with their own taxonomies. Neither was examined.

## 4. What quantitative evidence exists on national AI strategies, binding AI laws and AI safety institutes, and change to 2026?

### Takeaway
Counts depend heavily on definitions. The best series are the Stanford AI Index (laws and mentions), the OECD.AI database (initiatives), and Oxford Insights (strategies and readiness). Legislative activity has risen every year, strategy adoption has spread to emerging economies, and the number of safety institutes grew from 2 in 2023 to about 10 national bodies by late 2025, several of which have since dropped "safety" from their names. A single authoritative 2026 count of countries with a national AI strategy was not found.

- Voluntary firm instruments: 12 companies had published frontier AI safety policies by December 2025 (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Magic, Naver, Meta, G42, Cohere, Microsoft, Amazon, xAI, Nvidia) against 20 companies that had signed the Seoul commitments. [METR, December 2025](https://metr.org/blog/2025-12-09-common-elements-of-frontier-ai-safety-policies/)
- Participation counts: UN HLAB finding that 118 countries were party to none of the main non-UN AI governance initiatives and only 7 were party to all (reported via the World Economic Forum, October 2025). [WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/10/un-new-ai-governance-bodies/)
- Reported AI incidents rose from 233 in 2024 to 362 in 2025 (AI Index 2026 as reported by UNU). [UNU summary](https://c3.unu.edu/blog/2026-stanford-ai-index-report-takeaways)

### Inferences
- "AI-related law" in the Stanford series means any enacted law that mentions AI, not a comprehensive AI statute. The number of jurisdictions with a comprehensive, binding, horizontal AI law in force is far smaller (the EU, South Korea and Italy are the examples named by IAPP and Stanford). The report should keep these two counts apart.
- The 2026 Stanford chapter appears to have changed its global legislation data source (to Digital Policy Alert) and scope, so the 2016 to 2025 country totals are not directly comparable with the 2025 edition's totals (for example the US shows 27 for 2016 to 2024 in the 2025 edition and 25 for 2016 to 2025 as read from the 2026 edition). Do not splice the two series.
- Institute counts are unstable because of renaming and differing mandates. A count of "bodies with a mandate to evaluate advanced AI" is more defensible than a count of "AI safety institutes".
- Only 12 of 20 Seoul signatory companies had published a frontier safety policy about 18 months after committing. That is a direct measure of the gap between voluntary commitment and behaviour.

### Gaps
- No verified 2026 figure for the total number of countries with a published national AI strategy. Neither the Stanford 2026 summary nor the Oxford Insights landing page gave a total. The Oxford Insights 2025 report PDF and methodology report should contain it.
- No reliable count of jurisdictions with a comprehensive binding AI law in force at October 2026 was found. Developments after April 2026 (the AI Index publication) were not captured in any quantitative source.
- The Stanford 2026 figures were read through a summarising tool from a PDF with charts. The country bill counts in particular (for example "Japan 9 to 10") are approximate and should be checked.
- No up-to-date primary source list of institutes in the renamed international network was found.

## 5. What does the literature say about gaps between principles and practice at the international level?

### Takeaway
The literature and the 2025 to 2026 record agree on five gaps: instruments are non-binding and lack follow-up; the institutional field is a weak, fragmented regime complex that allows forum shopping; most states, mainly in the Global South, sit outside the instruments that matter; summit emphasis has moved from safety to adoption, investment and sovereignty; and firms hold agenda-setting power that civil society lacks. Geopolitical competition between the US and China is the most cited cause.

### Cited Findings
Non-binding status and weak follow-through:
- Official account: every instrument reviewed in section 1 other than the Council of Europe convention describes itself as voluntary or non-binding (for example the ASEAN Guide's "voluntary basis" clause, the New Delhi Declaration's voluntary pledges, the UN Dialogue as "not a negotiating forum"). [ASEAN](https://asean.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Expanded-ASEAN-Guide-on-AI-Governance-and-Ethics-Generative-AI.pdf); [UN Global Dialogue FAQ](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq)
- Behaviour: the summit series has "no governing framework", no central secretariat or coordinating body, and no mechanism to track progress on voluntary commitments. [Tech Policy Press, Multistakeholder Promises and Power Gaps](https://www.techpolicy.press/multistakeholder-promises-and-power-gaps-in-global-ai-summits/)
- Behaviour: 12 of 20 Seoul signatory companies had published a frontier safety policy by December 2025. [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-12-09-common-elements-of-frontier-ai-safety-policies/)
- Behaviour: 19 organisations filed in the first published round of HAIP reports in April 2025 (25 by the close of the first round). [OECD.AI blog](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/initial-submissions-g7-hiroshima-ai-process-reporting-framework); [OECD.AI HAIP overview](https://oecd.ai/en/transparency/overview)
- Behaviour: 55 UNESCO readiness reports against 193 adopting states. [UNESCO observatory](https://www.unesco.org/ethics-ai/en)
- Fjeld et al. and Jobin et al. both conclude that principles are insufficient without regulation, policy and professional practice, and that agreement on terms hides divergence on implementation. [Fjeld et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/339138141_Principled_Artificial_Intelligence_Mapping_Consensus_in_Ethical_and_Rights-Based_Approaches_to_Principles_for_AI); [Jobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)
- The World Economic Forum (October 2025) cites a figure that fewer than 1 per cent of organisations have fully operationalised responsible AI. The underlying survey was not checked, so treat as unconfirmed. [WEF](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/10/un-new-ai-governance-bodies/)

Fragmentation and forum shopping:
- Roberts, Hine, Taddeo and Floridi (International Affairs 100(3), 2024): "The global AI governance landscape is best understood as a weak regime complex, as it has a 'polycentric' structure with some linkages between institutions ... but work is generally siloed." [Roberts et al., preprint](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf); [journal record](https://academic.oup.com/ia/article/100/3/1275/7641064)
- They distinguish first-order cooperation problems (security competition; AI as dual-use technology; export controls and "arms race" narratives) from second-order problems (institutional dysfunction and lack of consensus on policy responses). [Roberts et al.](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf)
- On standards: "The ISO, IEC, IEEE, and ITU have all been active in AI standards-making initiatives. This enables companies to 'forum shop', thus weakening incentives for international cooperation." [Roberts et al.](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf)
- Their recommendation is to strengthen coordination, capacity and legitimacy of existing bodies (with the OECD as a relatively insulated technocratic forum) and not to create a new central agency. A 2025 International Affairs article argues the opposite case for an international AI agency. [Roberts et al.](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf); [International Affairs 101(4), 2025](https://academic.oup.com/ia/article/101/4/1483/8141294)
- CSIS (2026) identifies three competing architectures in mid 2026: Pax Silica (US), WAICO (China) and the UN Global Dialogue, and quotes the view that "the global contest around AI regulation is shaping up to be around which institutions will write the rules". [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)
- DFRLab (March 2026): the New Delhi Declaration's silence on the UN Panel and Dialogue shows "deeper fragmentation", and "parallel initiatives risk evolving in isolation rather than in concert". [DFRLab](https://dfrlab.org/2026/03/04/what-the-ai-impact-summit-reveals/)
- States also hedge across forums: Kazakhstan joined both Pax Silica and WAICO within two months. [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)

Exclusion of Global South states:
- The UN High-level Advisory Body's "Governing AI for Humanity" report found 118 countries were party to none of the main non-UN AI governance initiatives, and only 7, all developed, were party to all. [WEF, October 2025](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/10/un-new-ai-governance-bodies/)
- Jobin et al. found no AI ethics guidelines issued independently from Africa, South America or Central Asia in their 2019 sample. [Jobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)
- Roberts et al. cite the Partnership on AI's geographic imbalance as an example of skewed private governance. [Roberts et al.](https://www.sipotra.it/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Global-AI-governance-barriers-and-pathways-forward.pdf)
- Capacity gap in infrastructure: 2 to 8 state-backed GPU clusters in South Asia, Latin America and MENA against 44 in Europe and Central Asia and 85 in China (2025). [Stanford AI Index 2026, chapter 8](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)
- Official account of the response: the UN Panel and Dialogue are open to all 193 member states; New Delhi was the first summit in the series hosted by a Global South state; China's WAICO is pitched at developing countries; the G20 under South Africa launched AI for Africa. [UN Global Dialogue FAQ](https://www.un.org/global-dialogue-ai-governance/en/faq); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit); [CAIDP G20](https://www.caidp.org/resources/g20/)
- Behaviour: Tech Policy Press argues that Global South states risk "renting Big Tech's models", and Carnegie warns that India may stay in "consumption" mode without leverage over compute. A peer-reviewed article titled "Passengers in Flight: AI Governance Capacity in the Global South" (Digital Society, 2025) addresses the same capacity problem; only its title was seen. [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/multistakeholder-promises-and-power-gaps-in-global-ai-summits/); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit); [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44206-025-00195-6)

Shift from safety to action, investment and impact:
- CSIS: the series moved from "AI Safety Summit" (2023) to "safety, innovation, and inclusivity" (2024) to "AI Action Summit" (2025) to "AI Impact Summit" (2026), reflecting declining emphasis on governance and safety and rising emphasis on deployment and access. [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/state-ai-global-governance-and-its-implications-us-china-summit)
- Carnegie's count of "safety" mentions: 15, 14, 6, 0. [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)
- EPC on Paris: the US Vice President set out a "hands-off" regulatory stance, investment announcements dominated, and experts argued that US and China competition drove the move away from safety. [EPC](https://www.epc.eu/publication/The-Paris-Summit-Au-Revoir-global-AI-Safety-61ea68/)
- Institutional renaming follows the same line: UK AI Security Institute (February 2025), US CAISI (June 2025), network renamed (December 2025). [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2419745); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence_safety_institute)
- Counterpoints: Brookings argues New Delhi kept "secure, trustworthy and robust AI" as foundational. The UN Panel's July 2026 preliminary report puts the safeguards gap at the centre. Carnegie argues that "last-mile diffusion and development outcomes are legitimate organizing principles for global AI cooperation, not secondary concerns". [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/takeaways-from-the-india-ai-impact-summit/); [UN Panel Preliminary Report](https://www.un.org/independent-international-scientific-panel-ai/en/preliminary-report); [Carnegie](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/for-people-planet-and-progress-perspectives-from-indias-ai-impact-summit)
- US domestic policy moved the same way in 2025: the 23 January 2025 executive order on removing barriers, the July 2025 AI Action Plan, and the 12 December 2025 order to curb state AI laws. [Stanford AI Index 2026, chapter 8](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)

Corporate influence and civil society:
- Tech Policy Press describes a "participation paradox": civil society is "increasingly invited to speak at sessions and panels but remains largely excluded from agenda-setting and decision-making", and at New Delhi was not permitted to attend the Prime Minister's session with heads of state. It characterises summits as resembling "trade shows". This is commentary and is recorded as opinion. [Tech Policy Press](https://www.techpolicy.press/multistakeholder-promises-and-power-gaps-in-global-ai-summits/)
- In the US Congress, the industry share of AI hearing witnesses rose from 13 per cent to 37 per cent between 2017 and 2025, while the government share fell from 35 per cent to 10 per cent and academia from 26 per cent to 15 per cent. [Stanford AI Index 2026, chapter 8](https://hai.stanford.edu/assets/files/ai_index_report_2026_chapter_8_policy_and_governance.pdf)
- Jobin et al. found private companies were the largest single issuer group of AI ethics guidelines (22.6 per cent). [Jobin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.11668)

### Inferences
- The gap between official account and behaviour is measurable for several instruments and can be reported as ratios: UNESCO 55 of 193; Seoul commitments 12 of 20; HAIP 19 to 25 reporters out of the global developer population; New Delhi about 90 endorsers of a text with no obligations; one binding treaty not yet confirmed in force two years after opening.
- Breadth and depth trade off. Texts that keep the US and China both on board (Bletchley, New Delhi) are either very general or contain no obligations. Texts with more content (Paris on sustainability and inclusion, Seoul on safety science) lost one or more major powers.
- The causes cited most often are geopolitical competition (Roberts et al.'s first-order problems; CSIS; EPC), the commercial stakes signalled by the investment figures, and the US shift after January 2025. These are drivers of national policy as much as of international outcomes, so the report can use them in its "drivers" section.
- "Safety" has not disappeared. It has been relabelled as "security", "trust", "measurement and evaluation" and "standards", and moved from political declarations to technical bodies and to the UN scientific track. A theme set should use neutral labels such as "risk evaluation and assurance" so that it captures jurisdictions on both sides of the relabelling.
- Global South inclusion has improved in venue and voice (New Delhi, the UN Dialogue with 163 countries, WAICO) but the evidence on compute, models and capacity shows that material asymmetries remain.

### Gaps
- The HLAB "Governing AI for Humanity" report was not read directly (the Digital Watch copy was blocked). The 118 and 7 figures are reported second-hand through the World Economic Forum. The seven initiatives counted and the report's date (background knowledge: September 2024) should be confirmed in the UN original.
- Peer-reviewed empirical work published in 2025 or 2026 that measures compliance with international AI commitments was not found. The evidence on behaviour above comes from think tanks (CSIS, Carnegie, Brookings, EPC, DFRLab), METR and institutional self-reporting.
- No GovAI or Oxford Internet Institute paper was read directly in this session, although Roberts et al. are OII-affiliated authors.
- Sources named in the brief and not reached: Regulation and Governance and Policy and Society articles on AI regime complexity; Carnegie comparative regional work.
- No source was found that documents specific cases of states choosing one forum over another with stated reasons, beyond the US and UK refusal at Paris and the Kazakhstan dual membership. "Forum shopping" by states remains more asserted than evidenced; the firm-level standards version is from Roberts et al. only.
- The Tech Policy Press and EPC pieces are commentary. Their factual claims (civil society exclusion from specific sessions, figures on signatories) were not all cross-checked.
