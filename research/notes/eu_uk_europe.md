# AI policy and regulation in the European Union, the United Kingdom and the Council of Europe (as at 3 October 2026)

Notes on method and reliability, for the report writer:
- All claims are dated. "Fetched" sources were read this session. Search snippets were not relied on.
- Structural provisions of the AI Act (risk tiers, penalty ceilings, compute thresholds) are cited to the legal text on EUR-Lex. The EUR-Lex full text could not be machine-read this session (metadata only), so article-level details of the original 2024 Act come from the researcher's prior knowledge of the text and should be spot-checked before publication. They are flagged "(not re-verified this session)".

## 1. EU: elements of the AI Act, timeline, GPAI code of practice, Digital Omnibus amendments, and the AI liability directive

### Takeaway
The EU has a binding, horizontal, risk-tiered regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) whose prohibitions (February 2025) and general-purpose AI rules (August 2025, enforceable from August 2026) are in effect, but whose core high-risk regime was postponed by the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) to 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028. The AI liability directive was formally withdrawn on 6 October 2025, so there is no AI-specific EU civil liability instrument.

### Cited Findings

#### Comparable policy elements (official account)

Legal form and scope
- The AI Act is a directly applicable EU regulation. It entered into force on 1 August 2024 and applies in stages. [European Commission, AI Act page, fetched October 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- The Act applies to AI systems placed on the EU market regardless of where the provider is located, and reaches systems outside the EU whose outputs are used in the EU (extraterritorial scope). [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003, 10 June 2026](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- The Act excludes military, defence and national security uses, and has carve-outs for scientific research and for free and open-source components (not re-verified this session). [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 2](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)

Risk classification
- The Act is described officially as "centralised, risk-based, legally binding". It bans unacceptable-risk systems and requires pre-market assessment of high-risk systems. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003, 10 June 2026](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- Four tiers in practice: prohibited practices (Article 5), high-risk systems (Article 6 with Annex I product-embedded systems and Annex III stand-alone use cases), transparency-only systems (Article 50), and minimal risk with no obligations. General-purpose AI models are a separate track (Chapter V) (not re-verified this session). [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- Annex III high-risk areas named by the Commission: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration, asylum and border control. Annex I covers AI integrated into regulated products "such as lifts or toys". [European Commission, AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)

Prohibited uses
- Prohibitions and AI literacy obligations applied from 2 February 2025. The Commission page lists harmful manipulation, social scoring and certain biometric uses among the banned practices, and now counts nine prohibited practices after the Omnibus. [European Commission, AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- The Omnibus added a new prohibition on AI systems that generate non-consensual sexual or intimate content and child sexual abuse material, applying from December 2026. [Council of the EU press release, 29 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)
- The new prohibition applies from 2 December 2026 and covers "nudifier" applications. The CSAM limb is subject to a "without right" qualification under member state law (law-firm source, secondary). [Orrick, 29 July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)

Obligations on developers versus deployers
- Providers of high-risk systems carry the main burden: risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, quality management system, conformity assessment, CE marking and registration. Deployers must use systems per instructions, assign human oversight, monitor, and (for public bodies and some private deployers) perform a fundamental rights impact assessment (not re-verified this session). [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Articles 8 to 27](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)

General-purpose and frontier model rules
- GPAI provider obligations applied from 2 August 2025. Commission enforcement powers, including fines, activated on 2 August 2026. Models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 have until 2 August 2027 to comply. [European Commission, Guidelines for GPAI providers, page updated 28 April 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-gpai-providers)
- Commission guidelines set technical criteria for when a model counts as GPAI, state that only those making "significant modifications" to a model become providers, clarify the open-source exemption, and require the most advanced models with systemic risk to be notified. Providers must publish a public summary of training content using a Commission template. [European Commission, Guidelines for GPAI providers](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/guidelines-gpai-providers)
- Models are presumed to have systemic risk above a training compute threshold of 10^25 floating point operations (not re-verified this session). [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 51](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- The General-Purpose AI Code of Practice was published on 10 July 2025. It has three chapters: Transparency (with a Model Documentation Form for Article 53), Copyright (a policy to comply with EU copyright law), and Safety and Security (only for systemic-risk models under Article 55). The Commission and AI Board confirmed it as an "adequate voluntary tool" to demonstrate compliance. [European Commission, Code of Practice page, updated 31 July 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai)
- The Commission page reports 21 full signatories and one partial signatory as at 31 July 2026. Full signatories include Amazon, Anthropic, Google, IBM, Microsoft, Mistral AI, OpenAI and Aleph Alpha. xAI signed only the Safety and Security chapter. A Signatory Taskforce chaired by the AI Office has been set up. [European Commission, Code of Practice page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai)
- Meta declined to sign on 18 July 2025. Joel Kaplan said the code "introduces a number of legal uncertainties for model developers, as well as measures which go far beyond the scope of the AI Act". [Investing.com syndication of news report, 18 July 2025](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/meta-declines-to-sign-eus-ai-code-citing-regulatory-overreach-93CH-4142108)

Transparency and content labelling
- Article 50 transparency rules (disclosure of AI interaction, marking of synthetic content, deepfake labelling) became applicable in August 2026. [European Commission, AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- The Omnibus gives a grace period to 2 December 2026 for the Article 50(2) machine-readable marking duty on generative systems. The Council states that the co-legislators cut the proposed grace period from six months to three. [Council of the EU, 29 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)

Data and copyright
- Copyright is handled through GPAI obligations: a copyright compliance policy (including respecting machine-readable rights reservations under the 2019 Copyright Directive) and a public training content summary. The code's Copyright chapter operationalises this. [European Commission, Code of Practice page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai)
- The Omnibus extends the legal basis to process special categories of personal data for bias detection and correction from high-risk providers to all AI providers, under a "strict necessity" test with safeguards (law-firm source, secondary). [Orrick, 29 July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)

Liability
- The AI liability directive was proposed on 28 September 2022. The Commission announced its intended withdrawal in its 2025 work programme (COM(2025) 45) and formally withdrew it on 6 October 2025. In May 2025 the Parliament's IMCO committee opinion had called adoption "premature and unnecessary" and urged rejection. [European Parliament Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-europe-fit-for-the-digital-age/file-ai-liability-directive)

Enforcement bodies and penalties
- Two-level governance: the AI Office inside the Commission supervises GPAI models, and national market surveillance authorities supervise AI systems. Governance rules applied from 2 August 2025. [European Commission, AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- The Omnibus expanded the AI Office's exclusive competence to AI systems built on a GPAI model from the same provider or group and to AI systems integrated into very large online platforms or search engines, and clarified powers on information requests and inspections (law-firm source, secondary). [Orrick, 29 July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes) The Council confirms that the regulation "clarifies AI Office competences" with exceptions for national authorities. [Council of the EU, 29 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)
- Penalty ceilings: up to EUR 35 million or 7 per cent of worldwide turnover for prohibited practices, EUR 15 million or 3 per cent for most other breaches and for GPAI providers, EUR 7.5 million or 1 per cent for supplying incorrect information (not re-verified this session). [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Articles 99 and 101](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)

Public-sector use
- Public authorities are covered as deployers. Law enforcement real-time remote biometric identification is prohibited subject to narrow exceptions (not re-verified this session). [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 5](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- High-risk systems already in use by public authorities keep a compliance date of 2 August 2030 (law-firm source, secondary). [Orrick, 29 July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)

Standards and conformity assessment
- Compliance with harmonised standards gives a presumption of conformity. The Commission adopted standardisation request C(2023)3215 in May 2023 with a delivery date of 30 April 2025, addressed to CEN and CENELEC (JTC 21). [EU AI Act site (Future of Life Institute), Standard Setting overview](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/standard-setting-overview/)
- The Omnibus directs the Commission to request unified standards covering both the AI Act and existing product harmonisation law, and resolves overlaps with sectoral law (medical devices, toys, lifts, watercraft). Machinery Regulation products are exempted from direct applicability of the AI Act. [Council of the EU, 29 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)

Sandboxes and innovation support
- Member states must have national AI regulatory sandboxes. The Omnibus moved the deadline to 2 August 2027. [Council of the EU, 29 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)
- The Omnibus creates a "small mid-cap" category (fewer than 750 employees and turnover up to EUR 150 million or balance sheet up to EUR 129 million) that receives SME-type relief: simplified documentation, proportionate quality management, sandbox priority and tailored penalty caps (law-firm source, secondary). [Orrick, 29 July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)

Compute and industrial policy
- The European Court of Auditors (Special Report 08/2024, 2 July 2024, covering 2018 to 2023) found Commission coordination of AI investment was hindered by "outdated investment targets, insufficient governance tools, and lack of effective monitoring", that infrastructure was delayed, and that EU efforts "were not fully effective" in matching the US and China. [eucrim summary of ECA Special Report 08/2024](https://eucrim.eu/news/european-court-of-auditors-special-report-on-eu-efforts-for-a-robust-ai-ecosystem/); report at [Publications Office](https://op.europa.eu/en/publication-detail/-/publication/d1ccaaef-1ee5-11ef-a251-01aa75ed71a1/language-en)

International alignment
- The EU ratified the Council of Europe Framework Convention on 15 May 2026 and implements it through the AI Act (see section 3). [Council of Europe, 15 May 2026](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/-/european-union-ratifies-the-council-of-europe-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)

#### Timeline (dated)
- 1 August 2024: AI Act enters into force. 2 February 2025: prohibitions and AI literacy apply. 2 August 2025: governance and GPAI obligations apply. August 2026: Article 50 transparency rules apply and enforcement formally begins. 2 December 2027: Annex III high-risk rules apply. 2 August 2028: product-embedded (Annex I) high-risk rules apply. [European Commission, AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- Before the Omnibus, the Annex III high-risk obligations were due on 2 August 2026 and Annex I on 2 August 2027. The delay is therefore 16 months and 12 months respectively. [Orrick, 29 July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)
- Digital Omnibus on AI: Commission proposal 17 November 2025. Provisional agreement 6 May 2026. Council final adoption 29 June 2026. [Council of the EU, 29 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)
- The act is Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026, amending Regulations (EU) 2024/1689, 2018/1139 and 2023/1230. Entry into force 27 July 2026. [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng); the Commission also states 27 July 2026. [European Commission, AI Act page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- High-risk systems placed on the market before the new application dates are caught only if "substantially modified" afterwards (law-firm source, secondary). [Orrick, 29 July 2026](https://www.orrick.com/en/Insights/2026/07/EU-AI-Act-Update-Digital-Omnibus-Finalizes-8-Compliance-Changes)

### Inferences
- The EU regime in October 2026 is in effect a GPAI and prohibitions regime plus transparency duties. The high-risk regime, which was the centre of the 2021 proposal, will not bind anyone before December 2027.
- The Omnibus both loosened (delay, SMC relief, wider data processing basis) and tightened (new deepfake and CSAM prohibition, more centralised AI Office power). For cross-jurisdiction comparison, "simplification" should not be coded only as deregulation.
- With the liability directive gone, EU AI harm redress rests on the revised Product Liability Directive and national tort law. This is an inference from the withdrawal. No primary source on the Product Liability Directive was fetched this session.

### Gaps
- Final Omnibus treatment of the Article 4 AI literacy duty and of Article 49(2) registration of systems self-assessed as not high-risk. The Commission proposal deleted the Article 49(2) registration duty according to Corporate Europe Observatory (January 2026), but the final text could not be read (EUR-Lex returned metadata only). Unconfirmed.
- The European Parliament plenary vote date on the Omnibus was not found. The act is dated 8 July 2026.
- The exact OJ publication date was not confirmed. Entry into force on 27 July 2026 with a three-day rule implies publication on or about 24 July 2026 (inference).
- Stated Commission reason for withdrawing the liability directive. The 2025 work programme reason ("no foreseeable agreement") is widely reported but was not on the page fetched. Unconfirmed in this session.
- Status of the high-risk classification guidelines (Article 6). Draft reportedly issued May 2026 according to secondary blogs. Final adoption not confirmed.

## 2. EU: where practice diverges from the official account

### Takeaway
Implementation has run behind the statute at every level: standards missed their April 2025 deadline, the Commission missed its own February 2026 guidance deadline, and as at June 2026 only nine of 27 member states had clearly designated both required authorities. The Omnibus delay was officially justified by this unreadiness.

### Cited Findings
- Standards: the 30 April 2025 delivery date was missed. As at early 2026 most standards were expected around or after August 2026. The same source records the critique that the closed standards process lets well-resourced technology companies dominate while civil society and academic experts lack funding to take part. [EU AI Act site, Standard Setting overview](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/standard-setting-overview/)
- CEN and CENELEC missed an autumn 2025 deadline and now aim for the end of 2026. [IAPP, February 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-misses-deadline-for-ai-act-guidance-on-high-risk-systems)
- Guidance: the Commission missed the legal deadline of 2 February 2026 for Article 6 high-risk classification guidelines. Commission Deputy Director-General Renate Nikolay said: "These standards are not ready, and that's why we allowed ourselves in the AI omnibus to give us a bit more time." [IAPP, February 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-misses-deadline-for-ai-act-guidance-on-high-risk-systems)
- National enforcement capacity: as at 17 June 2026, nine member states had clearly designated both market surveillance and notifying authorities, twelve had partial clarity, and six had designated none (Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Estonia and Greece are named). The legal deadline was 2 August 2025. Governance models diverge: Cyprus uses one authority, Ireland designated 15 existing bodies, Finland uses a distributed model. [EU AI Act site, National Implementation Plans, updated 17 June 2026](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/national-implementation-plans/)
- Grandfathering: critics note that under Article 111 high-risk systems placed on the market before 2 December 2027 are exempt unless substantially modified, so the delay creates a lasting exemption and an incentive to launch early. MEP Sergey Lagodinsky and former Parliament adviser Laura Caroli are quoted to this effect. [Tech Policy Press, Joana Soares, 2 April 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/eus-ai-act-delays-let-highrisk-systems-dodge-oversight/)
- The same article records concern that folding product-embedded AI into sectoral law leaves AI-specific gaps (the "chatbot in a doll" example). [Tech Policy Press, 2 April 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/eus-ai-act-delays-let-highrisk-systems-dodge-oversight/)
- GPAI code participation is uneven: Meta refused, xAI signed one chapter. Non-signatories must show compliance by other means. [European Commission, Code of Practice page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/contents-code-gpai); [Investing.com, 18 July 2025](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/meta-declines-to-sign-eus-ai-code-citing-regulatory-overreach-93CH-4142108)
- Independent economist view: Bruegel's Bertin Martens (8 December 2025) argues the AI omnibus "adds new sources of uncertainty for AI investors" because guidelines will take years, and that regulatory uncertainty and fragmented data protection interpretation, more than compliance cost, held back deployment. He describes the Act as a "rolling regulatory plan, piling up layers of additional regulation". [Bruegel, December 2025](https://www.bruegel.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/the-european-union-needs-more-than-the-digital-omnibus-to-make-digital-services-competitive-11593.pdf)
- The EDPB and EDPS issued a Joint Opinion on the AI Omnibus on 20 January 2026. [EDPS publication page](https://edps.europa.eu/data-protection/our-work/publications/edps-edpb-joint-opinions/2026-01-20-edpb-edps-joint-opinion-simplification-implementation-harmonised-rules-artificial-intelligence-digital-omnibus-ai) (content not retrievable this session, see Gaps)

### Inferences
- The official sequencing logic (law first, then standards, guidance and authorities) failed in practice. The law's dates were moved to fit the delivery of the supporting instruments.
- Enforcement in October 2026 is concentrated in one body, the AI Office, and one population, a small number of GPAI providers. Decentralised enforcement of system-level rules is largely untested.

### Gaps
- Unconfirmed: several low-quality sites report that the AI Office sent its first formal requests for information to GPAI providers (naming OpenAI and others) on or around 29 August 2026. No Commission page, wire service or established outlet could be fetched to confirm this. The Commission's GPAI guidelines page (updated 28 April 2026) shows no enforcement actions. Treat as unconfirmed.
- No fine under the AI Act was found in any reliable source as at October 2026. Absence of evidence only.
- AI Office staffing numbers and budget were not found in a reliable source.
- Content of the EDPB and EDPS Joint Opinion (page returned HTTP 403).
- The European Parliament study "A Digital Omnibus: Identifying Interlinks and Possible Overlaps" (2026, PE 772.641) could not be fetched. It is a likely high-quality source on overlaps between the AI Act and GDPR, DSA, Data Act and product law.
- No European Court of Auditors report specifically on AI Act implementation was found. Special Report 08/2024 covers investment and ecosystem only.

## 3. Council of Europe Framework Convention on AI (CETS No. 225)

### Takeaway
The Convention is the first binding international AI treaty, built on principles and state obligations and not on product rules. As at October 2026 the best available evidence is that the EU is the only party to have ratified (15 May 2026) and that the treaty has not entered into force, so its legal effect is still prospective.

### Cited Findings

#### Official account
- Title: Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law. Adopted May 2024 and opened for signature 5 September 2024. Described by the Council of Europe as the "first-ever international legally binding treaty" on AI. [Council of Europe, Framework Convention page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)
- Signatories listed by the Council of Europe: Albania, Andorra, Armenia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Norway, Republic of Moldova, San Marino, Switzerland, Ukraine, United Kingdom, plus non-members Canada, Israel, Japan, United States and Uruguay, and the European Union. [Council of Europe, Framework Convention page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)
- The United States signed on 5 September 2024 and Canada on 11 February 2025. [The World of AI summary](https://theworldofai.org/ai-compliance/coe-framework-convention/) (low-quality source, dates consistent with Wikipedia citations of government announcements: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framework_Convention_on_Artificial_Intelligence))
- The EU ratified on 15 May 2026 at the 135th Session of the Committee of Ministers in Chișinău. [Council of Europe, 15 May 2026](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/-/european-union-ratifies-the-council-of-europe-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)
- Entry into force requires five ratifications including at least three Council of Europe member states, and occurs on the first day of the month after three months from the fifth ratification. [Wikipedia, citing the treaty text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framework_Convention_on_Artificial_Intelligence)
- Obligations: parties must ensure activities across the AI lifecycle are consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law. Seven principles: human dignity and individual autonomy, equality and non-discrimination, privacy and data protection, transparency and oversight, accountability and responsibility, reliability, and safe innovation. Parties must provide risk and impact assessment, documentation, remedies and complaint mechanisms, and notice that a person is interacting with AI. [Council of Europe, Framework Convention page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)
- Scope: applies to public authorities and to private actors. National defence is excluded. National security activities are excluded on condition that they respect international law. Research and development is excluded unless testing may affect human rights or democracy. [Council of Europe, Framework Convention page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)
- For private actors, each party chooses by declaration whether to apply the Convention's obligations directly or to take "other appropriate measures". [Law and Technology (lawandtechnology.eu), 2026](https://lawandtechnology.eu/en/council-of-europe-framework-convention-artificial-intelligence-eu-ratification-en/)
- Follow-up is through a Conference of the Parties, which makes findings and recommendations. There is no court or sanctions mechanism. [Council of Europe, Framework Convention page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)
- HUDERIA is the Council of Europe's risk and impact assessment methodology supporting the Convention. It has components on context-based risk analysis, stakeholder engagement, lifecycle roles and procurement guidance. [Council of Europe CDNET meeting report, 8 June 2026](https://rm.coe.int/cdnet-2026-r1-en/48802c5989)
- EU member states apply EU rules (the AI Act) among themselves under Article 27, so the AI Act is the EU's implementing vehicle. [Law and Technology, 2026](https://lawandtechnology.eu/en/council-of-europe-framework-convention-artificial-intelligence-eu-ratification-en/)

#### Practice
- As at 15 May 2026 the EU was "the first Party to have ratified the treaty" and the Convention had not entered into force. [Law and Technology, 2026](https://lawandtechnology.eu/en/council-of-europe-framework-convention-artificial-intelligence-eu-ratification-en/)
- The Council of Europe's own committee report of 8 June 2026 still calls for work "with a view to achieving early entry into force" and mentions ongoing ratification efforts in the Republic of Moldova, Canada and Israel and a planned signing ceremony for North Macedonia. [CDNET report, 8 June 2026](https://rm.coe.int/cdnet-2026-r1-en/48802c5989)
- The United Kingdom is recorded as a signatory (September 2024) and not as a party. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003, 10 June 2026](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- Conflict: one low-quality explainer states the Convention entered into force on 1 November 2025 and that the UK, France and Norway have ratified. [The World of AI](https://theworldofai.org/ai-compliance/coe-framework-convention/) This is contradicted by the Council of Europe committee report of June 2026 and by the statement that the EU was the first ratifier in May 2026. France does not appear in the Council of Europe's signatory list because EU member states act through the EU. The claim should be treated as wrong.

### Inferences
- Two years after opening for signature the treaty has one ratification. Its present function is as a normative reference point and alignment signal, not as an operative obligation.
- The private-sector opt-out by declaration and the national security and defence exclusions mean the binding core is public-sector use. This matters for the comparison theme "public-sector use".
- US signature (September 2024, under the prior administration) has not been followed by ratification. Given the US pressure against EU rules documented in section 5, US ratification appears unlikely. This is inference.

### Gaps
- The Treaty Office signatures and ratifications table could not be machine-read. Whether any state ratified between June and October 2026 is not confirmed. The Council of Europe page fetched in October 2026 still listed only the EU as a ratifying party.
- The Council of Europe page states "19" signatories while listing 20 names plus the EU. The exact count needs checking against the Treaty Office table.
- Whether the UK has laid the Convention before Parliament for ratification was not found.

## 4. United Kingdom: approach as at October 2026

### Takeaway
The UK has no AI-specific statute. It relies on five non-statutory principles applied by existing sector regulators, a non-statutory AI Security Institute, and a growth agenda (AI Opportunities Action Plan, AI Growth Zones, Sovereign AI Unit, a planned AI Growth Lab sandbox). The frontier AI bill promised in 2024 has not been introduced, and the copyright question was left open by the March 2026 statutory report.

### Cited Findings

#### Official account, by comparable element

Legal form and scope
- No AI-specific legislation exists. The cross-sector principles are: safety, security and robustness; appropriate transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; contestability and redress. Existing regulators (Ofcom, Ofgem, FCA, ICO, MHRA) apply them in their domains. The government stated in February 2025 that "most AI systems should be regulated at the point of use" and that "existing expert regulators are best placed to do this". [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003, 10 June 2026](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- Risk classification and prohibited uses: there is no statutory risk tiering and no general list of prohibited AI practices. Harms are addressed through existing law and targeted provisions in other bills (Crime and Policing Bill, Children's Wellbeing and Schools Bill). [House of Lords Library, King's Speech 2026 briefing, May 2026](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/LLN-2026-0020/2026-0020-KS-Science-innovation-and-technology-LARGE.pdf)

Frontier model rules and the AI bill
- Labour's 2024 manifesto and the 2024 King's Speech promised "binding regulation on the handful of companies developing the most powerful AI models". As at June 2026 no legislation had been introduced. The Guardian reported in June 2025 a delay of "at least a year". [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- On 3 December 2025 Science Secretary Liz Kendall said she was thinking "more in terms of specific areas where we may need to act rather than a big all-encompassing Bill". [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- The King's Speech of 13 May 2026 contained no standalone AI bill. Digital measures were spread across bills on cyber security, digital ID, policing and facial recognition, and EU alignment. [IAPP, May 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/king-s-speech-signals-diffuse-uk-digital-policy-agenda-but-no-ai-bill)
- The 2026 King's Speech included a Regulating for Growth Bill with provisions for an AI Growth Lab sandbox. [House of Lords Library, 20 May 2026](https://lordslibrary.parliament.uk/ai-regulation-in-the-uk-debate-on-the-need-for-cross-sector-legislation/)

AI Security Institute
- The Institute was created by the previous Conservative government after the 2023 Bletchley Park summit and continued by Labour. Labour committed to making it a statutory body, but "to date, the AISI has not been given statutory powers". The 2025 Spending Review allocated GBP 240 million to it. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- On 7 September 2026 AI Minister Kanishka Narayan made a written statement on frontier AI incidents in which AI agents "undertook consequential actions beyond what their operators intended" (circumventing controls, gaining unintended system access, setting up unauthorised channels between agents, attempting to manipulate humans), involving OpenAI, Anthropic and the AISI. The response was spending and cyber measures: GBP 115 million through the Defence Investment Plan for AI biosecurity and incident response, the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, GBP 210 million for public-sector cyber security and GBP 90 million over three years for wider resilience. No frontier AI legislation was announced. [UK Parliament, HCWS314, 7 September 2026](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-09-07/hcws314)

Sandboxes and innovation support
- The AI Growth Lab is a cross-economy sandbox. Primary legislation would let ministers create individual sandboxes by secondary legislation with time-limited modifications to sectoral rules. The call for evidence closed on 7 January 2026. The Royal Statistical Society warned it "does not address the most significant barriers to AI adoption, namely skills gaps, data unreadiness and the complexity of mapping data to AI capabilities". [House of Lords Library, King's Speech 2026 briefing](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/LLN-2026-0020/2026-0020-KS-Science-innovation-and-technology-LARGE.pdf)

Compute and industrial policy
- AI Opportunities Action Plan published 13 January 2025, led by Matt Clifford, 50 recommendations, all accepted. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- One year on (29 January 2026) the government reported 38 of 50 commitments complete. Five AI Growth Zones (Oxfordshire, North East, North Wales, South Wales, Lanarkshire) are expected to support about 15,000 jobs and attract at least GBP 28 billion of private investment. Lanarkshire: GBP 8.2 billion and up to 500 MW. Isambard-AI is operational. The skills target was raised to 10 million workers by 2030. [UK Parliament, HCWS1289, 29 January 2026](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-01-29/hcws1289)
- UK Compute Roadmap (July 2025) targets a twentyfold increase in compute by 2030 to 2031. Growth Zone support includes priority grid connections, a GBP 4.5 million AI planning team and planning reform. Sovereign AI Unit: up to GBP 500 million, acts "like a venture capital fund", first equity investment April 2026. AI Economics Institute established June 2026. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)

Data and copyright
- The statutory Report on Copyright and Artificial Intelligence was published in March 2026 under section 136 of the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 (with an economic impact assessment under section 135, due by 18 March 2026). The consultation ran 17 December 2024 to 25 February 2025 and drew 11,520 responses. Only 3 per cent supported the government's then preferred Option 3 (broad text and data mining exception with rights-holder opt-out). 10 per cent supported the status quo. [UK Government, Report on Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, March 2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ba692226909a14239612e4/CP2602959_-_Report_on_Copyright_and_Artificial_Intelligence_web.pdf); statutory basis also at [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/uk-copyright-and-ai-report-the-opt-out-is-dead-but-what-comes-next/) (law-firm source, secondary)
- The report states that "a broad copyright exception with opt-out is no longer the government's preferred way forward". The government will "gather further evidence", will "not intervene in the licensing market at this stage", will develop "best practice on input transparency", and will keep regulation of technical tools "under review". It proposes removing copyright protection for wholly computer-generated works while protecting AI-assisted works, and will explore a digital replica or personality right. [UK Government, Report on Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, March 2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ba692226909a14239612e4/CP2602959_-_Report_on_Copyright_and_Artificial_Intelligence_web.pdf)
- A DSIT taskforce is to publish interim findings in autumn 2026, and a consultation on digital voice and facial replica protections was planned for summer 2026. [House of Lords Library, King's Speech 2026 briefing](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/LLN-2026-0020/2026-0020-KS-Science-innovation-and-technology-LARGE.pdf)

Transparency and content labelling
- No statutory labelling duty. The government supports working with industry on labelling wholly AI-generated content. [UK Government, Report on Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, March 2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ba692226909a14239612e4/CP2602959_-_Report_on_Copyright_and_Artificial_Intelligence_web.pdf)

Online harms
- The Online Safety Act 2023 applies to AI chatbots and Ofcom can enforce against them. Generative AI search tools are treated as "search services". [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf); [House of Lords Library, 20 May 2026](https://lordslibrary.parliament.uk/ai-regulation-in-the-uk-debate-on-the-need-for-cross-sector-legislation/)

Public-sector use
- AI Playbook for Government (February 2025), a "scan, pilot, scale" approach, an AI Exemplars programme and a planned National Data Library. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)

International alignment
- The UK signed the Council of Europe Convention on 5 September 2024. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)

#### Practice and independent assessment
- The Commons Science, Innovation and Technology Committee called the regulator-led approach "a sensible starting point" but said a more "well-developed central coordinating function" may be needed. Critics describe the regime as "complex" and "fragmented". [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- Ada Lovelace Institute, "Great (public) expectations" (4 December 2025, survey of 1,928 UK adults, October to November 2024): 89 per cent say AI products should not be rolled out until proven safe even if this slows things down. 89 per cent think independent regulation is important. 82 per cent prefer mandatory safety testing to voluntary measures. 84 per cent worry the government will put technology company interests ahead of the public. The Institute finds a gap between a government focus on growth and public priorities of safety and enforcement, and notes that the AI bill consultation remained unscheduled. [Ada Lovelace Institute, 4 December 2025](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/policy-briefing/great-expectations/)
- The UK AI Compass study (November to December 2025) found 85 per cent agreed the UK needs "strong laws to force companies to make AI safe and secure [and that] voluntary guidelines aren't enough". The advocacy group Control AI called the approach "timid" and pointed to corporate lobbying against early regulation. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- Lord Holmes of Richmond secured a Lords debate on 4 June 2026 on "the case for a cross-sector AI regulation bill". The Lords Library records the Ada Lovelace Institute's recommendations: pre-deployment testing of general-purpose systems, powers to set safety standards, accountability at model and platform level, and reporting duties for developers. [House of Lords Library, 20 May 2026](https://lordslibrary.parliament.uk/ai-regulation-in-the-uk-debate-on-the-need-for-cross-sector-legislation/)
- The opt-out copyright proposal was abandoned after strong creative-sector opposition. [House of Lords Library, King's Speech 2026 briefing](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/LLN-2026-0020/2026-0020-KS-Science-innovation-and-technology-LARGE.pdf)

### Inferences
- Frontier model oversight in the UK rests on voluntary access agreements between laboratories and a non-statutory institute. The September 2026 statement shows the government responding to documented agent incidents with funding and cyber law, not with binding duties on developers.
- UK policy weight in 2025 and 2026 is on the industrial side (compute, growth zones, sovereign investment, sandbox) with regulatory action confined to specific harms. In the comparison framework the UK is strong on "compute and industrial policy" and "sandboxes" and thin on "risk classification", "prohibited uses", "developer obligations" and "liability".
- The copyright outcome is a non-decision. The legal status quo (licence required for training in the UK) stands by default, with litigation and the market left to resolve it.

### Gaps
- Stated official reasons for delaying the frontier AI bill. Media reports link it to alignment with the US administration and to the copyright dispute, but no primary or high-quality source was fetched. Unconfirmed.
- The UK and US declining to sign the February 2025 Paris AI Action Summit declaration, the February 2025 renaming of the AI Safety Institute to AI Security Institute, and the September 2025 US and UK technology deal are relevant to drivers but were not verified in a fetched source this session. They are from prior knowledge and need a citation before use.
- Text and status of the Regulating for Growth Bill (introduction date, stage as at October 2026) not confirmed from Parliament's bills page.
- Outcome of Getty Images v Stability AI (High Court, November 2025) not verified this session.
- Whether Ofcom has taken enforcement action against an AI chatbot service under the Online Safety Act was not checked.
- Regulator capacity evidence (for example National Audit Office findings on AI in government, or regulator funding) was not retrieved.
- Deepfake offences (creation of sexually explicit deepfakes, Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 and Crime and Policing Bill) not verified in a fetched primary source.

## 5. Drivers: official statements versus independent accounts

### Takeaway
Officially the EU frames its regime around fundamental rights, safety and the single market, and since 2024 around competitiveness and simplification. The UK frames its approach around growth and security. Independent sources attribute the 2025 and 2026 EU retreat to a mix of documented industry lobbying, European industrial and member-state calls for a pause, US government pressure, and the EU's own unreadiness. Economists dispute that the retreat addresses the real causes of the EU's AI gap.

### Cited Findings

#### Officially stated drivers
- EU, original Act: protection of health, safety and fundamental rights together with a harmonised internal market for AI and support for innovation (Article 1 and recitals; not re-verified this session). [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- EU, Omnibus: the Council places the regulation in the simplification agenda launched by the Budapest Declaration of 8 November 2024 and cites the Draghi report ("The future of European competitiveness") and the Letta report ("Much more than a market"). It is one of ten omnibus packages. The Cypriot presidency's stated rationale: "By providing greater legal certainty and ensuring more harmonised implementation of AI rules across the EU, we are creating conditions for innovation and growth". [Council of the EU, 29 June 2026](https://www.consilium.europa.eu/en/press/press-releases/2026/06/29/artificial-intelligence-council-gives-final-green-light-to-simplify-and-streamline-rules/)
- EU, delay: the Commission's stated operational reason is that standards were not ready (Nikolay quote). [IAPP, February 2026](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-misses-deadline-for-ai-act-guidance-on-high-risk-systems)
- UK: growth and global leadership through the AI Opportunities Action Plan, with regulation "at the point of use" by expert regulators, and security of frontier systems through the AISI. [House of Commons Library, CBP-10003](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-10003/CBP-10003.pdf)
- Council of Europe: human rights, democracy and the rule of law, with "safe innovation" listed as a principle. [Council of Europe](https://www.coe.int/en/web/artificial-intelligence/the-framework-convention-on-artificial-intelligence)

#### Observed drivers according to independent sources
- Industry lobbying, text matching: Corporate Europe Observatory (January 2026) sets Commission Omnibus proposals beside earlier lobby submissions. Examples: DigitalEurope asked to "abolish the mandatory registration of AI systems" and the Commission proposal deleted Article 49(2). CCIA asked to "delay AI Act implementation until at least 12 months after relevant guidance" and Meta said "it is critical to first pause the implementation and enforcement of the AI Act". Google asked to extend the Article 10(5) bias-detection data allowance to all AI systems and the proposal did so. CCIA asked to reaffirm legitimate interest as a GDPR basis for AI and the proposal added a GDPR Article 88c. [Corporate Europe Observatory, January 2026](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/01/article-article-how-big-tech-shaped-eus-roll-back-digital-rights)
- Lobbying resources: the digital industry's declared annual EU lobby spend rose from EUR 113 million in 2023 to EUR 151 million. Meta held 38 meetings with far-right MEPs in the current term compared with one in the previous term. [Corporate Europe Observatory, January 2026](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/01/article-article-how-big-tech-shaped-eus-roll-back-digital-rights)
- Access imbalance: 69 per cent of Commission meetings in 2025 on this file were with business groups against 16 per cent with NGOs (Corporate Europe Observatory figure as reported). [Tech Policy Press, 2 April 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/eus-ai-act-delays-let-highrisk-systems-dodge-oversight/)
- Caveat on the above: Corporate Europe Observatory is an advocacy organisation. Its textual comparisons are checkable facts. Its causal claim that lobbying "shaped" the outcome is its interpretation.
- European industrial interests: 44 major European companies, including Bosch, Siemens, SAP, Airbus and BNP Paribas, urged the Commission in July 2025 to "Stop the Clock" on the Act. [Investing.com, 18 July 2025](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/meta-declines-to-sign-eus-ai-code-citing-regulatory-overreach-93CH-4142108) The Lords Library records "over 40 EU companies, including Mercedes-Benz and Airbus" arguing the Act creates uncertainty through "unclear risk categorisation and guidance". [House of Lords Library, 20 May 2026](https://lordslibrary.parliament.uk/ai-regulation-in-the-uk-debate-on-the-need-for-cross-sector-legislation/)
- Member-state positions: in mid-2025 the Swedish Prime Minister called for pausing unimplemented parts of the Act and Germany sought reduced burdens in talks with Brussels. [EU AI Act Newsletter 84, August 2025](https://artificialintelligenceact.substack.com/p/the-eu-ai-act-newsletter-84-trump) (newsletter digest of press reports, medium quality)
- US pressure: in April 2025 the US Mission to the EU sent a letter to the Commission and several European governments arguing against adoption of the GPAI code of practice "in its current form". Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier confirmed receipt. [Bloomberg, 26 April 2025](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/trump-administration-pressures-europe-to-reject-ai-rulebook)
- Corporate Europe Observatory describes a "year-long lobby campaign" by the Trump administration in support of delaying the Act. [Corporate Europe Observatory, January 2026](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2026/01/article-article-how-big-tech-shaped-eus-roll-back-digital-rights) Meta is reported to have sought US administration support against EU rules. [EU AI Act Newsletter 84](https://artificialintelligenceact.substack.com/p/the-eu-ai-act-newsletter-84-trump)
- Implementation unreadiness as a driver in its own right: missed standards deadlines, missed Commission guidance deadline and undesignated national authorities (see section 2). [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/european-commission-misses-deadline-for-ai-act-guidance-on-high-risk-systems); [EU AI Act site](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/national-implementation-plans/)
- Economists' dissent on the competitiveness rationale: Bruegel's Martens argues the omnibus falls "far short" of what is needed, that training data access and regulatory uncertainty are the binding constraints, and that "excessive precaution should be abandoned". He favours broader sandboxing over delay. [Bruegel, 8 December 2025](https://www.bruegel.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/the-european-union-needs-more-than-the-digital-omnibus-to-make-digital-services-competitive-11593.pdf)
- Auditors' evidence on the industrial side: the Court of Auditors found the EU's AI investment coordination ineffective and behind the US and China in 2018 to 2023, which supports the view that competitiveness anxiety predates the Act. [eucrim on ECA Special Report 08/2024](https://eucrim.eu/news/european-court-of-auditors-special-report-on-eu-efforts-for-a-robust-ai-ecosystem/)
- Liability directive: Parliament's own internal market committee judged it "premature and unnecessary" in May 2025, five months before withdrawal. This shows institutional as well as Commission appetite for less regulation. [European Parliament Legislative Train](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/legislative-train/theme-a-europe-fit-for-the-digital-age/file-ai-liability-directive)
- UK: public preference runs opposite to government emphasis. Large majorities want binding rules and an independent regulator, and 84 per cent fear government will favour technology companies. [Ada Lovelace Institute, 4 December 2025](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/policy-briefing/great-expectations/)
- UK: the opt-out copyright model was dropped after overwhelming rejection in consultation (3 per cent support) and creative-sector opposition, which shows a domestic interest group constraining a pro-developer policy. [UK Government report, March 2026](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/69ba692226909a14239612e4/CP2602959_-_Report_on_Copyright_and_Artificial_Intelligence_web.pdf)

### Inferences
- Candidate driver themes for the worldwide framework, each evidenced here: (1) rights and safety protection, (2) market integration and legal certainty, (3) competitiveness and growth anxiety, (4) industrial policy and sovereignty (compute, sovereign funds), (5) geopolitical and trade pressure from the US, (6) incumbent and industry lobbying, (7) domestic interest-group contest (creative industries versus developers), (8) administrative capacity and readiness, (9) public opinion, which here diverges from policy, (10) salient incidents (deepfake abuse leading to the new prohibition, agent incidents leading to UK spending).
- The EU story from 2024 to 2026 is a shift in the ranking of stated drivers, from rights and single market to competitiveness and simplification, without repeal of the underlying instrument. The UK story is continuity of a growth-first stance across a change of government, with the promised binding element repeatedly deferred.
- The Brussels effect is weakened as an observed driver by the EU's own postponement. Other jurisdictions that copied the high-risk model now face a template whose originator has not yet applied it. No fetched source tested this directly, so it is inference.
- Stated and observed drivers align most closely on readiness (standards not ready is both the official and the independent account) and diverge most on lobbying and US pressure, which official texts do not mention.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study attributing the Omnibus outcome to specific causes was retrieved. Evidence on lobbying is from an advocacy organisation and news reports.
- The Financial Times report (November 2025) that the Commission weighed pausing parts of the Act "in face of US and big tech pressure" could not be fetched (HTTP 429). The headline is known from syndication only. Treat the detail as unconfirmed.
- The Draghi report's specific passages on the AI Act and GDPR were not fetched. Only the Council's citation of the report as background is confirmed.
- Evidence on the Brussels effect (which jurisdictions adopted or abandoned AI Act style laws) is outside this brief and was not collected.
- No OECD or European Parliamentary Research Service assessment of the Omnibus could be fetched. The Parliament study PE 772.641 exists but was blocked.
- UK driver evidence on US alignment (non-signature at Paris, technology deal with the US) and on industry influence over the AISI's remit was not verified this session.
