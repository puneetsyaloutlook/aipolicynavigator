## China: what is regulated, with which instruments, and why

### Takeaway
China regulates AI through a stack of narrow, binding administrative rules led by the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC), each targeting a specific service type (recommendation algorithms, deep synthesis, generative AI, labelling, companion AI, minors), enforced through filing and registration that works as quasi-licensing, plus a broad promotion agenda (AI Plus). A comprehensive AI law was removed from the 2025 legislative plan and returned to the State Council's 2026 plan as "comprehensive legislation" to be accelerated, but no draft has been published. Independent analysts read the system as driven first by information control and social stability, with strictness rising and falling with economic conditions and technological confidence, and with visible bargaining among CAC, NDRC, MOST and MIIT.

### Cited Findings

#### Legal form and overall architecture
- China has no single AI statute as at October 2026. Governance has developed through sector-specific regulations (recommendation algorithms, deepfakes, generative AI, synthetic content labelling) after China "delayed its plans to establish a comprehensive national AI law, instead pursuing a path focused on incremental changes to its existing AI regulatory architecture" (Carnegie, July 2025). Source: [Carnegie Endowment, Singer and Sheehan, "China's AI Policy at the Crossroads", July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Cybersecurity Law amendments took effect on 1 January 2026 and are the first time AI governance appears in a national statute. The new provision says the state will strengthen AI ethics regulation and enhance AI risk assessment and governance, while supporting innovation and the development of training data resources. Maximum fines under the amended law rise to RMB 10 million for entities, and responsible individuals can be fined up to RMB 1 million. Source: [IAPP, "China's Cybersecurity Law amendments introduce AI provisions"](https://iapp.org/news/a/notes-from-the-asia-pacific-region-china-s-cybersecurity-law-amendments-introduce-ai-provisions)
- The AI provision in the Cybersecurity Law is a statement of state policy (promote plus govern). It does not itself create a risk classification, a list of prohibited uses or operator duties. Source: [IAPP, same article](https://iapp.org/news/a/notes-from-the-asia-pacific-region-china-s-cybersecurity-law-amendments-introduce-ai-provisions)

#### Status of a general AI law
- The State Council's 2025 Legislative Work Plan removed the AI Law as a "preparatory item". The NPC Standing Committee's 2025 plan instead said "legislative items concerning the sound development of AI shall be researched and drafted promptly". Legal scholar Zhang Linghan wrote in Legal Daily (28 May 2025) that the new wording reflected "a more systematic and strategic approach" rather than reduced urgency. Source: [Geopolitechs, "China's AI Law: Recent Developments and Legislative Signals" (newsletter, summarising Legal Daily and official plans)](https://www.geopolitechs.org/p/chinas-ai-law-recent-developments)
- East Asia Forum (26 December 2025, Ben Hu and Adam Au) describes Beijing as having "quietly removed" the overarching AI law from the 2025 agenda in favour of pilots, standards and targeted rules. The authors note compliance confusion from inconsistencies between local rules such as Shanghai's AI regulations and the Personal Information Protection Law, and argue a major AI incident could trigger comprehensive legislation in the way a 2016 fraud case accelerated privacy law. Source: [East Asia Forum, 26 December 2025](https://eastasiaforum.org/2025/12/25/china-resets-the-path-to-comprehensive-ai-governance/)
- In April 2026, state media articles argued that "conditions are not ripe" for unified national AI legislation and that a systematic AI law would be too rigid for rapid technological change. In the same month a Nanjing University group published a 13-page draft "AI Basic Law" (9 April 2026) proposing a tiered risk framework with prohibited uses (national security harm, terrorism, serious dignity violations, large-scale unlawful discrimination, manipulation of minors, large-scale unlawful surveillance). It was the third expert-group proposal. Source: [Concordia AI, AI Safety in China brief no. 27, May 2026 (specialist newsletter)](https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/china-bans-ai-partners-for-minors)
- In May 2026 the State Council's 2026 legislative work plan stated an intention to "improve AI governance and accelerate comprehensive legislation for the sound development of AI", naming data, computing power, algorithms, intellectual property, cybersecurity and supply chains as areas for legislation. Source: [South China Morning Post, 17 May 2026 (lead; primary plan text not retrieved)](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/politics/article/3353834/what-do-chinas-plans-comprehensive-new-ai-law-mean-future-technology)
- Conflict to flag: the April 2026 state media line ("conditions are not ripe") and the May 2026 State Council plan ("accelerate comprehensive legislation") point in different directions. No draft law text has been published as at October 2026 in any source I found.

#### Algorithm recommendation provisions (in force 1 March 2022)
- Drafted by CAC, co-signed by MIIT, the Ministry of Public Security and SAMR. Draft August 2021, final December 2021, in force 1 March 2022. SAMR's involvement added anti-monopoly style provisions tied to the platform crackdown. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, Sheehan, "Tracing the Roots of China's AI Regulations", February 2024](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)
- The primary motivation was the Party's loss of control over content distribution as Toutiao (ByteDance) rose. People's Daily wrote in 2017 that "in an era dominated by algorithms, there is an even greater need for 'editors-in-chief' who can guard, guide, and lead". Source: [Carnegie, Sheehan, February 2024](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)
- Labour provisions for delivery and gig workers were added after the September 2020 Renwu magazine investigation "Delivery Workers, Trapped in the System" went viral and state media took up the issue. This is a documented case of journalism and public opinion shaping the rule. Source: [Carnegie, Sheehan, February 2024](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)
- The rules required algorithms to promote "positive energy" and socialist values. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)

#### Deep synthesis provisions (in force 10 January 2023)
- CAC with MIIT and MPS. Draft January 2022, signed November 2022, in force 10 January 2023. Triggered by face-swap apps and a February 2019 viral face-swap video. Civil Code likeness-rights provisions followed in 2019. Source: [Carnegie, Sheehan, February 2024](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)
- Industry shaped the framing. A May 2020 Tencent Research Institute report argued "deepfake" was unscientific and proposed "deep synthesis"; the term did not appear in state media before that report and was adopted in the regulation. The report warned against regulatory overreach. Source: [Carnegie, Sheehan, February 2024](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)

#### Generative AI Interim Measures (July 2023, in force August 2023)
- The April 2023 draft was "extraordinarily restrictive". After "unprecedented pushback from China's AI policy community" the July 2023 Interim Measures were softened. NDRC became the second signatory and introduced "macro-policy orientation consistency evaluations" to test regulations for economic impact. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Carnegie judged parts of the 2023 measures "too vague to be enforced", for example the requirement to "actively transmit positive energy". Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)

#### Algorithm and model filing or registration (quasi-licensing)
- The algorithm registry requires companies to file basic algorithm information and a security self-assessment. By 2023 regulators were treating it as a quasi-licensing mechanism, withholding approval until generative AI models passed safety standards. October 2023 draft standards required less than 4 per cent objectionable content in sampled training data and more than 90 per cent acceptable outputs on sensitive topics. Source: [Carnegie, Sheehan, February 2024](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)
- Volume: 64 generative AI services filed in 2023 and 238 in 2024. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era). 346 by 31 March 2025. Source: [Xinhua, 8 April 2025](https://english.news.cn/20250408/290d2e9a29eb4db78309ba3c49561c06/c.html)
- As at 31 August 2026, cumulative totals were 1,112 generative AI services filed nationally and 731 applications registered at provincial level. There are two tracks: filing (beian) at national level for firms that operate or train models, and a lighter provincial registration (dengji) for products built on already-filed models through an API. In July to August 2026 new registrations (133) exceeded new filings (124) for the first time. A pathway for on-device models opened in July 2026. Source: [China AI Dispatch, September 2026 (newsletter reading CAC announcements; not checked against CAC list)](https://chinaaidispatch.substack.com/p/app-registrations-doubled-while-model)
- Caveat from the same source: the registry measures paperwork, not usage or whether a product is still live.

#### AI content labelling measures (in force 1 September 2025)
- "Measures for Labelling of AI-Generated Synthetic Content" issued March 2025. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- The Measures are a binding departmental rule paired with a mandatory national standard, GB 45438-2025, which entered into force on the same day. They require both explicit labels (visible to users) and implicit labels (metadata). Duties fall on three groups: generating service providers (primary labelling duty), distribution platforms (verification), and users (disclosure). There is no exemption for artistic or satirical content, unlike Article 50 of the EU AI Act. Source: [ComparativeAI.org, China content labelling page (academic-style tracker)](https://comparativeai.org/topics/content-labeling-provenance/china/)

#### Ethics review measures (issued 2 April 2026)
- "Measures for the Administration of AI Science and Technology Ethics Review and Services (Trial)" were issued on 2 April 2026 by MIIT and MOST with eight further departments (ten bodies in total, including CAC and the National Health Commission). They apply to AI research and development in China that may pose ethical risks to human dignity, public order, life and health, or the environment. Universities, research institutes, health institutions and enterprises must set up ethics committees, typically with no fewer than five multidisciplinary members. Enhanced (expert) review applies to systems that influence human behaviour or psychological states, shape public opinion, or make highly autonomous decisions in safety-critical settings. The measures have no penalty scale of their own and rely on the Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law and PIPL. Source: [SESEC (EU-funded standards liaison office in China), 8 April 2026](https://sesec.eu/2026/04/08/china-issues-new-regulations-on-ai-ethics-review-for-responsible-development/)
- Third-party service centres may conduct reviews, with a conflict rule that the same centre cannot carry out both the initial and the expert review of one activity. Source: [Concordia AI brief no. 27, May 2026](https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/china-bans-ai-partners-for-minors)

#### Human-like or companion AI (final 10 April 2026, in force 15 July 2026)
- Timeline: CAC draft for comment 27 December 2025, comments closed 25 January 2026, final adopted 10 April 2026, in force 15 July 2026. Source: [Digital Policy Alert (University of St Gallen tracker)](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/39272-interim-measures-for-the-administration-of-anthropomorphic-artificial-intelligence-interaction-services-including-artificial-intelligence-authority-governance-enter-into-force). Draft text: [China Law Translate](https://www.chinalawtranslate.com/en/chatbot-measures-draft/)
- Issued by CAC with NDRC, MIIT, MPS and SAMR. Scope is services that simulate human personality and communication style to provide "ongoing emotional interaction" (AI companions, emotional care). Customer service, question answering, work assistants, education and research tools are excluded. Providers must not generate content that endangers national security, encourages self-harm, manipulates emotions into harmful decisions, or "excessively cater[s] to users, inducing emotional dependency or addiction". Other duties: a pop-up reminder after two consecutive hours of use, user options to delete interaction data, no use of sensitive personal information for training without separate consent, special protections for elderly users. Security assessments go to provincial cyberspace administrations before launch and on reaching 1 million registered users or 100,000 monthly active users. App stores must verify compliance before listing. Penalties: warnings and rectification orders, RMB 10,000 to 100,000 for refusal to rectify, and RMB 100,000 to 200,000 where harm to health results. Source: [Latham & Watkins client note (law-firm summary; consistent with the China Law Translate draft on thresholds and the two-hour reminder)](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/china-introduces-rules-for-ai-companion-and-emotional-interaction-services)
- Changes from draft to final: scope narrowed to "continuous emotional interaction" and tutoring bots and productivity assistants were carved out; a requirement for manual human takeover was softened to "necessary intervention measures" plus contacting guardians in high-risk cases; child protection was tightened with an outright ban on "virtual family members, virtual partner, and other virtual intimate-relationship services" for minors, age-tiered modes, and parental consent under 14. Source: [Concordia AI brief no. 27, May 2026](https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/china-bans-ai-partners-for-minors)

#### Minors
- The companion AI measures (above) ban virtual intimate-relationship services for minors from 15 July 2026.
- On 18 September 2026 CAC published further draft rules for comment that would prohibit platforms from offering "AI-driven virtual companions" to minors, ban "algorithms designed to foster addiction", and bar minors from hosting livestreams or interacting with strangers on social networks (with an exception for those aged 16 and over whose own income is their main livelihood). Proposed penalties include confiscation and fines of up to ten times illegal gains above RMB 1 million, service suspension and licence revocation. Source: [Caixin Global, 21 September 2026](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2026-09-21/china-seeks-to-protect-minors-with-proposed-ai-rules-102487250.html)

#### Agentic AI and standards
- In April 2026 TC260 (the national cybersecurity standards committee) published a 90-page report identifying 11 agent security threats and proposing eight standards, six prioritised for 2026 to 2027, covering data leaks, unauthorised tool use, cascading failures, and limits on behavioural scope and autonomy. Source: [Concordia AI brief no. 27, May 2026](https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/china-bans-ai-partners-for-minors)
- Standards are a hard-edged instrument in China: the labelling regime rests on a mandatory national standard (GB 45438-2025). Source: [ComparativeAI.org](https://comparativeai.org/topics/content-labeling-provenance/china/)

#### AI Plus initiative (industrial and diffusion policy)
- State Council guideline released 27 August 2025. Targets: penetration of new-generation intelligent terminals and AI agents above 70 per cent by 2027 and above 90 per cent by 2030, and an "intelligent economy and society" by 2035. Six application areas: science and technology, industry, consumption, people's wellbeing, governance, and global cooperation. Supporting pillars: model capability, data supply, computing power, open-source ecosystem, talent. Source: [State Council English site, 27 August 2025](https://english.www.gov.cn/policies/latestreleases/202508/27/content_WS68ae7976c6d0868f4e8f51a0.html)
- The same August 2025 opinions called for expanded AI skills training and employment risk assessment, pairing technological development with social safeguards. Source: [Geopolitechs, January 2026 (newsletter summarising Xinhua and the State Council opinions)](https://www.geopolitechs.org/p/china-will-release-policy-document)

#### Labour and job displacement
- Official: on 27 January 2026 the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security (MOHRSS) said it would "implement a job stabilisation, expansion and quality-improvement action, introduce employment support measures for key industries, and issue a document on responding to the impact of AI and promoting employment". Source: [Xinhua, 27 January 2026 (Chinese)](https://www.news.cn/politics/20260127/8af067692da94844adee34b643b39444/c.html)
- Official: the State Council's 2026 to 2030 employment plan (signed 11 June 2026, published 17 to 18 June 2026) orders a system to track how AI creates and destroys jobs, with an early-warning mechanism for at-risk occupations, promotion of "human-machine collaboration" roles, and algorithm transparency duties for platform companies covering flexible workers. The plan sets no numerical job-creation target for 2030, unlike earlier plans. Sources: [The Next Web, June 2026 (news report; plan text not retrieved)](https://thenextweb.com/news/china-five-year-plan-ai-jobs-employment-monitoring); [South China Morning Post, June 2026](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3357518/china-unveils-employment-first-plan-includes-developing-new-ai-related-jobs)
- Practice (adjudication): in December 2025 a Beijing labour arbitration case published by the municipal human resources bureau held that "AI replacing the job function" is not a valid ground for dismissal and awarded RMB 791,815. Employers must first consider contract variation, retraining or redeployment. Source: [ChinaTalk, 2026 (specialist newsletter)](https://www.chinatalk.media/p/china-on-ai-job-loss-no-matrix-for)
- Practice (adjudication): the Hangzhou Intermediate People's Court, in a statement published 28 April 2026, held that a firm could not dismiss a quality assurance worker whose role was automated by AI, saying "companies cannot unilaterally lay off employees or cut salaries due to technological progress". Source: [Fortune, 3 May 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/05/03/chinese-court-layoffs-workers-ai-replacement-labor-market/)
- Practice (deployment restraint): in mid-2024 authorities froze expansion of Baidu's robotaxi service after taxi driver protests. Youth unemployment was 16.9 per cent in March 2026. In February 2026 labour economist Cai Fang proposed a universal basic income and subsidies for low-productivity jobs. Source: [Lawfare, Ruby Scanlon, 3 May 2026](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-political-limits-of-china-s-ai-diffusion-ambitions)

#### International alignment: Global AI Governance Action Plan
- Released 26 July 2025 at the World AI Conference and High-Level Meeting on Global AI Governance in Shanghai. Thirteen points, including: seizing AI opportunities, innovation, industry empowerment, digital infrastructure with support for the Global South, an open ecosystem and open source, data supply, energy and environment, standards and norms, public-sector leadership, AI safety governance (risk assessment, tiered management, interpretability), implementing the UN Global Digital Compact, capacity building, and a multi-stakeholder model. Source: [Permanent Mission of the PRC to the UN, 29 July 2025](https://un.china-mission.gov.cn/eng/zgyw/202507/t20250729_11679232.htm)
- Carnegie (Arindrajit Basu, 21 May 2026) argues China has moved from exporting infrastructure through the Digital Silk Road to "a more comprehensive effort aimed at recrafting global rules, norms, and institutions of AI governance", pairing low-cost technology diffusion in the Global South with normative diplomacy at the UN. The proposed World AI Cooperation Organization remains vague on operations. The US retreat from norm-setting bodies is named as an enabling condition. Source: [Carnegie Endowment, "China's Pivot on Global AI", 21 May 2026](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/05/chinas-pivot-on-global-ai)
- China established the China AI Safety and Development Association (CnAISDA) as a safety institute counterpart and took part in the Bletchley Park summit in November 2023. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)

#### Enforcement in practice
- CAC's 2025 campaign against AI misuse reported more than 3,500 AI products taken down, over 960,000 items of illegal or harmful content removed and more than 3,700 accounts closed or penalised. A 2026 campaign with the Ministry of Public Security targets AI-enabled fraud and impersonation, AI-driven "water army" manipulation, large models that have not completed filing, and training data management. Source: [The Next Web, 2026 (news report of CAC announcements)](https://thenextweb.com/news/china-ai-misuse-campaign-2026)
- Shanghai's cyberspace administration issued an enforcement notice penalising non-compliant generative AI providers in June 2025. Source: [Geopolitechs](https://www.geopolitechs.org/p/chinas-ai-law-recent-developments)
- Selective, firm-specific control: after DeepSeek's rise, Zhejiang authorities reportedly screened its investors, told headhunters to stop recruiting its staff, and some staff had passports held. Compliance audits focus on "ensuring high rates of appropriate responses to queries regarding politically sensitive information". Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)

#### Drivers: official account
- The official framing is "development and security" together. The Cybersecurity Law provision couples ethics regulation and risk governance with support for innovation and training data. Source: [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/notes-from-the-asia-pacific-region-china-s-cybersecurity-law-amendments-introduce-ai-provisions)
- AI Plus presents AI as the engine of an "intelligent economy" and of socialist modernisation by 2035. Source: [State Council, 27 August 2025](https://english.www.gov.cn/policies/latestreleases/202508/27/content_WS68ae7976c6d0868f4e8f51a0.html)
- Internationally China presents itself as champion of inclusive, UN-centred governance and Global South capacity building. Source: [PRC Mission to the UN, 29 July 2025](https://un.china-mission.gov.cn/eng/zgyw/202507/t20250729_11679232.htm)

#### Drivers: independent analysis
- Information control is the constant. Recommendation algorithms disrupted "officially sanctioned top stories" and deepfakes threatened information integrity. These remained CAC's core concerns in all periods. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Carnegie identifies four eras: "Go-Go" (2017 to early 2020, MOST-led, minimal regulation), "Crackdown" (2020 to late 2022, CAC dominant), "Catch-Up" (late 2022 to early 2025, loosened after ChatGPT, US chip export controls of October 2022, GDP growth of 3.1 per cent in 2022 and youth unemployment of 21.3 per cent in June 2023), and "Crossroads" (from early 2025, after DeepSeek restored confidence while the economy stayed weak). The explanatory variables are economic performance and technological confidence: strong growth and perceived parity with the US permit control; weak growth and a perceived gap force leniency. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Self-reliance under export controls: US semiconductor controls and the Dual Circulation strategy drove the pragmatism of the Catch-Up era. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Social stability: the November to December 2022 "white paper" protests signalled "cracks in social stability" and pushed priority toward economic recovery. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Bureaucratic competition: MOST led in 2017 to 2020; CAC rose with the algorithm rules; NDRC rose from 2023 as a counterweight and institutionalised economic-impact checks on regulation. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- Sheehan's "policy funnel": regulations are shaped in four layers, namely real-world roots, Xi Jinping and Party ideology, the "world of ideas" (academics, think tanks such as CAICT, corporate research arms, journalists, and foreign debates), and the Party-state bureaucracies. Chinese experts cite foreign precedents such as New York City's 2019 algorithm directory as inspiration for the registry. Source: [Carnegie, Sheehan, February 2024](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2024/02/tracing-the-roots-of-chinas-ai-regulations)
- Employment as a political limit on diffusion: Lawfare (May 2026) argues the Party's "performance legitimacy" makes mass displacement politically unacceptable, so AI Plus adoption targets are bounded by stability concerns. Source: [Lawfare, Scanlon, 3 May 2026](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-political-limits-of-china-s-ai-diffusion-ambitions)

### Inferences
- For a comparative themes table, China scores as: legal form = binding administrative rules plus mandatory standards, with a statute-level hook since January 2026; risk classification = none of the EU kind, with scope set by service type and by "public opinion attributes or social mobilisation capacity"; prohibited uses = content-based prohibitions and, since July 2026, a categorical ban on companion AI for minors; frontier model rules = pre-release filing and security assessment, in effect a licence; transparency = the most prescriptive labelling regime in the group; enforcement = campaign-style and discretionary.
- The gap between the official "development and security" formula and practice is mainly about discretion. Rules are vaguely worded, approval throughput is dialled up or down, and enforcement comes in campaigns. The same text supports lenient or strict practice depending on the economic and political cycle.
- Small formal fines for companion AI (maximum RMB 200,000) suggest that the real sanction is loss of filing status and app store removal, not monetary penalty. This is my inference from the penalty scale and the filing mechanism.
- Labour protection against AI displacement is being built through courts, arbitration and monitoring systems, not through AI-specific legislation. The promised MOHRSS document appears to be still pending.
- The repeated softening between draft and final (2023 generative AI measures, 2026 companion AI measures) shows industry and economic agencies have real influence on scope, while child protection and content control provisions tighten.

### Gaps
- No primary text retrieved for the State Council 2026 legislative work plan. The "comprehensive legislation" wording rests on SCMP reporting. Whether a draft AI law will go to the NPC Standing Committee in 2026 or 2027 is unconfirmed.
- I could not confirm that the MOHRSS document on AI and employment promised on 27 January 2026 has been issued. A July 2026 MOHRSS item concerns "AI plus human resources and social security" applications, which appears to be a different document. Treat issuance as unconfirmed.
- The exact adoption date and article number of the Cybersecurity Law AI provision were not captured from a primary source (commonly reported as adopted late October 2025, new Article 20). Unconfirmed in my retrieved sources.
- The effective date of the April 2026 ethics review measures was not stated in the source retrieved.
- Issuing bodies and exact issue date of the labelling measures (commonly reported as CAC, MIIT, MPS and NRTA, 14 March 2025) were not confirmed from a primary text here; only "March 2025" and the 1 September 2025 start are sourced.
- No reliable source found on penalties actually imposed under the labelling measures or the companion AI measures since they took effect.
- Compute and chip industrial policy (domestic chip mandates, subsidies, data centre programs) was not researched in depth in this pass; only the export control driver is sourced.
- Copyright and training data: Chinese court decisions on AI output and training were not researched in this pass.

## Japan: AI Promotion Act, AI Basic Plan, soft-law guidelines, copyright exception, AI Safety Institute

### Takeaway
Japan has a promotion-first framework statute with no penalties, operationalised by a cabinet-level AI Strategy Headquarters, an AI Basic Plan (first plan 23 December 2025, second plan July 2026) and non-binding guidelines. The stated driver is that Japan lags in AI adoption and investment and faces labour shortages. The first test of the "name and request" model (Grok sexualised images, January 2026) and the July 2026 plan revision show the government now openly considering penalties.

### Cited Findings

#### Legal form, scope and obligations
- The "Act on the Promotion of Research and Development and the Utilisation of AI-Related Technologies" passed the Diet on 28 May 2025; most provisions took effect on 4 June 2025. It is a basic (framework) law. Businesses have one direct duty, to "endeavour to cooperate" with government measures. There are no penalties. The government may collect information on rights infringements, give guidance, and publish the names of businesses. An AI Strategy Headquarters chaired by the Prime Minister formulates the Basic Plan. Source: [Future of Privacy Forum, 2025](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)
- The Act applies to foreign entities, and AI-utilising businesses must cooperate with measures of national and local governments. Source: [Nagashima Ohno & Tsunematsu, 29 May 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.nagashima.com/en/publications/publication20260529-1/)
- No risk tiers, no prohibited uses, no registration or licensing, and no distinct developer versus deployer duties are created by the Act. Source: [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)

#### AI Basic Plan
- First AI Basic Plan adopted by Cabinet on 23 December 2025 under Article 18(1) of the Act. Four pillars: accelerating AI use, strengthening development capability, advancing AI governance, and transforming economic and social systems. Source: [OECD.AI policy observatory](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/policy-initiatives/ai-basic-plan)
- A Second AI Basic Plan is dated 14 July 2026 (Cabinet Office provisional English translation). Goal: make Japan "the most AI-friendly country in the world" through "AI Transformation (AX)". Pillars: "Adopt AI", "Create AI", "Enhance AI Trustworthiness", "Collaborate with AI". It states that "the gaps between Japan and other growing countries in utilization rates and private investment had in fact widened by 2025". It cites population decline, labour shortages and stagnant wages as reasons. It reports 180,000 government employees using the government AI system, projects public-private investment by fiscal 2040 of 23.1 trillion yen (semiconductors), 10.5 trillion yen (robots) and 68.0 trillion yen (intelligent systems), commits to "responsible agile governance" for agentic AI, and to strengthening the AI Safety Institute's technical evaluation capacity and staffing. Source: [Cabinet Office of Japan, Second AI Basic Plan, provisional translation as of 14 July 2026](https://www8.cao.go.jp/cstp/ai/ai_plan/aiplan_eng_20260714.pdf)
- News reporting of the revision says it adds a commitment to keep the legal framework under review including possible penalties for operators that refuse to cooperate with government investigations into rights infringements, stronger measures against AI-enabled cyberattacks, early government access to advanced unreleased models, and an expanded AISI role in vulnerability assessment. Source: [Asia News Network (Japan News/Yomiuri), July 2026](https://asianews.network/japan-cabinet-approves-revision-to-ai-basic-plan/). Note: this report gives the first plan's date as December 2024, which conflicts with OECD.AI and the law-firm summary (December 2025); the 2025 date is correct on the weight of sources. The approval date is reported variously as 14 or 16 July 2026.

#### Soft-law guidelines
- AI Guidelines for Business (METI and MIC): version 1.0 April 2024, version 1.01 on 28 March 2025. Three tiers: foundational values, ten cross-sector principles, practical tools. Executive-level responsibility is expected. Source: [International Bar Association, Inoue and Kamata](https://www.ibanet.org/japan-emerging-framework-ai-legislation-guidelines)
- Version 1.2 issued March 2026, stressing "agile governance" and continuous risk assessment. Guidelines "for ensuring appropriateness" under the AI Promotion Act were established on 19 December 2025. METI published a "Guide on the Interpretation and Application of Civil Liability in AI Utilisation, Version 1.0" on 9 April 2026, which distinguishes assistive from substitutive AI when allocating liability under existing civil law. Source: [Nagashima Ohno & Tsunematsu, 29 May 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.nagashima.com/en/publications/publication20260529-1/)

#### Copyright (Article 30-4) and data
- Article 30-4 of the Copyright Act permits exploitation of works for "non-enjoyment" purposes such as data analysis and AI training without permission. The Agency for Cultural Affairs' "General Understanding on AI and Copyright in Japan" (May 2024) sets limits: the exception does not apply where use would "unreasonably prejudice the interests of the copyright owner", for example copying databases sold for analysis or circumventing technical measures against scraping; fine-tuning on a specific creator's works to produce similar output, and some retrieval-augmented uses, have an enjoyment purpose and need permission; knowingly collecting from pirate sites can attract liability; output infringes where there is both similarity and dependence. Source: [Agency for Cultural Affairs, overview, May 2024](https://www.bunka.go.jp/english/policy/copyright/pdf/94055801_01.pdf)
- From August 2025, Yomiuri Shimbun, Nikkei and Asahi Shimbun filed suits against Perplexity alleging copyright infringement. Source: [Nagashima Ohno & Tsunematsu, 29 May 2026](https://www.nagashima.com/en/publications/publication20260529-1/); [Nikkei Asia](https://asia.nikkei.com/business/technology/artificial-intelligence/japanese-newspapers-nikkei-and-asahi-sue-perplexity-ai-over-copyright)
- Privacy: the Personal Information Protection Commission warned OpenAI in June 2023. The triennial review of the APPI is considering relaxing consent requirements for AI development. Source: [International Bar Association](https://www.ibanet.org/japan-emerging-framework-ai-legislation-guidelines)

#### Practice: first test of the no-penalty model
- On 16 January 2026, Kimi Onoda, minister for AI strategy and economic security, said the Cabinet Office had asked X Corp to make immediate improvements to stop Grok generating sexualised images of real people, that the company had not yet responded, and that "we plan to promptly examine all possible options, including legal measures". Source: [Reuters via MarketScreener, 16 January 2026](https://hk.marketscreener.com/news/japan-probing-musk-s-grok-ai-service-over-inappropriate-images-ce7e58dedc81f120)
- A tracker reports that as at 25 September 2026 no public resolution of the Grok matter had been reported. Source: [CASRAI guide (tracker, secondary; lead only)](https://casrai.org/guides/japan-ai-promotion-act-no-penalties-grok-deepfakes)

#### Drivers: official account
- Japan ranked 12th in AI investment in 2023 to 2024. The Act aims to reverse low adoption and make Japan the world's "most AI-friendly country". Source: [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)
- The Second Basic Plan ties AI to labour shortages, population decline and wage stagnation. Source: [Cabinet Office, Second AI Basic Plan, July 2026](https://www8.cao.go.jp/cstp/ai/ai_plan/aiplan_eng_20260714.pdf)

#### Drivers: independent analysis
- The Act is characterised as non-binding soft law that "encourages voluntary compliance through political signalling". Source: [International Bar Association](https://www.ibanet.org/japan-emerging-framework-ai-legislation-guidelines)
- FPF contrasts Japan's minimal-burden approach, designed to attract investment, with the EU's prescriptive model and Korea's middle path. Source: [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/understanding-japans-ai-promotion-act-an-innovation-first-blueprint-for-ai-regulation/)

### Inferences
- Japan's comparables: legal form = framework statute with a cooperation duty only; risk classification = none; enforcement = information gathering, guidance and publication of names; copyright = the broadest statutory training exception in the group, narrowed by administrative interpretation and now being tested in court; liability = existing civil law with an interpretive guide.
- The direction of travel in 2026 is toward harder edges. The Grok episode exposed the lack of leverage over a foreign provider that does not respond, and the July 2026 plan revision puts penalties on the table. Security framing (AI-enabled cyberattack, early access to frontier models) is newly prominent in the second plan.
- The stated driver (catch up on adoption and investment) and the observed behaviour are aligned. The divergence is on the trust side: the "trustworthiness" pillar relies on instruments with no compulsion.

### Gaps
- Japan AI Safety Institute: I found the commitment to expand it in the Second Basic Plan but no reliable figures on current staffing, budget or evaluations performed.
- The content of the 19 December 2025 appropriateness guidelines and of AI Guidelines for Business v1.2 was not retrieved from METI or the Cabinet Office directly.
- The Japan Times and Bloomberg reports on Grok were paywalled. Whether formal guidance under the Act was ever issued to X Corp is unconfirmed.
- Status of the APPI amendment bill in 2026 was not confirmed.
- No peer-reviewed analysis of Japanese drivers was retrieved in this pass. Driver analysis rests on FPF and IBA commentary.
- Outcome of the Perplexity litigation is not known.

## South Korea: AI Basic Act, enforcement decree, high-impact and generative AI duties, grace period

### Takeaway
Korea's Framework Act on AI Development and Trust took effect with its Enforcement Decree on 22 January 2026, making it the first comprehensive AI statute in Asia to be fully applicable, but fines (capped at KRW 30 million) are deferred for a grace period of at least one year and the ministry has promised "minimum regulation". The official driver is to become one of the top three AI powers by pairing promotion with a trust framework; observers see a race to be first, with unclear definitions, low penalties and criticism from both startups and civil society.

### Cited Findings

#### Legal form, timeline and institutions
- Enacted December 2024, effective January 2026. As of April 2025 the Minister reaffirmed a commitment to "minimum regulations". Source: [US International Trade Administration, 2025](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-artificial-intelligence-ai-basic-act)
- The Act and its Enforcement Decree became effective on 22 January 2026. MSIT is applying a grace period of at least one year in which administrative fines are generally deferred, except in cases involving serious social harm. MSIT runs an AI Basic Act help desk through the Korea Software Industry Association. Source: [US International Trade Administration, 2026](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act)
- The Act was revised before commencement. The Presidential Council on National AI Strategy has statutory status and coordinating authority across ministries. Universities and private firms may establish AI research institutes. Agencies must prioritise AI solutions in procurement and civil servants are shielded from personal liability for resulting damage absent intent or gross negligence. Digital inclusion provisions cover people with disability, older people and disadvantaged groups. Source: [Korea Times, 2 January 2026](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/tech-science/20260102/how-newly-revised-ai-basic-act-will-reshape-koreas-ai-landscape)
- New institutions include a national AI committee and an AI Safety Research Institute. Source: [Cooley, 27 January 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)

#### Scope, risk classification and obligations
- The law covers both AI developers and businesses that integrate AI into products or services, and applies extraterritorially where Korean users or markets are affected. Source: [Cooley, 27 January 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)
- High-impact AI: human oversight is required in areas including nuclear safety, drinking water production, transport, healthcare and financial services such as credit evaluation and loan screening. Companies must give advance notice to users of high-impact or generative AI and clearly label AI output that is hard to distinguish from reality. Source: [Reuters via Yahoo Finance, 22 January 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-050945594.html)
- High-impact AI operators should prepare risk management plans, explainability measures, user protection and documentation. Source: [US International Trade Administration, 2026](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act). Law-firm summaries add impact assessments on fundamental rights and list hiring and biometrics among high-impact areas. Source: [Cooley (law-firm summary)](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)
- Frontier or high-performance AI: additional safety obligations apply to systems whose cumulative training compute exceeds 10^26 floating-point operations, among other criteria. Source: [US International Trade Administration, 2026](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act)
- Generative AI: disclosure is required, particularly for downloadable synthetic media such as images and video, through notices or technical measures such as watermarks. Source: [US International Trade Administration, 2026](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act); [Korea Times, 2 January 2026](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/tech-science/20260102/how-newly-revised-ai-basic-act-will-reshape-koreas-ai-landscape)
- Domestic representative: foreign operators with no Korean address must appoint one if prior-year total revenue is about USD 662 million or more (KRW 1 trillion), prior-year AI service revenue is about USD 6.6 million or more (KRW 10 billion), or average daily domestic users reach one million over the previous three months. Source: [US International Trade Administration, 2026](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-ai-basic-act); [Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-01-27-south-koreas-ai-basic-act-overview-and-key-takeaways)
- The Act has no list of prohibited AI practices of the EU kind in any source I retrieved. Sources describe only high-impact, generative and high-performance categories.

#### Enforcement and penalties
- Maximum fine is KRW 30 million (about USD 20,400), for example for failure to label generative AI output. Authorities will enforce after a grace period of at least one year, which the government has said it may extend. Source: [Reuters via Yahoo Finance, 22 January 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-050945594.html)
- The grace period emphasises guidance and certification support over penalties. Source: [Korea Times, 2 January 2026](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/tech-science/20260102/how-newly-revised-ai-basic-act-will-reshape-koreas-ai-landscape)

#### Drivers: official account
- Science Minister Bae Kyung-hoon called the law "a critical institutional foundation" for Korea's ambition to be among the top three global AI powers. Source: [Reuters via Yahoo Finance, 22 January 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-050945594.html)
- Officials argue that early rules reduce uncertainty and let Korea shape international norms while the US is gridlocked and the EU moves slowly. Source: [Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10653018)

#### Drivers and practice: independent accounts
- Industry: Lim Jung-wook of Startup Alliance asked "why do we have to be the first to do this?" Researchers warned that vague wording may push firms to over-cautious choices. Source: [Reuters via Yahoo Finance, 22 January 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-050945594.html)
- A survey of local AI startups found 98 per cent had not begun formal compliance steps because guidance was unclear. One industry source said "there's no way to know if what we're building qualifies as high-impact AI". An unnamed law professor called it "a policy race for headlines, not sustainable governance". Civic groups say the law lacks independent oversight and meaningful enforcement on algorithmic bias and biometric surveillance. Source: [Korea Herald](https://www.koreaherald.com/article/10653018) (anonymous quotes, treat as indicative only)
- Civil society groups criticised the draft decree for limited scope, lack of a human-rights risk perspective and delayed penalties. Source: [Business and Human Rights Centre summary of Korean press (page title only; full text not retrieved)](https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/s-korea-draft-decree-for-ai-basic-act-spark-backlash-over-limited-scope-and-delayed-penalties/)
- Source conflict: the Korea Times summary describes "high-impact AI" as systems above the 10^26 FLOP threshold. The ITA and Cooley descriptions treat the compute threshold as a separate "high-performance" safety category, with high-impact defined by sector of use. The latter reading is better supported.

### Inferences
- Korea's comparables: legal form = binding framework statute plus decree; risk classification = sector-based "high-impact" tier plus a compute-based frontier tier, no prohibited tier; obligations fall on both developers and deployers as "AI business operators"; transparency = notice and labelling duties for generative AI; enforcement = MSIT fact-finding and low administrative fines, suspended until at least January 2027.
- Practice diverges from the "first comprehensive AI law" framing. In 2026 the regime operates as guidance and a help desk. Maximum fines are trivial for large providers. The combination suggests the statute is as much an industrial promotion and signalling instrument as a control instrument.
- Korea is the only jurisdiction in this group with an explicit compute threshold for frontier models (10^26 FLOPs), which makes it directly comparable with thresholds used in the EU and US debates.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve MSIT primary materials or the full list of high-impact areas in the Act (commonly reported as about ten or eleven domains). The lists above come from Reuters and law-firm summaries and differ slightly.
- No reliable report of any enforcement action, fact-finding investigation or designation of a high-impact system during 2026 was found.
- Whether the grace period has been formally extended beyond January 2027, and whether further amendments passed the National Assembly in 2026, is unconfirmed.
- No peer-reviewed analysis of Korean drivers retrieved in this pass.
- Compute and industrial policy (national GPU procurement, data centre programs, sovereign AI funding) was not researched in this pass.

## Singapore: Model AI Governance Frameworks, AI Verify, sectoral guidance, online safety and election deepfake laws

### Takeaway
Singapore has no general AI law. It governs through voluntary model frameworks (2019/2020, generative AI 2024, agentic AI January 2026 and updated May 2026), testing tools (AI Verify), sector supervisors (MAS), and targeted binding laws on specific harms (election deepfakes 2024, AI-generated intimate images 2025, online harms relief 2025). The stated rationale is nimble, pro-innovation governance; analysts tie it to hub competitiveness and note that binding law is being added harm by harm.

### Cited Findings

#### Legal form and instruments
- Singapore has no omnibus AI law and uses sectoral laws and voluntary guidelines. Voluntary frameworks: Model AI Governance Framework (2nd edition 2020), Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI (finalised May 2024), Model AI Governance Framework for Agentic AI (January 2026). Testing: AI Verify (May 2022, 11 principles), Project Moonshot (2024) for LLM evaluation, with crosswalks to the NIST AI RMF (October 2023) and ISO/IEC 42001 (June 2024). Guidance: PDPC Advisory Guidelines on use of personal data in AI systems (March 2024), CSA Guidelines on Securing AI Systems (October 2024), Ministry of Law guide on generative AI in the legal sector (6 March 2026), MAS FEAT principles. Source: [Chambers and Partners, Artificial Intelligence 2026, Singapore chapter (practitioner guide)](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore)
- The original Model Framework covers four areas: internal governance, human involvement in decisions, operations management, and stakeholder interaction. The Generative AI framework has nine dimensions including accountability, data, safety testing and content provenance. MAS issued FEAT principles in 2018 and the Veritas toolkit in 2022 to 2023. Source: [Allen, Loo and Luna, "Governing intelligence: Singapore's evolving AI governance framework", Cambridge Forum on AI: Law and Governance, 2025 (peer reviewed)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5E54A373E193E2D51354ADC1F509B9B4/S3033373324000127a.pdf/governing_intelligence_singapores_evolving_ai_governance_framework.pdf)
- Agentic AI: IMDA released the framework in January 2026 and an updated version on 20 May 2026. The update adds access controls, guardrails and monitoring as agent components, multi-agent and third-party agent risks, a clearer split of roles between platform providers and system developers, guidance on automation bias (monitoring override rates), change management, a note on skill degradation where agents take over entry-level tasks, and case studies from companies and agencies. It is guidance, not a legal requirement. Source: [Baker McKenzie, June 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/06/singapore-imda-updates-model-ai-governance-framework-for-agentic-ai)

#### Sectoral: financial services (MAS)
- MAS consulted in November 2025 on formal Guidelines on AI Risk Management for financial institutions; consultation closed January 2026; a 12-month transition after issue was proposed. Project MindForge phase two published an AI risk management toolkit in March 2026, developed with 24 financial institutions. As of May 2026 the Guidelines had not been finalised. In late April 2026 MAS convened chief executives of major financial institutions to discuss risks from a frontier model with offensive cyber capability. Source: [Cloud Security Alliance research note, May 2026 (industry body; cites MAS media releases and Bloomberg)](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-singapore-mas-emergency-ai-governance-2026/). MAS consultation paper: [MAS, November 2025](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/mas-media-library/publications/consultations/bd/2025/final_consultation_paper_on_guidelines_on_ai_risk_management_forrelease.pdf)

#### Binding law on specific harms
- Elections (Integrity of Online Advertising) (Amendment) Act 2024: the first Singapore statute to mention AI. It bans online election advertising containing digitally generated or manipulated content that falsely depicts a candidate's speech or actions during the election period. Penalties for individuals are a fine up to SGD 50,000 and/or imprisonment. IMDA and the Returning Officer can issue corrective directions to platforms and individuals. Sources: [Chambers 2026](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore); [Reed Smith (law-firm summary; its "November 2024" passage date and "five years" term conflict with other reports of passage on 15 October 2024 and a 12-month maximum term, so treat penalty details as unconfirmed)](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/singapore-bans-deepfakes-in-elections/)
- The law was in place for the first time at the 2025 general election. Source: [Asia News Network (Straits Times)](https://asianews.network/law-banning-deepfakes-of-candidates-in-place-for-first-time-in-ge2025-among-other-guidelines-eld/)
- Criminal Law (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 2025 covers synthetic intimate images and child abuse material generated wholly by AI. The Workplace Fairness Act 2025 applies fair employment duties to hiring decisions regardless of whether AI tools are used. Source: [Chambers 2026](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore)
- Online Safety (Relief and Accountability) Act: passed 5 November 2025. It creates an Online Safety Commission that can direct removal of content, restrict accounts and require platforms to disclose user identity information so victims can pursue claims. It starts with five harms (online harassment, intimate image abuse, image-based child abuse, doxxing, online stalking) and will extend to 13. It creates statutory torts. Establishment of the Commission was scheduled for the first half of 2026. Source: [gov.sg explainer, November 2025](https://www.gov.sg/explainers/parliament-nov2025/). The Act includes a tort of "inauthentic material abuse" covering AI-generated content. Source: [Chambers 2026](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore)

#### Data, copyright, liability, public sector, industrial policy
- Section 244 of the Copyright Act 2021 permits computational data analysis using copyrighted works, which covers AI training, separately from fair use. The PDPA applies to personal data used in AI. No special AI liability rules exist; tort and contract apply. Government use includes the "Pair" assistant across more than 100 agencies and court trials of AI translation and summarisation (2025). Budget 2026 measures include a National AI Council chaired by the Prime Minister, extension of the Enterprise Innovation Scheme to AI spending, and free access to premium AI tools for workers in selected training courses. Source: [Chambers 2026](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore)

#### Drivers: official account
- Deputy Prime Minister Gan Kim Yong described the approach as "nimble" and "flexible, but at the same time, we are very careful in providing guidance". Singapore relies on "existing laws and regulations that address common consumer and societal harms". Source: [Harjani, Issues in Science and Technology, 15 September 2025](https://issues.org/singapore-artificial-intelligence-regulation-harjani/)
- National AI Strategy 2.0 positions AI as essential to economic prosperity, alongside the Smart Nation agenda. Source: [Allen, Loo and Luna, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5E54A373E193E2D51354ADC1F509B9B4/S3033373324000127a.pdf/governing_intelligence_singapores_evolving_ai_governance_framework.pdf)

#### Drivers: independent analysis
- Harjani (RSIS) names economic competitiveness and hub positioning (Singapore ranked 10th in AI investment, USD 7.27 billion over 2013 to 2024), a small-state strategy of piloting governance models that gain international credibility (AI Verify), and regional leadership. He argues the light-touch approach is exposed by US-China geopolitical pressure, domestic inequality risks, and intentional misuse: "too little attention has been devoted to understanding and mitigating potential harm from intentional manipulation of AI". Source: [Issues in Science and Technology, 15 September 2025](https://issues.org/singapore-artificial-intelligence-regulation-harjani/)
- Allen, Loo and Luna describe a "quasi-regulatory", collaborative model of "responsible optimism", note Singapore's role in ASEAN AI guidance, and warn that reliance on voluntary compliance may be inadequate as generative AI spreads, possibly requiring "stronger accountability measures". Source: [Cambridge Forum on AI: Law and Governance, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5E54A373E193E2D51354ADC1F509B9B4/S3033373324000127a.pdf/governing_intelligence_singapores_evolving_ai_governance_framework.pdf)

### Inferences
- Singapore's comparables: legal form = voluntary frameworks plus targeted statutes; risk classification = none in law, with risk-proportionate guidance; frontier rules = none; transparency = recommended, not required, except in elections; enforcement = existing regulators (IMDA, PDPC, MAS) and a new Online Safety Commission; data and copyright = a statutory text and data mining exception; international alignment = crosswalks to NIST and ISO, ASEAN guidance.
- Practice shows a two-track pattern. AI as a technology is governed by soft frameworks and assurance tooling that double as export products for Singapore's governance brand. AI-enabled harms to elections, sexual integrity and individuals online are governed by hard law with criminal penalties and takedown powers. The "no AI law" description is accurate for the first track only.
- MAS moving from principles (FEAT) to supervisory Guidelines shows that sector regulators are where voluntary norms harden first.
- The agentic framework's mention of skill degradation in entry-level work is one of the few places a governance document in this region links AI governance to labour effects.

### Gaps
- Whether MAS issued the final AI Risk Management Guidelines between May and October 2026 is unconfirmed.
- I could not confirm the actual launch date of the Online Safety Commission or any enforcement figures (the Bird & Bird note titled "Singapore Launches Online Safety Commission" was paywalled). Launch in the first half of 2026 was the plan; treat actual launch as probable but unverified.
- No reliable data found on enforcement of the election deepfake law during the 2025 general election.
- Exact statutory penalties under the election deepfake provisions need checking against Singapore Statutes Online.
- AI Verify adoption figures and the Global AI Assurance Pilot or Sandbox outcomes were not retrieved.

## Taiwan: AI Basic Act status

### Takeaway
Taiwan's Artificial Intelligence Basic Act passed the Legislative Yuan on 23 December 2025 and was promulgated and took effect on 14 January 2026. It is a 20-article principles statute with no penalties, which names the National Science and Technology Council as competent authority, tasks the Ministry of Digital Affairs with a risk classification framework, and sets deadlines for government to review its own AI use and align other laws within two years.

### Cited Findings

#### Status and legal form
- Passed third reading on 23 December 2025. NSTC is designated the competent authority. Source: [Focus Taiwan (CNA), 23 December 2025](https://focustaiwan.tw/politics/202512230010)
- Promulgated and in effect on 14 January 2026. Source: [Library of Congress Global Legal Monitor, 7 May 2026 (page blocked; date confirmed through the Library's own announcement of the article)](https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2026-05-07/taiwan-new-law-establishes-basic-framework-on-artificial-intelligence); corroborated by [Tech Policy Press, Mok and Chu, 25 February 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/taiwans-ai-basic-act-can-be-a-model-for-asia/)
- The Act has 20 articles and no penalties. It defines AI as a system with autonomous operational capability that can produce predictions, content, recommendations or decisions. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 25 February 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/taiwans-ai-basic-act-can-be-a-model-for-asia/)

#### Content
- Seven principles: sustainable development and wellbeing, human autonomy, privacy and data governance, cybersecurity and safety, transparency and explainability, fairness and non-discrimination, accountability. Source: [Taipei Times, 24 December 2025](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/front/archives/2025/12/24/2003849407)
- General restriction: AI applications "must not harm people's lives, freedom or property, nor undermine social order, national security or the environment, or involve bias, discrimination, false advertising, misinformation or fabrication". Source: [Taipei Times, 24 December 2025](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/front/archives/2025/12/24/2003849407)
- Government duties: a national AI strategy committee chaired by the Premier meeting at least annually; budget for research, application and infrastructure; subsidies; risk-based management aligned with international standards; and protection of labour rights through retraining and employment assistance for workers displaced by AI. Source: [Taipei Times, 24 December 2025](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/front/archives/2025/12/24/2003849407)
- MODA is to develop an AI risk classification framework interoperable with international standards, and sectoral regulators apply it. Timelines after commencement: 3 months for impact assessments regarding minors, human rights and gender; 6 months for government review of existing AI deployments; 12 months for internal government AI use rules; 24 months for alignment of laws and regulations. Agencies must do risk assessments before deploying AI, which creates indirect duties for vendors. The Act promotes privacy by design and data minimisation. Source: [Baker McKenzie, January 2026 (law-firm summary)](https://www.bakermckenzie.com/en/insight/publications/2026/01/taiwan-ai-basic-act)
- The July 2024 NSTC draft also provided for regulatory sandboxes, certification and testing mechanisms, training data respecting copyright unless licensed or fair use, and future rules on liability, compensation and insurance. Source: [IAPP, 2024 (describes the draft, not the final Act)](https://iapp.org/news/a/taiwan-s-ai-governance-framework-unveiled)

#### Politics and practice
- The designation of NSTC as competent authority was supported by the KMT and Taiwan People's Party and opposed by the DPP, the governing party. Source: [Taipei Times, 24 December 2025](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/front/archives/2025/12/24/2003849407)
- Taiwan budgeted more than NTD 30 billion (about USD 950 million) for AI in 2026, with multi-year spending possibly above NTD 100 billion. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 25 February 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/taiwans-ai-basic-act-can-be-a-model-for-asia/)

#### Drivers: official account
- The government stated that legal interpretations concerning AI regulation should not hinder the development of new AI technologies. Source: [IAPP, 2024](https://iapp.org/news/a/taiwan-s-ai-governance-framework-unveiled)
- The Act "tries to balance AI promotion with social welfare". Source: [Focus Taiwan, 23 December 2025](https://focustaiwan.tw/politics/202512230010)

#### Drivers: independent analysis
- Mok and Chu (Stanford CDDRL affiliates) identify supply chain trust given semiconductor dominance, priority for innovation over strict regulation, alignment with the soft-law approach of the US and Japan in contrast to the EU and Korea, and economic security through technological indispensability. They criticise weak digital rights protection and unclear civil society participation. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 25 February 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/taiwans-ai-basic-act-can-be-a-model-for-asia/)
- Hsiao (National Taipei University, peer reviewed, 2 June 2026) describes a "hybrid, risk-based co-regulatory framework" and identifies gaps: undefined "high-risk AI" criteria, unclear allocation of authority between agencies, little on general-purpose AI, and data governance limited to personal data. He recommends operational transparency duties, whole-of-government coordination, refined risk classification, and wider data governance. Source: [Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 2 June 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07751-6)

### Inferences
- Taiwan's comparables: legal form = basic act binding mainly on government; risk classification = delegated and not yet defined; prohibited uses = a general harm clause without sanctions; obligations on developers and deployers = none direct, expected through sectoral rules by January 2028; labour = an explicit statutory duty on government to assist displaced workers, which is unusual in this group; enforcement = none in the Act.
- The dispute over the competent authority, decided by the opposition majority against the governing party's preference, suggests that institutional design was shaped by divided government as much as by policy design. This is an inference from the vote pattern reported.
- Statutory deadlines of 3, 6, 12 and 24 months from 14 January 2026 give checkpoints at mid-April 2026, mid-July 2026, January 2027 and January 2028. Whether the first two were met is not established.

- Whether provisions on copyright in training data, sandboxes and liability from the 2024 draft survived into the final Act was not confirmed article by article.
- Taiwan's separate measures on election deepfakes and fraud were not researched in this pass.

## Cross-jurisdiction: stated drivers versus observed drivers, and where practice diverges

### Takeaway
All five governments state a dual aim of promotion and trust, but the instruments reveal different primary drivers: regime information control and stability in China, catch-up on adoption in Japan, first-mover positioning in Korea, hub competitiveness in Singapore, and supply-chain trust in Taiwan. In every case practice is softer on industry than the formal text suggests, except for content control in China and specific online harms in Singapore.

### Cited Findings
- China: enforcement intensity follows economic conditions and technological confidence, not a uniform application of rules. Source: [Carnegie, Singer and Sheehan, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era)
- China: drafts are softened after industry and ministry pushback (generative AI 2023; companion AI 2026). Sources: [Carnegie, July 2025](https://carnegieendowment.org/china/research/2025/07/chinas-ai-policy-in-the-deepseek-era); [Concordia AI brief no. 27](https://aisafetychina.substack.com/p/china-bans-ai-partners-for-minors)
- China: employment stability constrains diffusion in practice (robotaxi freeze, court rulings against AI-based dismissal), while policy documents promote 70 and 90 per cent penetration targets. Sources: [Lawfare, 3 May 2026](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/the-political-limits-of-china-s-ai-diffusion-ambitions); [State Council, 27 August 2025](https://english.www.gov.cn/policies/latestreleases/202508/27/content_WS68ae7976c6d0868f4e8f51a0.html)
- Japan: the statute has no penalties, and the government's first confrontation with a foreign provider in January 2026 relied on requests and the threat of unspecified "legal measures". The July 2026 plan revision contemplates penalties. Sources: [Reuters via MarketScreener, 16 January 2026](https://hk.marketscreener.com/news/japan-probing-musk-s-grok-ai-service-over-inappropriate-images-ce7e58dedc81f120); [Asia News Network, July 2026](https://asianews.network/japan-cabinet-approves-revision-to-ai-basic-plan/)
- Korea: fines deferred for at least a year, capped at KRW 30 million, and the minister has promised "minimum regulation". Sources: [Reuters, 22 January 2026](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-050945594.html); [US ITA](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/south-korea-artificial-intelligence-ai-basic-act)
- Singapore: voluntary frameworks for AI as a technology, and binding law with criminal penalties for election deepfakes and AI-generated intimate images. Source: [Chambers 2026](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/artificial-intelligence-2026/singapore)
- Taiwan: principles without penalties, with risk classification still to be defined. Source: [Hsiao, Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 2 June 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07751-6)
- Regional alignment labels used by analysts: Taiwan and Japan are placed with the US in a "soft law" group, Korea and the EU in a stricter group. Source: [Tech Policy Press, 25 February 2026](https://www.techpolicy.press/taiwans-ai-basic-act-can-be-a-model-for-asia/)

### Inferences
- Candidate policy themes that all five jurisdictions can be scored on: legal form (statute, administrative rule, soft law); presence of a risk tier; presence of a frontier or compute threshold (Korea only); pre-market filing or licensing (China only); labelling duty (China mandatory and technical, Korea mandatory, others voluntary or harm-specific); protections for minors and companion AI (China only so far); copyright training exception (Japan and Singapore statutory); labour measures (China through courts and planning, Taiwan in statute, Singapore in guidance); penalties (China moderate and discretionary, Korea low and deferred, Japan and Taiwan none, Singapore harm-specific criminal law); state institutions (headquarters or councils chaired by the head of government in Japan, Korea, Singapore and Taiwan).
- Candidate driver themes: regime security and information control (China); social stability and employment (China, emerging in Japan and Singapore as labour shortage or skills framing); economic catch-up and adoption deficit (Japan); first-mover and norm-shaping prestige (Korea, China internationally, Singapore regionally); hub competitiveness and investment attraction (Singapore, Japan, Taiwan); technological self-reliance under export controls (China) and supply-chain trust (Taiwan); bureaucratic competition (China documented, Taiwan visible in the competent authority dispute); child protection and public scandal as triggers for hard rules (China companion AI, Japan Grok, Singapore intimate images); divided government (Taiwan).
- A common pattern across the four non-PRC jurisdictions is a head-of-government AI council plus a basic or framework law whose binding force falls mostly on the state itself. The "promotion act" is a distinct legal form worth its own category in a global comparison.
- Hard rules arrive fastest where there is a concrete, visible harm to identifiable people (minors, sexual deepfakes, election impersonation) and slowest for general model risk.

### Gaps
- No single comparative peer-reviewed study covering all five jurisdictions as at 2026 was retrieved. The comparison above is assembled from jurisdiction-level sources.
- Compute and semiconductor industrial policy across the five was not covered in depth and should be sourced separately if it is to be a theme.
- Public-sector AI use rules are documented here for Singapore, Taiwan, Korea and Japan only at headline level.
- OECD.AI was used for Japan only. Entries for the other four were not checked.
